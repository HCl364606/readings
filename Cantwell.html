8. The geopolitics of academic science Brendan Cantwell and Adam Grimm

INTRODUCTION

The purpose of this chapter is to assess the geopolitics of academic science. At the time of writing, in 2017, science is politically charged. Political activation is connected to turbulent events. The role of academic science in the Anglo-American sphere (which remains dominant) is in flux following the Brexit referendum and election of Donald Trump as President of the United States. But changes are not limited to these two examples. The Hungarian government launched an assault on the Open Society Foundation-funded Central European University, and the government of Turkey has arrested many professors and cracked down on academic freedom following a failed military coup. These developments have occurred at a time when other countries, including one-party regimes such as Russia and China and multi-party democracies such as Germany and Japan, have set establishing globally competitive research universities as a policy priority. Our aim is to be sensitive to these developments in our appraisal of scientific geopolitics without being fully blinded by the glare of recent events. We attempt to accomplish this by understanding geopolitics not as a state of affairs, but rather as a method for analyzing the geographic, economic, political, and social dimensions that influence international relations and foreign policy. In this chapter, we are interested in international relations and foreign policy as they relate to academic sciences. Particular focus is given to understanding and evaluating the concepts that have been used to understand the geopolitics of academic science. Attention is also given to the ways in which states compete with one another in the realm of academic science, policies designed to secure a competitive edge in this competition, and contemporary developments in the geopolitics of science. The chapter concludes by discussing potential avenues for research and theorization to better understand the geopolitics of academic science.

Why Geopolitics?

Why is it useful to understand academic science geopolitically? In ideal accounts, science is described as curiosity-driven and disinterested systematic investigation into falsifiable hypotheses using the scientific method. In this telling, the ultimate goal of science is to find truth without regard to political, social, or cultural interests (Merton, 1973). Scientific investigation is often housed within universities as a means of insulating science and scientists from political and economic pressures. Within these settings, skepticism has been identified as a mechanism that regulates organizational behavior in order to allow science to operate as largely described above (Owen-Smith, 2001). Science is a norm-guided institution dedicated to identifying truth. Scientists are motivated by curiosity and the intrinsic value of knowledge, but they also operate in a context in which economic and political incentives powerfully shape academic science (Stephan, 2012).

In order to understand academic science geopolitically we need to identify the sources of advantage that a state might enjoy from control (direct or indirect) of academic science. Of the potential sources of advantage derived from academic science:

at least four seem to have direct relevance for geopolitical competition: (1) higher education can contribute to stronger states; (2) a strong higher education system provides access to the world science system and can facilitate innovation; (3) higher education generates human capital and creativity hubs that can strengthen economic vitality; (4) a strong higher education system can help to improve a country’s reputation and influence abroad. (Cantwell, 2017, p. 313)

Given the expansive scope of the topic, this chapter begins with concepts of academic science–state–society relations that emerged following the Cold War.

POST-COLD WAR THEORIES OF ACADEMIC SCIENCE

The decade following the Cold War was an optimistic time among scholars who theorized science–society relations. Many in this period identified a wealth creation capacity of science. While it is not possible to review all of the scholarship on the topic from the period, we review four prominent concepts: Mode 2, Triple Helix, Entrepreneurial University, and Academic Capitalism.

Four Concepts

Among the most influential work from the period is New Production of Knowledge by Gibbons et al. (1994). The centerpiece of the book is the concept of “Mode 2” knowledge production. Gibbons et al. argued that Mode 2 would supplant the traditional disciplinary-based mode of knowledge production (Mode 1). Mode 2 knowledge production is distinguished from traditional science production by six points of departure: (1) priority is given to application rather than to the intrinsic interest of a scientific problem; (2) knowledge is produced transdisciplinarily because the boundaries of discipline are dissolved; (3) knowledge production occurs heterogeneously and universities lose their monopoly; (4) knowledge production is organizationally flexible as research groups come together quickly to solve a problem, dissolve when their work is done, and reform in a different constellation to address a new problem; (5) science is widely held accountable to society at large, rather than only to peer review; and (6) knowledge production is reflexive because researchers are conscious of what they are doing and why they are doing it (Gibbons et al., 1994). The Mode 2 concept’s emphasis on application, reflectivity, and accountability has geopolitical implications because it suggests that science can and should be deployed to achieve specified objectives in the social interest, which could be defined by the state. Mode 2 does not conceptualize the ways in which the state may interact with science.

The “Triple Helix” thesis by Etzkowitz and Leydesdorff (1995) provides a clearer account of the role of the state. Like Mode 2, the Triple Helix model predicts that science will become more applied and become dedicated to discovery that drives innovation. However, the Triple Helix idea is about the way sectors interact in steering the scientific enterprise rather than the organization of scientific production. The model assumes that scientific advancement is the product of interaction between the university, industry, and government. The role of the state in this model is to strategically link the university with industry, but by facilitating collaboration through regulatory reform and funding, and by coaxing universities out of self-reference and into broad social accountability and engagement.

Both Mode 2 and the Triple Helix concepts address science–society relations. Other prominent theories from the 1990s gave greater consideration to the role of universities. Burton Clark’s (1998) Creating Entrepreneurial Universities proved to be an enormously influential work on higher education from the decade. Clark’s concept of the “Entrepreneurial University” developed from case studies of Warwick University in England, Twente University in the Netherlands, Strathclyde University in Scotland, the University of Chalmers in Sweden, and the University of Joensun in Finland. The combination of less direct public funding, increased demands for social accountability, and the growing importance of knowledge and science in national economic performance all created new challenges and opportunities for universities. This environmental sea change, Clark argued, required that university leaders take the helm of their institutions and steer it through the choppy waters of change by encouraging entrepreneurial innovation and new forms of organization. The idea of the Entrepreneurial University is one that has a tight steering core that can nimbly chart a new path in response to the prevailing winds. This concept was taken up quickly, especially by policy-makers in Europe who sought to use it as a guideline for policy-making that encouraged more independent, innovative, and socially responsive universities (Rhoades and Stensaker, 2017). Although the Entrepreneurial University concept did not give exclusive attention to science, it built upon the idea that flexible and interdisciplinary academic organizations were best suited to advancing social and national goals.

The concept of “Academic Capitalism” also focused primarily on universities as organizations. This concept was first identified through empirical work in Australia, Canada, and the United States (US) by Slaughter and Leslie (1997), and then further elaborated by Slaughter and Rhoades (2004) from only the US context. Academic Capitalism is also predicated on a shifting environment including declines in public funding and increased calls for innovation. But Academic Capitalism identifies new patterns of resource dependence in which universities had come to compete for revenue and to convert academic research into commercial activity. A centerpiece of the idea of Academic Capitalism is that universities increasingly prize the sort of scientific activities that can be monopolized thorough patents, and commercialized through technology transfer. Academic Capitalism is generally a critical concept that points to a privatization of public goods (knowledge production) that creates stratification within and between universities, and that emphasizes only areas of science that enjoy short-term economic application rather than basic forms of discovery.

Conceptual Critique

While each of the four concepts are different, they also share broad similarities. All observe that state funding for academic science had shifted over the course of the 1980s and 1990s from a model of direct provision to one where universities competed for funds awarded through grants and other similar mechanisms. All identified a growing role for corporations and private actors in directing and participating in academic science. Each also described increased institutional autonomy, often in the form of more robust and corporate-like university management. What these concepts demonstrate is that the geopolitical implications of academic science were quite optimistic. Much thinking during the 1990s was that technological advancement could strengthen states by advancing economic growth and social well-being. These ideas were embedded in works such as Castells’s (1996) Rise of the Network Society and Florida’s (2002) Rise of the Creative Class, which saw interconnected hubs of talent, skill, and technology as propelling economic and social development. The overall policy implication was that states could become strengthened with carefully calibrated policies that unleashed the potential of academic science by encouraging research that was targeted to application through collaboration with industry. Although critical concepts such as Academic Capitalism identified power concentration and stratification, most policy-makers saw harnessing the power of academic science for economic application as a means for strengthening the competitive positions of states. This was perhaps most clear in European Union policy which treated academic science as a key plank in a policy program to make Europe the most innovative and economically competitive region in the world (Slaughter and Cantwell, 2012).

Two features of the post-Cold War geopolitics of academic science are worth emphasizing. First, it was generally assumed that the state’s role was of facilitation and steering rather than direct control. Second, the idea of science and technology-driven economic growth was largely understood in terms of the shared geopolitical benefits rather than absolute, zero sum gains. While competition among states was intense and increasing, it produced stronger science and a broad realization that all countries and regions could grow and develop in ways supported by mutual growth and development. Everyone, it was thought, could be a winner. The goal of competition was to be a big winner, not to ensure that some other state was a loser.

On the one hand, it would be wrong to dismiss out of hand this assessment of the role of academic science in geopolitical arrangements. The academic science enterprise is part of a global production network that links actors and sources of capital in resource constellations that both concentrate power within nation-states, and extend beyond the control of states (Kauppinen and Cantwell, 2014). As Ellen Hazelkorn (2017, p. 21) explained: higher education’s “foremost role in talent and knowledge production makes it integral to national and global power relations.” On the other hand, there are limits of these concepts for understanding the contemporary geopolitics of academic science. One limitation is that the concepts described above tend to be Anglo-centric and overlook state-led systems such as China. Even critical theories of academic science may have placed too much emphasis on “the market” as an organizing force and source of scientific firepower (Marginson, 2013). By the early 2000s developments challenged an understanding of academic science as central to economic growth and national welfare, but in a way that is broadly distributed in a decentralized, win–win rather than zero-sum framework. In short, the state re-emerged as a key actor engaged in rivalries, zero-sum type competition for dominance in academic science, even if the ultimate geopolitical power of academic science is moderate.

Post-2001 Geopolitical Environment

The macro-political environment constitutes one set of developments affecting the geopolitics of higher education. Beginning with the terrorist attacks on September 11, 2001 and subsequent wars, geopolitical tensions that may have been easing following the Cold War increased, and inter-state conflict increased. Much more recently, the ascendance of nationalist governments in many countries seems to compound international tensions. Under such conditions, it seems likely that all forms of competition and intra-state rivalries are likely to increase, including for academic science.

Within the sector, world university rankings have proven to have a profound effect. In 2003 the Academic Rankings of World Universities, at that time associated with Shanghai Jiao Tong University, was first issued. Other world rankings systems, including from Times Higher Education, quickly followed. Rankings have had the effect of motivating national policy in many countries to compete with others for position (Hazelkorn, 2013).

LINES OF COMPETITION

Geopolitics involves competition between states. Countries compete in many ways. In this section we consider what we believe to be two prominent lines of competition in the geopolitics of academic science. The first is competition for internationally mobile researchers. The second is competition to develop the strongest research universities.

Competition for Talent

As national higher education systems push to enhance and invest in domestic capacity, international mobility and flows of students, scholars, and researchers continue to complicate the notion of global academic science. The past two decades saw significant growth in the numbers of students pursuing degrees overseas at both the undergraduate and graduate levels, a boom in international postdoctorates, the unseen workforce propelling many labs (Cantwell and Lee, 2010), and increasing international mobility of faculty engaged in both short- and long-term appointments across the globe (Jacob and Meek, 2013). Internationally mobile students and scholars are important for at least two reasons. First, in most systems overseas students pay fees, and so enrollment of international students at scale is a major industry. Second, international graduate students and researchers often make substantial scientific contributions and can increase national capacity in academic science. It is important to note that talent competition is in some respects a zero-sum game because the movement of a scientist to one country means that the scientist is not working in any other country (save a rather small cadre of truly transnational researchers).

In 2014, 4.3 million students studied in tertiary education programs outside of their home countries (UNESCO, 2016). China, India, South Korea, and Saudi Arabia lead as the world’s largest sender countries, while the United States, the United Kingdom, Australia, and France lead as the top study destinations. Despite continued absolute growth in the number of international students at higher education institutions (HEIs) in the United States, coupled with the United States’ traditional leading role as a host country, recent figures suggest that the historical dominance of the United States as a study destination is being eroded. According to statistics from the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD), the US market share of internationally mobile post-secondary students shrunk from 23 percent in 2000 to 16 percent in 2012 (OECD, 2014). Forecasts for future enrollment, furthermore, predict that although the total number of internationally mobile students is likely to grow, traditional study destinations such as the United States are expected to lose out as traditional sender countries, such as China, build higher education capacity and in turn become host countries themselves. A report by the British Council’s Education Intelligence predicts that as the United States, the United Kingdom, and other traditional host countries lose market share, internationally mobile students will choose to study at institutions within their own regions (British Council, 2015).

Ben Wildavsky (2012) argues in The Great Brain Race that the international movement of minds is likely to benefit all participants worldwide. The concept of the “brain race” is the latest of “brain” buzzwords related to the geographic movement of talent. The concept of a “brain drain,” first used by the British Royal Society in describing the outflow of scientists and engineers after the Second World War (Balmer et al., 2009), is perhaps the most familiar of the “brain” concepts. More recently, the notion of the brain drain has been conceptualized to refer more broadly to the loss of talent or human capital resulting often from economic migration from one geographical location to another (Miyagiwa, 1991). On the other side of the brain drain sits the “brain gain,” where more economically advanced localities enjoy the self-perpetuating benefits of attracting and retaining human capital (Stark et al., 1997). More complicated flows of human capital have been termed as “brain return” (or “reversing the brain drain”), where talents trained overseas and returning to their home country (Mayr and Peri, 2009; Zweig, 2006); “brain circulation,” where “highly skilled personnel” remain in host countries for a short time and follow international job opportunities (Cao, 1996, p. 269); and “brain exchange,” where human capital flows move between areas and there is no net loss or gain (Pellegrino, 2001).

Regardless of terminology, the statistics on mobility presented above do indeed imply high levels of “brain movement.” Jacob and Meek (2013, p. 339) argue that international movement of students and scholars is due to “economic globalization on the one hand, and an academic arms race for the best and brightest, fueled by an aging and retiring academic professoriate in many developed countries, and the need for highly skilled human capital in developing countries, on the other.” In the context of global academic science, certain patterns and trends imply that policy at the institutional or national level can have an effect on where human capital goes and whether it stays, ultimately deciding whether or not, as Wildavsky (2012, p. 5) argues, the “free trade of minds” is able to break past zero-sum thinking of brain drain and brain gain. Government investment in and favorable policy toward higher education, for example, can influence brain flows by attracting talent to well-funded research positions in well-equipped labs. Even so, countries enact policies to monopolize the time and talents of researchers because they believe that this sort of “capture” is of benefit. In China, for instance, several policies and programs seek to attract global talents to research and faculty programs at Chinese universities, such as the 2011 National Recruitment Program of Global Experts, an extension of the 2008 Thousand Talents Plan (1000 Plan, 2008). Other countries have implemented favorable immigration policies in order to attract highly skilled persons. In Australia and Canada, notably, foreign-born individuals in skilled employment can enjoy a “transparent and predictable talent-for-citizenship exchange” which eases and hastens the flow of talent into these countries (Shachar, 2006). Use of citizenship in wealthy, stable, and desirable countries to attract high-performing academic scientists demonstrates the value of considering academic science geopolitically, because it demonstrates the use of national and foreign policy to gain an edge in this competition.

What is a World-Class University?

The prowess of national academic science operations is tied to the status and competition of the universities located within specific countries. Further, the idea that research university power contributes to national economic and political influence has prompted many countries to engage in policies designed to establish “world-class universities.” The notion of “world class” when referring to universities is pervasive throughout education literature, and even more so in the brochures and official websites of institutions across the globe. The field of higher education, however, is yet to agree on a standard definition of exactly what a “world-class” university is or what it should look like (Altbach, 2004). That said, the prevailing discussion of a university’s “world-classness” is intrinsically tied to the role of global rankings as a means to measure how well a given university measures up against HEIs worldwide. Thus, a university can be considered world class if it performs well in highly regarded rankings systems such as the Shanghai Jiaotong University’s Academic Ranking of World Universities (ARWU), the Times Higher Education World University Rankings (THEWUR), or the US News Best Global Universities Ranking (BGUR). Broadly defined, a world-class university produces a high volume of high-quality scientific research, provides state-of-the-art research training, and attracts students and researchers on an international basis.

Simplifying this further, a university is world class if it is recognized as world class. One way, then, to obtain a better idea of what it means to be world class is to examine the criteria for global rankings systems and critique the ranking metrics. The three aforementioned rankings systems evaluate universities based on criteria that emphasize research reputation and research output. US News and THE, in addition to including criteria and indicators measuring quantitative research output, publications and citations, also include research reputation indicators based on survey data (Morse et al., 2016; Times Higher Education, 2016). Shanghai Jiao Tong’s ARWU does not include reputational survey data, but adds the number of Nobel Prize and Field Medal winners that are included amongst a university’s alumni or faculty (Shanghai Jiao Tong University, 2015). Due to these differences in methodology, each ranking system produces a slightly different list of top universities. HEIs that consistently maintain high positions, however, are those that promote and maintain a robust research capacity. Thus, when considering what makes a university “world class,” research productivity in general, and science research in particular, is at the heart of this discussion. Despite controversy or questions over shortcomings (Marginson and Wende, 2007; Hazelkorn, 2013), these rankings are widely referenced by institutions and higher education scholars when they consider what it means to be world class, mostly because by these rankings order universities in a way that is easily consumed by the broadest audience (Salmi and Saroyan, 2007).

Other scholars agree that research is a central component of world-class universities, but offer other perspectives on what constitutes such an institution. Salmi (2009) provides a visual model for depicting how key characteristics align in a world-class university. In his conceptualization, concentration of talent (students, teaching staff, researchers, and internationalization) combines with abundant resources (public budget resources, endowment revenues, tuition fees, and research grants), and favorable governance (supportive regulatory framework, autonomy/academic freedom, leadership team, strategic vision, and a culture of excellence) to form a world-class university (WCU).

While these characterizations of WCUs are useful in providing understanding of the shared features possessed by universities recognized as world class, it is ultimately the institution’s capacity in research productivity that determines whether or not it is world class. Alongside the discussion of WCUs, a related body of literature discusses the role of the research university in a competitive global knowledge economy. Mohrman et al. (2008) characterize the emerging global model (EGM) of the research university to describe a worldwide trend in which top research universities maintain eight common characteristics. These characteristics are: “global mission, research intensity, new roles for professors, diversified funding, worldwide recruitment, increasing complexity, new relationships with government and industry, and global collaboration with similar institutions” (Mohrman et al., 2008, p. 5). In describing the same set of institutions as well as similar phenomena and policy preferences as discussions on world-class universities, the authors allude to “world class” only once in the article, in a discussion of the role of university rankings. Nevertheless, research remains a core component in Mohrman et al.’s EGM conceptualization.

The world-class university project, or the new global model for research universities, has indeed contributed to greater research output across the board and to higher levels of collaboration among scholars and universities. These changes, however, are not without their critics. As countries or universities aspire to increase their research capacities, a zero-sum mentality has led to the concentration of resources at a few elite universities and within certain disciplines (science, engineering, and technology), depriving other institutions and fields of necessary funds (Deem et al., 2008). In many cases, such concentration has led to a bifurcation of education systems between elite, world-class universities and demand-absorbing institutions. Moreover, several scholars argue that the notion of world class “has increasingly come to be synonymous with Western” (Birnbaum, 2007, p.9), and that “the quest for international benchmarking or the urge for integration with the global system could be developed as processes in asserting dominant groups’ interests and identities but subjugating those of the smaller or less developed ones” (Mok, 2007, p.448). Faculty in non-native English-speaking countries who wish to engage in publication, and international scientific collaborations or discussions, often need to publish in English and adopt methods dominant in international research journals (Lillis and Curry, 2010). International organizations (IOs) and the World Bank in particular play a role in shaping the conversation around higher education among policy-makers at the system and local and institutional levels by indexing countries and institutions and laying out best practices for higher education systems and institutions (Shahjahan, 2012).

The Research University’s Role in the Economy and Society

Why do governments invest in research universities in order to establish institutions that are ostensibly competitive beyond national borders? The answer to this question likely lies in assumptions about the significance of research universities to contemporary economic and social development. As Altbach and Salmi (2011, p. 3) explain: “Research universities are considered among the central institutions of the 21st-century knowledge economies.” The centrality of these institutions is dependent upon ideas of contemporary social and economic organizations. Post-industrial forms of economic and social organization may rely on the production of knowledge, its application to practical problems, and its use by skilled workers, rather than on the exploitation of natural resources and unskilled labor. As a result research universities, which both generate knowledge and train “knowledge workers,” are often seen as central. Research universities are characterized by having adequate infrastructure in laboratories, libraries, and other facilities to support high-quality knowledge production and dissemination (Altbach, 2007). Within their national contexts, research universities often operate at the elite level and educate only a small portion of students. These same institutions are also responsible for educating the majority of graduate and PhD students, and account for the majority of the research output (Altbach, 2011). The model of the research university and the concept of the world-class university have been pursued both implicitly and explicitly through targeted policy initiatives worldwide. A large volume of recent scholarship has examined the pursuit of excellence initiatives in both the developed and the developing world. The motivation and level of interest varies along with the conditions and capacities of each country (Altbach and Balan, 2007). Nevertheless, the focus of bolstering the nation’s knowledge production capacity by investing in research universities is a clear and growing global trend. We can consider a few regional national policies to demonstrate the extent and scope of this effort.

Since the start of the twenty-first century the European Union (EU) has stated explicitly that higher education and especially investment in research capacity is central to its strategy to strengthen the Union and its member states. Blustering the capacity of research universities, drawing skilled labor to the region through academic networks, and translating scientific discovery into product and service innovation, the EU aims to be the most competitive region in the world. For example, in 2004 Germany established an excellence initiative designed to channel resources into the highest-performing academic research centers in order to improve the global standing of German academic science. As Barbra Kehm (2013, pp. 93–94) explained, “it can be said that the Excellence Initiative was based on a political prognosis of the global competitiveness of the German higher education, research and innovation system which had identified a number of problems.” By stratifying the higher education system through resource concentration, the government hoped to strengthen Germany’s economic and political prowess.

Outside of regional frameworks, individual countries have also launched programs to promote world-class universities. Beginning in 2006 the Russian government established an ambitious program to improve the standing of Russian universities internationally. Concerned that Russian universities lagged behind international competitors, the government advanced a set of reforms to increase university autonomy while also targeting funds to boost research production, with the explicit goal of improving standing in world rankings (even if the government contested the methods used in dominant ranking systems) (Smolentseva, 2015). Investment in Russian research capacity can be understood as part of a larger project to improve the prestige and power of Russia in the post-Soviet era.

Perhaps the most aggressive and notable efforts to promote world-class universities come from Asia. Scholars often point to China as the quintessential example of pursuing world-class universities through national policy and investment. The government-initiated “211” and “985” projects both utilize a target and investment schema for selecting top universities and prioritizing them for funding. The 211 Plan, initiated in 1995, selected around 100 of the nation’s top universities with the vision of improving research capacity through standards and investment (Ministry of Education, 2008). Five years later, the 985 Plan named nine elite Chinese universities for selective funding. The program was eventually expanded to 39 (Ministry of Education, 2011). To get an idea of scope, one policy analysis posted that, “In percentage terms, the 985 Project initiated by the Chinese government is a critical component of one of the largest sustained increases of investment in university research in human history” (Zhang et al., 2013, p. 765). Studies and measures have already indicated that with these standards and investments, many universities have been successful in improving quality and increasing research output, climbing the rankings accordingly (Zhang et al., 2013). It is impossible to understand China’s efforts to enhance the research capacity of its universities outside of the context of the country’s waxing economic and political power.

Japan has four universities in the top 100 (ARWU). Yet the Japanese have initiated plans to promote the status of education. The processes was initiated with a 2008 plan to foster 30 world-class institutions, referred to as the “Global 30” project (Oba, 2008), later revised to select research units as “Centers of Excellence in the 21st Century” (Yonewawa and Shimmi, 2011). One reason that Japan continues to struggle advancing more universities higher in rankings might be due in part to standards for English language scholarship and publication across the hard and soft sciences (Ishikawa, 2009). Japan’s efforts to enhance the standing of its best research universities reflect the struggles that even wealthy non-Western countries have in establishing prominent intuitions that aid in political, cultural, and economic influence.

In Korea, government-initiated projects sought to promote research output by directly funding academic research proposals in the Brain Korea 21 project, begun in 1999 (Shin, 2009), and recruiting internationally renowned faculty through the World Class University (WCU) project (Byun et al., 2012). Initial evaluation of these programs suggests that although research production in Korea did increase after policy implementation, Korea still continued to lag behind global and regional leaders and failed to “lessen the gap between Korean universities and world-class universities in the US or Japan in number of publications” (Shin, 2009, p.679). In other words, scientific output is seen as a line of geopolitical contest for Korean policy-makers.

There are, of course, examples beyond Germany, Russia, China, Japan, and Korea. Means and goals of the policies are different in each case, yet they share the geopolitical objectives of seeking to improve the country’s capacity to facilitate innovation and economic competitiveness, enhance the country’s reputation and prestige, and to attract new skills and workers to the jurisdiction. All of these seek to advance the ultimate geopolitical goal of enhancing state power.

CONTEMPORARY LANDSCAPE

It is reasonable to ask to what extent policy strategies, like those described above, are able to change the balance of power in academic science. It is commonly claimed that the United States occupies a hegemonic position in academic science. But empires do not last forever and the past decades have seen a pluralization of scientific power. The United States remains the largest performer of research and development and produces more science than any other country in the world, though the magnitude of this advantage is declining.

One way to consider the scientific power of a country is by examining aggregate levels of domestic research and development (R&D) expenditures. This method is admittedly crude because it includes all public and private sources of research expenditures, including those expenditures that occur outside of academic settings. But it is nevertheless a useful benchmark for assessing overall scientific activity and is a handy device to demonstrate changes in the relative position of several countries. The first set of columns in Table 8.1 show the top 20 countries in terms of R&D expenditures in 2015, and the second set of columns report on the same countries in 1990. Some remarkable differences are observed.

The United States leads R&D spending in both periods but spent notably less in real terms in 2015 than in 1990. Over the past quarter-century the absolute position of the US in terms of R&D expenditures has declined. The relative status of R&D spending has changed even more dramatically. In 1990, the US spent nearly four times as much as the second-ranked country, Japan; but in 2015 the gap between second-placed China and the US was less than $100 million. China spent more than 27 times as much in 2015 as it did in 1990. South Korea also emerged as a top ten R&D country in 2015. Germany, which dropped from the third to the fourth spot, nearly doubled its R&D expenditures over the period. The United Kingdom increased spending but nonetheless fell a place on the table. What is clear from these data is that the gap between the US and the rest of the world has narrowed considerably, and that new players, and especially China, have realigned the R&D funding landscape. Overall the balance of activity is moving East and (to a lesser extent) South.

Another way to assess the academic science is by measuring scientific paper production. Between 1997 and 2013 the number of scientific papers produced annually grew by 270 percent, from more than 0.5 million to more than 2 million papers. The United States was the largest producer of papers during the entire period. However, the relative position of the US declined. In 1997 the US produced more than three out of every ten papers published in the world. By 2013 that figure had dropped to fewer than two out of every ten (Table 8.2).

The rise of China as a scientific superpower is perhaps the most remarkable observation to be drawn from these data. In 1997 China produced just 2 percent of the world’s science papers, but this figure rocketed to 18 percent by 2013. China now rivals the United States in terms of the volume of paper production. Countries such as India, South Korea, Turkey, and Iran also impressively grew their number of papers produced over the period.

Historical European scientific powers such as Germany, the United Kingdom, and France all grew their paper output since the late 1990s but at a rate slower than global growth.

From these broad trends a reasonable picture of the contemporary landscape of academic science emerges. The world spends far more on R&D today than in the 1990s, and one result has been impressive growth in the number of science papers published by individual countries and in total worldwide. Competition among states to expand scientific capacity has resulted in a world that produces much more science than in the recent past. The United States remains the strongest producer of academic science in the world, but the US advantage has declined since the 1990s. The rise of the People’s Republic of China (PRC) has been tremendous since the 1990s and the PRC is now an academic science global power. A reasonable description is that the global landscape of academic science includes three centers of power and numerous “mid-major” (to borrow a term from US college sports) players. The United States, China, and Europe (the cohesion of post-Brexit European science is yet to be seen) are anchoring global powers in academic science. But the system is also pluralizing in that the US advantage is shrinking and a number of countries are now reasonably important producers of academic science.

TOWARDS A NEW CONCEPTUALIZATION OF THE GEOPOLITICS OF ACADEMIC SCIENCE

The global concepts of academic science that developed in the 1990s attended closely to public private interactions and to the movement of ideas, capital, and talent. The globalization of academic science is well established and we agree that much academic science is produced through transnational production networks (Kauppinen and Cantwell, 2014); competition for internationally mobile researchers as discussed above is one example of this. Such conceptualizations do not and should not exclude the role of nation-states. And when considering the geopolitics of academic science it may be useful to bring the state to the fore. Given the rise of nationalisms and state-centric competition, we turn now to the concepts for understanding the geopolitics of academic science. In doing so, it is worth considering concepts that explicitly pay attention to the dynamics of nation-states and the global order.

Power in academic science has shifted eastward. So, then, perhaps the origins of the concepts we used to analyze the geopolitics of academic science should reflect this shift. The term “techno-nationalism” (or sometimes “scientific-nationalism”) refers to the policies or mindset within a given state to promote and protect scientific and technological advancements within the national context (Ostry and Nelson, 1995). The term originated in Japan, which during the 1960s, 1970s, and 1980s developed scientific and technical capacity in order to advance national interests. Nakayama was the first to translate the Japanese phrase 技術立国 or gijutsu rikkoku (literally meaning “using technology to build the nation”) to “techno-nationalism.” Since then, techno-nationalism has been used in the literature in reference to “the state of affairs in which technology is promoted by a nation state and for the sake of national interest” (Nakayama, 2012, p.11). Much of this related discussion focused on the policies of East Asian countries – South Korea, Japan, and China – to promote and protect domestic science and technology in the wake of the Second World War and to sustain and grow economically during the Cold War. Techno-nationalist policies often took the form of protecting domestic firms from foreign competition through import substitution, while simultaneously investing in indigenous R&D and innovative capacities, particularly those with military or national security applications (Ostry and Nelson, 1995).

The idea of techno-nationalism gave way to techno-globalism in the second half of the twentieth century (Ostry and Nelson, 1995; Nakayama, 2012). While techno-nationalism relied on a national security justification, techno-globalism is rooted in international market-oriented forces of global trade and production networks. In other words, technology, innovation, and R&D now transcend national borders and give rise to global innovation networks (GINs), or the alignment of “dispersed engineering, product development, and research activities across geographic borders” (Ernst, 2009, p.1). Where the state is the central actor under techno-nationalist policies, in a techno-globalized world the profit-oriented firm is paramount. In addition to the firm, techno-globalism incorporates a variety of international actors from industry, government, and academia and implies a more complex, yet cooperative worldview (Ostry and Nelson, 1995). This cooperative worldview is evident in the 1990s-era concepts examined at the start of the chapter. Universities and individual researchers often play an important role in the complicated cooperatives that make up GINs. Britto et al. (2013) posit that there are four ways in which universities participate in GINs. The first involves university–industry partnerships within a national context. The second enters the international dimension with a transnational enterprise forming a partnership with a university in its home country. The third adds another level of complexity as transnational enterprises interact with universities in both their home and host countries. The final type of relationship consists of consortia of firms, universities, and research institutes, usually coalescing around an international mission (Britto et al., 2013, pp. 81–83). Despite higher levels of cooperation in a technoglobalized world, such cooperation has not resulted in a flat and equal playing field for all participants. North America, Europe, and Japan maintain dominance in GINs, even as other countries are playing catch-up (Ernst, 2009).

Academic science is now, it seems, at once a global and cooperative enterprise (with vestiges of 1990s concepts), and an nationalist endeavor designed to bolster state power relative to rivals. Adequately conceptualizing the geopolitics of academic science requires taking both realities into account. Scholars have recently posited the idea of “neo-techno-nationalism” (Shim and Shin, 2016) or “techno-glocalism” (Felker, 2009) to explain how some countries are seeking to attract global markets and transnational corporations (TNCs) with the intent of protecting and promoting domestic capacity. Such a characterization indicates a move toward techno-nationalism, but in a way that continues to include elements of techno-globalism. Felker (2009, p.479) argues that countries in Southeast Asia pursue a form of techno-glocalization which is rooted in a “governance bargain; in which host governments, in exchange for technological upgrading, facilitate TNC’s exercise of governance authority transnationally as well as in coordinating local investments in innovation.” In exploring government policies within the Chinese telecommunications sector, Shim and Shin (2016, p.206) advanced that such national science and technology programs and policies took on a form of neo-techno-nationalism in which China leveraged advantages of globalization to pursue national security and economic interests. These observations seem to suggest that despite their integration in GINs, countries in the periphery are not content with their position in such networks and thus seek to advance their standing. Where techno-globalism seems to place the interest of the nation-state below that of multinational enterprises, “Neo-techno-nationalism, by contrast, seeks to enhance local innovation capabilities to create leverage over the distribution of economic rewards within transnationally integrated production networks” (Felker, 2009, p.475).

While the literature on techno-nationalism, techno-globalism, and neo-techno-nationalism often does not address the role of academic research directly, policy and programmatic orientations toward either nationalism or globalism are certain to have implications for higher education institutions. As had been pointed out elsewhere (see Perkmann et al., 2013) academy-industry partnerships in R&D, which often drive collaborations under techno-globalism, have been criticized for placing emphasis on applied research at the expense of basic research. With the high pace of advanced, international technological development, it is difficult to incentivize long-term, sustained research projects (Ostry and Nelson, 1995, p. 110).

If we are indeed seeing a movement towards neo-techno-nationalism, we might anticipate a harder line being taken by countries such as China. As they hope to benefit from globalization, while protecting maintenance of territorial social, economic, and political control, academic science within China may continue to engage global science on terms of its own choosing. The goal of such a strategy is to absorb the benefits of open exchange and cooperation without ceding any authority of the state. But China is not alone in seeking a geopolitical position that is open to economic and technical exchange through academic science, but seeks to control the conditions in which academic science is performed and to monopolize the benefits for national advantage. During the Brexit debate advocates for the “leave” the EU position claimed that the United Kingdom (UK), including British universities and science, could maintain their global position while at the same time regulating the ways in which UK universities engaged with Europe (and the world) in order to ensure enduring Britishness and regain regulatory independence from Brussels. The Trump administration in the US has advanced an “America First” policy, and as of June 2017 Trump’s budget proposals include deep cuts to programs that support educational and scientific exchange. Some observers have speculated, with good reason, that Brexit and Trump’s policies will diminish the global standing of Anglo-American academic science. The future will show if this prediction proves correct. But it is also worth noting that these developments, while jarring to the prevailing order, may reflect a new geopolitics of academic science. They may also reflect differential success in strategy. The rise of China as a scientific superpower is indicative of savvy techno-nationalism, where early developments indicate that nationalism in the US and UK reflect short-sighted reactionary sentiments rather than a winning strategy.

Extending the concepts discussed above, we may be entering a period of cultural-economic nationalism, coupled with a technological-information globalism. Information and knowledge could be widely shared and transformed, commercial technology exchanged, while the production of academic science could occur in regimes that are regulated to achieve ethnocentric and nationalist aims. In such an eventuality, the geopolitics of academic science may reflect a constant tension not to compete for top talent, as described in a previous section, but to reap the gains of global technology development for national purposes while struggling to maintain production models that are closed and serve national political and cultural objectives. Under this scenario relative advantage, in a zero-sum calculus, would be prized; while the 1990s-era absolute growth and innovation emphasis may be left behind. It is tempting and probably partially correct to view the possible move to a nationalist geopolitics of higher education as the result of right-wing populism. But it is worth observing that we do not anticipate all elements of global science to recede. Publishing in international venues and the cross-border exchange of research design and ideas seem likely to remain the prime vehicles for registering the outputs of academic science. Further, the zero-sum mentality of a nationalist-techno-globalist geopolitics is baked into the world rankings and world-class university movements. And these developments are related to the expansive and open view of academic science developed in the 1990s. In other words, the move to nationalism in academic science is less a departure of the current geopolitical order than a transformation of the existing order to serve nationalist interests.
