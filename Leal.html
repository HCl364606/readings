Locating Cultural Holes Brokers in Diffusion Dynamics Across Bright Symbolic Boundaries

Abstract

Although the literature on cultural holes has expanded considerably in recent years, there is no concrete measure in that literature to locate cultural holes brokers. This article develops a conceptual framework grounded in social network theory and cultural sociology to propose a specific solution to fill this measurement gap. Agent-based computational experiments are leveraged to develop a theoretical test of the analytic purchase and distinctiveness of the proposed measure, termed potential for intercultural brokerage (PIB). Results demonstrate the effectiveness of PIB in locating early adopters that can achieve widespread levels of diffusion in societies segregated along bright symbolic boundaries. Findings also show the superiority of PIB when compared to classic alternative measures in the network literature that focus on locating early adopters based on structural holes (e.g., network constraint, effective size), geodesics (e.g., betweenness centrality), and degree (e.g., degree centrality), among other classic network measures. Broader implications of these findings for brokerage theory are discussed herein.

Keywords

Brokerage, structural holes, cultural holes, agent-based modeling, diffusion

Introduction

What type of actors are better positioned to effectively diffuse innovations widely? To answer this question, a stream of research has focused on the role of sociometric (network) space. That literature suggests certain strategies to achieve widespread diffusion, like targeting “popular” individuals, that is, actors with a wealth of network connections; or targeting “structural holes” brokers, namely, actors linking sociometrically disconnected people (see Burt [1992] on the concept of structural holes). Experimental evidence does show that actors’ network positions are causally related to the diffusion of innovations (Aral and Walker 2012; Centola 2010; Paluck, Sheperd, and Aronow 2016). A second stream of research shows that the diffusion of innovations is greatly facilitated when actors are close in cultural space, that is, when actors are similar along dimensions such as gender, ethnicity, or political ideology (Axelrod 1997; DiMaggio and Garip 2011; Strang and Meyer 1993)—even when actors’ social networks are held constant experimentally (Centola 2011) or when actors are actually not sociometrically connected at all (Burt 2005; Goldberg and Stein 2018; Suh, Shi, and Brashears 2017).

This study contributes to the literature on the identification of innovators capable of diffusing innovations widely, that is, actors well-suited to minimize intergroup inequalities in the adoption of innovations (Aral and Walker 2012; DiMaggio and Garip 2011; Gondal 2015; Paluck, Sheperd, and Aronow 2016). This contribution is realized here in two ways. First and foremost, this paper proposes a new measure, termed Potential for Intercultural Brokerage (PIB), as a principled way to locate ‘cultural holes’ brokers, that is, actors that connect culturally segregated alters (see Pachucki and Breiger [2010] and the Theory section for details on the concept of cultural holes). This is critical since, as explained in detail below, there is no concrete measure in the literature on cultural holes to locate cultural holes brokers. Such measurement issue is present even in the two foundational pieces that introduced the concept of cultural holes (Breiger 2010; Pachucki and Breiger 2010). Second, through a novel agent-based model (ABM), this study develops an in silico experimental design where the diffusion potential of both structural holes brokers and cultural holes brokers is directly compared. By doing so, the ABM introduced here provides a theoretical test of the purchase and distinctiveness of PIB, and of cultural holes brokers in general, to understand diffusion dynamics in segregated societies.

As discussed later in the paper, correlation analyses, detailed in the Appendix in the supplemental material, based on 12 different real-world networks offer support regarding the empirical distinctiveness of PIB vis-à-vis classic sociometric brokerage measures. That said, performing a fully fledged empirical test of the in silico findings reported here is outside the scope of this paper. There is a well-established literature that focuses on developing similar in silico tests of theoretical statements (e.g., hypotheses, expectations) based on simulation models. This paper aims to contribute to that literature (e.g., Axelrod 1980; Carley 1991; Centola and Macy 2007; DellaPosta, Shi, and Macy 2015; Epstein 2004; Goldberg and Stein 2018; Kitts 2006a; Nowak, Szamrej, and Latané 1990; Watts and Strogatz 1998).

Overall, the main contribution of this paper is to show that actors wellpositioned to span cultural holes based on PIB—the first measure of cultural holes brokerage proposed in the literature thus far—are highly effective at diffusing innovations widely when compared to conventional structural holes brokers located based on measures such as network constraint, effective size, betweenness centrality, or degree centrality, among others. Broader implications of these results for both the diffusion of innovations literature and the brokerage literature are provided in the Discussion session. For replicability purposes, Appendix A in the supplemental material contains a standalone version of the ABM implemented in the free specialized software NetLogo (Wilensky 1999), as well as the data related to all main outputs from the computational experiments.

Theory

This study borrows two concepts from cultural sociology: (a) symbolic boundaries, understood as cultural and conceptual classification systems to separate practices, people, and objects (e.g., citizenship regimes, musical genres, organizational (sub)cultures) (Lamont and Molnár 2002; Schachter 2016); and (b) cultural holes, that is, “contingencies of meaning, practice, and discourse that enable social structure” (Breiger 2010: 39). The concept of cultural holes “points to the structuring of boundaries and the lack of com plete connections among cultural forms.” (Pachucki and Breiger 2010: 216; emphasis added). In other words, cultural holes demarcate the contours of symbolic boundaries, that is, the existence of disconnections between cultural forms (e.g., national identities, musical genres, organizational vocabularies) and the actors that produce such forms (e.g., states, musicians, managers) (Lizardo 2014; Tasselli, Zappa, and Lomi 2020; Vilhena et al. 2014).

The “cultural stuff” (Barth 1969: 15) that symbolic boundaries enclose (e.g., meanings, practices, tastes) is key to signaling membership in social groups or categories (e.g., citizens vs. foreigners; native speakers vs. nonnative speakers; working class vs. middle class vs. upper class). Erecting and preserving such boundaries, that is, actively sustaining symbolic divisions between social categories (i.e., ‘holes’ in cultural space), is thus critical to (re)producing durable inequalities between groups (Burt 2005: 237–240; Tilly 1998: 8–9). This paper aims to study the role of a certain class of actors, namely, cultural holes brokers, in the spanning of such symbolic divisions by both proposing a measure to locate said cultural brokers, and by evaluating their ability to diffuse innovations across members of categorically distinct groups.

Alba (2005) has suggested an important theoretical distinction between fuzzy (or blurred) symbolic boundaries and bright symbolic boundaries. Fuzzy boundaries denote cultural classification systems that are relatively easy for an actor to change, cross, or straddle (e.g., musical genres, academic disciplines).1 Bright boundaries denote cultural classification systems that are relatively difficult for an actor to change, cross, or straddle (e.g., citizenship regimes, ethnoracial boundaries). Unlike fuzzy boundaries, which are recurrently studied in the formal diffusion literature on opinion change (Carley 1991; Macy et al. 2003; DellaPosta, Shi, and Macy 2015; Goldberg and Stein 2018; Mark 1998; Nowak, Szamrej, and Latané 1990), bright boundaries can act as formidable barriers to intergroup diffusion precisely because they demarcate distinct sociocultural worlds that are significantly taxing to span (Alba 2005; Anzaldúa 1987; Schachter 2016). Put simply, when symbolic boundaries are erected—especially bright ones—the emergence of short-circuits or disconnections, that is, holes in social space, can be expected to follow.

Until recently, the imagery of a hole in social space almost unequivocally meant a hole in sociometric space, a structural hole (Burt 1992). To advance brokerage theory and measurement, this section theorizes about the lack of connections in social space, both sociometric space and cultural space, as a general phenomenon. In doing so, this section also helps fill a gap highlighted in a prominent review of the brokerage literature, namely, understanding how “the prevalence and character of brokerage is closely connected to the macrolevel structure of a time or place.” (Stovel and Shaw 2012: 153, emphasis added).

The theoretical framework proposed below focuses on two macro-level conditions for the emergence of holes in social space and, ultimately, for the rise of brokers that can span such holes. The two macro-level conditions considered here are the level of closure of sociometric space, and the level of brightness of the symbolic boundaries demarcating cultural space. First, the more (less) closure there is in a group, the denser (sparser) the web of sociometric connections, and the lower (higher) the chances of observing structural holes. Second, the brighter (fuzzier) symbolic boundaries are, the harder (easier) it would be to change, cross, or straddle them and, thus, the more (less) these symbolic boundaries would act as siloes shaping the contours of cultural holes. Quadrant A in Figure 1 (Monocultural Communities) depicts an ideal-type scenario in which very few or no holes in either sociometric or cultural space exist. Theoretically, groups in this quadrant are close to Tönnies Gemeinschaft (community) and its characteristic mechanical solidarity. Empirically, hunter-gatherer societies with a homogeneous culture in which all members are also connected to each other, such as local/territorial groups of the Nukak people in Colombia (Cabrera, Franky, and Mahecha 1999),2 would be located in quadrant A. Highly homogenous schools (e.g., elite boarding schools) or abbeys and monasteries would also be located in quadrant A as long as their members are connected to (most of) all other members.

Quadrant B in Figure 1 (Multicultural Simmelian Systems) depicts an ideal type scenario in which a group would exhibit holes in cultural space along bright boundaries and very few or no holes in sociometric space. These are the kind of spaces where Simmelian bridges proliferate. A Simmelian bridge is a tie that connects two nodes that are: (a) located on different sides of a cultural boundary; while (b) being sociometrically connected to all the same third parties (Tortoriello and Krackhardt 2010). Examples of groups located in quadrant B would be that of transnational or mixed migratory status families (Roche et al. 2015), where all members are sociometrically connected through kinship ties while, at the same time, they can also sit on opposite sides of bright symbolic boundaries that are relatively difficult to span (e.g., language, citizenship). Cohesive groups with tokens along gender or race lines, such as men-dominated board of directors with very few women (Torchia, Calabró, and Huse 2011), would also be examples of groups in quadrant B.

Quadrant C in Figure 1 (Enclaves) depicts an ideal-type scenario in which a group would exhibit holes in sociometric space and very few or no holes in cultural space along bright symbolic boundaries. An example of groups located in quadrant C would be that of large, radical, and highly culturally homogenous white supremacist organizations such as the Ku Klux Klan in the U.S. or the Nazi party under Hitler’s rule. Here it is argued that, since bright symbolic boundaries are ubiquitous in contemporary societies (Alba 2005; Anzaldúa 1987; Moody 2001; Schachter 2016), developing concrete measures to locate actors spanning cultural holes is key to understanding current brokerage dynamics. As such, this paper conceptually focuses on societies in which: (a) bright boundaries exist and segregate actors in cultural space (i.e., brightness is high); and (b) there are varying degrees of network closure describing actors’ connectivity in sociometric space (i.e., closure goes from low to high). That is, the analysis here focuses on societies all along the theoretical continuum between quadrants B and D in Figure 1. In that context, to evaluate the analytic purchase of the brokerage measure proposed here, the following expectation is proposed:

When sociocultural space is comprised by a set of bright boundaries, the diffusion potential of actors that mediate contact across culturally dissimilar and disconnected parties (cultural holes brokers) will tend to outperform the diffusion potential of actors located in sociometrically advantageous positions, including actors that span structural holes (structural holes brokers).

Introducing PIB

This paper uses an agent-based model (ABM) with “low-dimensional realism” (Bruch and Atwell 2015), that is, an ABM based on a relatively small number of parameters. Specifically, only three “central” parameters, explained in detail in this section, are manipulated in the ABM developed here: (a) seed innovator type (I), the kind of agent selected to be the early adopter, or ‘seed’ innovator; (b) Probability of In-group Selection (S), agents’ probability of being connected to in-group alters in sociometric space; and (c) Consolidation (C), the level of segregation in cultural space resulting from agents’ positions in a set of bright boundaries. The first finding that deserves to be highlighted in Figure 5, panel A, is that it reproduces a key qualitative pattern reported by Centola (2015) and Zhao and Garip (2021), namely, the finding that relatively high levels of segregation, that is, relatively high levels of Consolidation (C) and In-group Selection Bias (S) (i.e.,0.5≤S,C<1)( \mathrm { i . e . , } 0 . 5 \leq S , C < 1 ) , are needed to observe diffusion (see also Burt 2005: Chapters 2 and 3; van Wijk et al. 2013). Relatedly, and perhaps unsurprisingly, results also show that in extremely segregated societies (i.e., S and C > .9), the cross-cutting ties needed for widespread diffusion are very few or non-existent, leading to relatively low overall diffusion levels (Blau and Schwartz 1984; Centola 2015).

When focusing on the region of the S, C space where widespread diffusion tends to emerge (i.e.,0.5≤S,C<1)( \mathrm { i . e . , } 0 . 5 \leq S , C < 1 ) , on average, the innovation is adopted by 39% of all agents when the seed innovator is chosen at random (Figure 5, panel A). Critically, the innovation is adopted by 70% of all agents, on average, in this same region (i.e., 0.5 ≤ S, C < 1) when the Cultural Holes Seed (CHS)—the agent with the highest PIB calculated based on agents’ group memberships in the primary boundary—is chosen as the seed innovator (Figure 5, panel B). This is the first piece of evidence showing that, under the scope conditions specified in the ABM—including both the idea that sociocultural space is segregated along bright symbolic boundaries and that the adoption of innovations is predicated on the level of cultural similarity between ego and her network neighbors—cultural brokers along bright boundaries (i.e., CHS) located through PIB are highly capable of diffusing innovations widely.

It reports a direct comparison of the overall levels of diffusion achieved by CHS and the four sociometric seed innovators introduced in Table 1: SRS, SIS, SNS, and SHS. For instance, Figure 6, panel D, shows the difference in overall diffusion between actors spanning cultural holes located based on PIB and actors spanning structural holes located based on network constraint (i.e., CHS – SHS). As can be seen in Figure 6, cultural holes brokers clearly outperform the level of diffusion achieved by the sociometric seed innovators under analysis in the region of the S, C space where diffusion tends to emerge (i.e.,0.5≤S,C<1)( \mathrm { i . e . , } 0 . 5 \leq S , C < 1 ) This is evidence in support ofthe idea that, under the scope conditions ofthe ABM, when sociocultural space is both culturally and sociometrically segregated, the diffusion potential of cultural holes brokers located through PIB tends to outperform the diffusion potential of sociometric seed innovators, including actors spanning structural holes. 14

Figure 7 reports differences in “out-group adoption,” understood as the level of adoption of alters that do not share the seed agent’s group membership in the primary boundary. In that context, no meaningful difference between cultural holes brokers and the sociometric seed innovators emerges. The combined findings from Figures 7 and 8 are critical because they show what is unique about CHS, namely, CHS are able to leverage their liminal position in-between bright boundaries to diffuse the innovation across culturally different out-group alters (Figure 7) without significantly compromising their ability to diffuse the innovation among their culturally similar in-group alters (Figure 8). Deepening the Explanation. Testing Two Counterarguments

A first alternative explanation to the results of the ABM presented here suggests that what might really matter for spreading innovations widely is a seed agent’s overall tendency to have out-group ties, irrespectively of those ties actually bridging (or not) disconnected out-group alters that happen to belong to different groups themselves. Tortoriello and Krackhardt (2010) have proposed the E–IE – I index to measure actors’ overall tendency to have outgroup ties, instead of in-group ties. The E-I index of actor i is defined as

E−Ii=Ei−IiEi+Ii E \mathrm{-} I _ {i} = \frac {E i - I i}{E i + I i}\tag{6}

where EiE _ { i } is the number of i′𝐒i \mathbf { \ ' } _ { \mathbf { S } } ties connecting her to out-group members and IiI _ { i } is the number of i′𝐒i \mathbf { \ ' } _ { \mathbf { S } } ties connecting her to in-group members.

The E-I index ranges from −1 (complete in-group orientation) to 1 (complete out-group orientation). The E-I Seed (EIS) is defined here as the actor with the highest E-I index. Tortoriello and Krackhardt (2010) provide empirical evidence that actors’ tendency to have a high E-I index is both empirically different from actors’ network constraint (i.e., access to structural holes) and predictive of actors’ tendency to innovate. Therefore, the use of the E-I index here is theoretically and empirically sensible.

A second alternative explanation suggests that any ego at the center of a two-path (i.e., actor i in Figure 2) has potential for brokerage, irrespective of her alters’ group memberships. The Structural Triadic Seed (STS) is thus defined here as the actor that is located the most times at the center of two paths in a given network. The comparison between CHS and STS is highly significant because the only difference between them is the tendency of CHS to span cultural boundaries. Technically, this means that the only difference between CHS and STS is that the latter removes the term mjkm _ { j k } from equation (1). Formally, STS is the agent with the highest Potential for Structural Triadic Brokerage (PSTB), defined as

$$ P S T B _ {i} = \sum_ {j < k} \sum_ {k} \frac {S _ {j i k}}{S _ {j k}}, \quad S _ {j i k} \neq 0 a n d i \neq j \neq k\tag{7} $$

where all the terms are as defined in equation (1).

Figure 9 shows evidence of the superiority of CHS over both EIS and STS. The evidence shows that CHS are vastly better than EIS at diffusing the innovation among in-group members (Figure 9, panel B). This suggests that focusing on out-group ties (i.e., having a high E-I index) can prevent actors from capitalizing on the in-group closure typical of segregated societies. This is a critical flaw because segregated societies (i.e., societies with high levels of in-group closure) are the most fertile ground for widespread diffusion processes to take off (see also Centola 2015; Zhao and Garip 2021).

Second, panels C and D in Figure 9 compare the out-group and in-group diffusion rates for CHS and STS, respectively. Here similar qualitative patterns to the ones already reported in Figures 7 and 8 arefound, which is evidence that by ‘turning off’ the tendency of CHS to have cross-cutting ties (i.e., by removing the term mjkfrom equation (1)), CHS end up achieving diffusion patterns similar to those of classic sociometric seeds. More specifically, CHS performs at about the same level as STS when considering in-group diffusion (Figure 9, panel D) while outperforming STS in terms of their capacity for out-group diffusion (Figure 9, panel C). Therefore, Figure 9, panel C, shows that what gives CHS their distinctive advantage are the cross-cutting ties they can leverage in culturally and sociometrically segregated societies, which are societies with high in-group closure and, therefore, where crosscutting ties are most valuable for widespread diffusion.

Discussion and Conclusion

The evidence presented here suggests that, under the scope conditions specified in the ABM introduced here—including the ideas that sociocultural space is segregated along bright symbolic boundaries and that the adoption of innovations is predicated on the level of cultural similarity between ego and her network neighbors—CHS are powerful potential spreaders when compared to a wide array of ‘pure’ sociometric seed innovators (i.e., SRS, SIS, SNS, SHS, EIS, STS). This demonstrates the analytical purchase of PIB, a muchneeded measure in the relatively new yet extensive literature on cultural holes, a literature that lacks a concrete measure to locate brokers along cultural holes.

Classic measures of the spanning of holes in sociometric space, such as network constraint (Burt 1992), are tone-deaf to the labels that distinguish cultural insiders from outsiders and, thus, to potentially help locate those spanning the cultural holes such labels can engender. This shortcoming is especially taxing when dealing with sociometrically and culturally segregated societies, like the ones modeled here. The inability of pure sociometric seeds to explicitly ‘see’ culture highlights the need to expand the prevailing theory of holes in social space, structural holes theory. It is imperative to make brokerage theory better suited to analyzing bridging in societies where holes in social space can be both sociometric and cultural (e.g., Everett and Borgatti 2012). This is truly essential since such societies are the norm, rather than the exception (Alba 2005; Anzaldúa 1987; Moody 2001; Schachter 2016). Theoretically, two key differences between structural and cultural holes brokers, explained in detail below, need further research.

First, cultural and structural brokerage likely have different implications for the brokers’ individual-level well-being and for their own identities. While extensive evidence shows that spanning structural holes is typically good for the broker’s health and material well-being (Burt 1992, 2004, 2005; Cornwell 2009; see also Buskens and van de Rijt 2008), some evidence suggests that cultural brokerage along bright symbolic boundaries can be psychologically distressing (Lämmermann 2023). Being in-between group boundaries can, for instance, give rise to identity ambiguity issues like the so-called ‘double-agent’ dilemmas (Levy et al. 2019; see also Alba 2005;

Harrell and Quinn 2023; Iorio 2022; Krackhardt 1999). More generally, being at the interstices of bright boundaries can readily spur questions about actors authenticity (Boda and Néray 2015). Thus, here it is argued that, when compared to structural holes brokers, cultural holes brokers along bright boundaries are likely to see their mental health negatively affected. This is likely to be especially true if there is a status difference between the actors being connected by the cultural broker, especially if the broker is of a lower status herself (Roche et al. 2015; Wang and Collins 2016).

The role of the brokers’ identity on brokerage behavior is something that current brokerage theory, that is, structural holes theory, tends to overlook. Indeed, much of classic social network analysis attempts to explain human behavior by giving primacy to patterns of relations such as structural equivalence, closure, or brokerage, irrespective of peoples’ cultural attributes (e.g., Burt 1992; White, Boorman, and Breiger 1976). Emirbayer and Goodwin (1994: 1414) show the extent to which this “anticategorical imperative” runs deeply through the classic social network literature. However, as put by DiMaggio (1992), social networks are necessarily structured by persons categorical attributes, that is, by culturally embedded formal and informal labels. It is not a coincidence that work documenting the emergence of cultural holes along bright symbolic boundaries highlights the key role of sharp stigmatization processes to characterize members of the out-group as ill-spirited, self-interested, abject, brutal, or atrocious (e.g., Emirbayer and Goodwin 1994; Ingram and Silverman 2016). Given their larger societal role as mediators of cultural difference, supporting the (mental) health conditions of cultural holes brokers along bright boundaries could hypothetically contribute to the development of better-integrated societies.

Second, cultural in-betweenness along bright boundaries (i.e., spanning cultural holes) can have long-lasting effects by giving rise to what some scholars call states of “permanent liminality” (Leal 2018; Menjívar 2006; Telles and Sue 2009). In fact, the “stickiness” of cultural in-betweenness can be transmitted through several generations of people that are culturally “neither here, nor there.” Anzaldúa (1987) provides accounts of fifth-generation Mexican Americans living in the borderland that are still repeatedly deported from the U.S. to Mexico due to their phenotype (see also the idea of “generations of exclusion” in Telles and Ortíz [2009]). Structural holes brokers, and their offspring, do not experience the “stickiness” of structural in-betweenness in network space (i.e., the spanning of structural holes) in such a long-lasting way. Quite the opposite, evidence suggests that structural holes brokers in organizational contexts can oscillate between periods of closure (deep embeddedness in a group) and brokerage (bridging different groups), and that actors that engage in such oscillatory behavior are actually rewarded when compared to those who maintain a stable brokering orientation (Burt and Merluzzi 2016). Clearly, the distinction between cultural and structural holes brokers is highly generative and needs further exploration. A concept like PIB can be potentially useful in such endeavors.

Future research should work on going beyond the limitations of this paper. First, brokering holes in social space is a socially learned skill and a social process (Anzaldúa 1987; Leal 2018; Wang and Collins 2016). However, unlike the bridging of structural holes, the bridging of cultural holes along bright symbolic boundaries is typically a skill learned from interactions between actors with different status (Anzaldúa 1987; Leal 2018; Wang and Collins 2016). The status dynamics tangled in the spanning of cultural holes, and how to incorporate status in future measures of brokerage across cultural holes, are exciting questions that remain unanswered. Second, more sophisticated measures of the potential for intercultural brokerage should be developed for cases where the location of cultural brokers’ positions is based on more than one (primary) boundary. Third, many different things can be diffused, from gossip and viral infections to money and goods or attitudes and behaviors (Borgatti 2005; Centola and Macy 2007; Gondal 2023). Similarly, not only networks and culture are co-constitutive (Lizardo 2006), but also different types of networks, from role relationship networks (e.g., friendship networks) to interaction networks (e.g., shared activities networks), sentiment networks (e.g., trust networks), or organizational networks, can have different impacts on diffusion and actors’ behaviors (Kitts and Leal 2021). The potential of cultural holes brokers to diffuse innovations widely should be theoretically and empirically evaluated on different types of networks and for different types of innovations. Future work should address these challenges.

Author Biography

Diego F Leal is an associate professor of sociology at the University of Arizona. He is broadly interested in the analysis of social network dynamics.
