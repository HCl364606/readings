What drives global science? The four competing narratives

Simon Marginson a,b

aHigher Education, Department of Education, University of Oxford, Oxford, UK; bInstitute of Education, National Research University Higher School of Economics, Moscow, Russia

ABSTRACT

Since 1990 there has been remarkable growth and diversification of worldwide capacity and output in science, and a distinctive global science system has emerged, primarily grounded in research universities, fostered by Internet-mediated communication and publication in English, cross-border authorship and researcher mobility. While global science overlaps with and is afected by national science systems, it is constituted by pan-national knowledge flows and collegial collaboration and has partial autonomy. Four diferent interpretive frameworks (narratives) have evolved to explain global science: science as an expanding cross-border network; science as an arms race between competing nations; science as a global market of competing ‘World-Class Universities’; and science as a centre-periphery hierarchy in which emerging countries are permanently constrained by Euro-American dominance. The paper reviews each narrative in relation to the literature, especially in scientometrics, and empirical tendencies in global science, tracked in secondary data derived from bibliometric collections. While each narrative contains at least a grain of truth, each also conflicts with the others and each is radically insuficient. A better explanation of the drivers of global science combines (1) flat open networked relations with (2) the inequalities and closures shaped by global hegemony, arbitrarily modified by (3) national governments and specific resources.

KEYWORDS

Science; research; globalisation; networked collaboration; university rankings; world systems theory

Introduction: global science

Prior to the emergence of the Internet in 1990 science was organised and produced solely in national science systems. Though knowledge flowed freely across borders there were lags and internationa collaboration was modest: of papers published in 1970 and indexed in Web of Science just 1.9% have authors from more than one country (Olechnicka, Ploszaj, and Celinska-Janowicz 2019, 78). In Theory ofSociety, Luhmann (2012) notes that the decisive step towards world society was ‘the full discovery of the globe as a closed sphere of meaningful communication’ (85). This is what has happened in science. Electronically mediated communication has made possible the birth and rapid expansion of a global science system, not driven by technology as such but by human agents.

By ‘global’ is meant activities and relations that constitute a world space of activity and perception (a planetary ontology) and tend towards the integration of human afairs in this world space (Conrad 2016; Marginson 2011). By ‘system’ is meant simply a set of elements that form an interactive whole within defined boundaries. The global science system consists of instantaneously circulated knowledge in the global language of science, English, together with the collegial interactions of scientists in disciplinary and cross-disciplinary networks, supported by the underlying conditions: resources, infrastructures, equipment, rules, training. In 2018, 22.5% of all papers published in Scopus had co-authors from more than one country (NSB 2020, Table S5A-32). Some scholars of science define the global system as limited to these internationally co-authored papers (e.g. Wagner et al. 2015), but global knowledge with collective cognitive potential includes also papers authored solely within one country that receive global attention through publication and citation. In five countries in 2018, including the United States (US) and China, domestic only co-authored papers had higher average global citations than did internationally co-authored papers (Kwiek 2020, 16).

Wagner, Whetsell, and Leydesdorf (2017) establish empirically ‘that the global network has a culture, pathways, and norms of communication specific to its structure, and diverging from national, regional, or disciplinary norms’ (1646). The point is that the global system is distinct and diferent, not just existing separately from national science systems but also orthogonal to them. Unlike the diferent national science systems, the global science system is not normatively integrated from a single centre, the nation-state. It is grounded in the autonomous self-managed association of scientists themselves (Royal Society 2011, 6), regulated by autonomous professional conventions and standards (King 2011) and by global publishing. It is the pure self-referencing form of science specified by Leydesdorf:

Scientists have had a particular need for functional differentiation, since they need room for provisiona interpretations or hypotheses that they may wish to change with hindsight. In the longer run, the sciences can allow for normative control only over the conditions of the communication (e.g. resource allocations), but not over the substantive and reflexive contents of these communications. Thus, the diferentiation from normative integration has been a functional requirement for the further development of natural philosophy, that is, the new sciences. This crucial conflict was fought in Western Europe between the appearance of Galileo’s Dialogo in 1632 and the publication of Newton’s Principia in 1687. From that time onwards, functional diferen tiation has been further institutionalized in the social system. (Leydesdorf 2007, 382)

How then does the recently emerged global science system relate to the continuing national science systems, and multilateral agencies in Europe, where science is largely resourced, housed and employed? After all, governments are the main source of resources for basic science and expect it to fulfil policy objectives, as is discussed further below. National investment and organising of science have grown markedly, along with the global system. Research organisations such as universities and institutes, themselves constituted nationally, also administer and resource science, and want to provide discipline-based scientists with strategic direction. In most countries, the infrastructure of science has been partly built on the back of massified higher education systems (Cantwell, Marginson, and Smolentseva 2018), dominated by selective research-intensive universities, in which the Humboldtian teaching-research nexus is the vehicle for a wholesale transfer from teaching funding to research. And what of commercial publishing companies and industry users of research, some of which operate nationally and others cross-nationally, who also afect scientific activity?

The answer is that all these activities overlap, with a mix of synergies and tensions between the various domains. Science is produced in a complex eco-system in which scientists connect to a range of funders and networks in diferent scales. Scientists who are globally active often figure in national science systems and university centres. Science is not either global or national/local. It is both at the same time. Wagner, Park and Leydesdorf refer to ‘a process of co-evolution between the nationa institutional structure and the global network’ (11). However, within the complex eco-system of science, the relatively new element, which the fast-growing global system of relations and activities, stands out. Research on science identifies the self-perceived autonomy of global science and the afective power of that autonomy. It is partial, like other forms of organisational autonomy, but substantial, especially for established scientists in well-resourced settings. The global network not only provides scientists with most of their codified knowledge, the sea in which they swim but also ofers a robust collective identity and a shelter from direct interference.

Many scientists wear two diferent hats and have two sets of loyalties, to cross-border disciplinebased networks and to national and institutional authorities. The first allegiance can be stronger (Adams 2013), but for the most part, scientists are able to manage and reconcile their multiple lines of accountability. For their part, while governments, university executives, companies and publishers want to turn science to their advantage, they cannot trump scientists’ judgment of science. Where governments or university managers second guess the cognitive judgments of disciplinary practitioners, science goes less well. Ironically, global referencing by scientists is encouraged by those same national governments, for whom ‘internationalisation’ promises access to the cutting edge of technological innovation. Global links are also routinely supported by universities, for whom global science is a potentially limitless source of status, talent and resources. Star researchers and research universities symbiotically maximise each other’s prestige (Kwiek 2020, 4; Melguizo and Strober 2007). Yet collegial links in science are more bottom up than top down. One US survey by Finkelstein, Walker, and Chen (2013) found that in institutions in which faculty-led internationalisation activities the odds of collaborating with international colleagues in the research were nearly twice as great as in cases in which international activities were administratively led. The autonomy of individual scientists serves the autonomy of the global system, and also vice versa.

A large literature investigates or reflects on motivations for cross-border association between scientists (Chen, Zhang, and Fu 2019). These include what can be called cognitive accumulation, grounded in knowledge formation: not just curiosity but the desire to make a distinctive contribution by working at the site of possible breakthroughs; and also intellectual afinities with others, and the value of diverse perspectives, complementarity and division of labour in research teams (Winkler et al. 2015). ‘Researchers who collaborate … do not just add their individual expertise for a joint output but also exchange information and learn from each other’ (Graf and Kalthaus 2018, 1199). Collaboration is especially facilitated by prior cognitive agreement (Frenken, Hardeman, and Hoekman 2009, 230) but can also ofer cognitive strengthening, cross-disciplinary fertilisation and new directions. The quality or ‘excellence’ of a partner research group is motivating, as are the availability and communicability of scarce resources such as knowhow, research samples and data. Some studies focus on personal chemistry, psychic benefits and shared social afinities (e.g. Melin 2000).

Another set of explanations has developed around the sociological notion of preferential attachment, whereby junior or subaltern agents attach themselves to highly cited scientists to secure recognition and career advantages. Here global science is imagined as a field of positional competition in which decisions are driven by self-advancement. In contrast with cognitively based explanations that are domain-specific, the apparent outcomes of preferential attachment – papers, citation recognition, career achievements – can be understood in generic terms. Wagner, Whetsell, and Leydesdorf (2017) demonstrate across six diferent disciplines parallel empirical patterns of coauthorship, that they attribute to preferential attachment, though the study by Cabanac, Hubert, and Milard (2015) finds preferential attachment more plausible at the beginning of careers than later. Katz and Ronda-Pupo (2019) question whether the preferential attachment is ‘the active mechanism’ in observed patterns (1047). Though both cognitive and positional goals are met also in local collaboration, especially cognitive goals, the global ofers a wide field of ideas and partners.

Research also focuses on the nexus between collaboration and performance. All else being equal international co-authorship is associated with greater citation rates (e.g. Cimini, Zaccaria, and Gabrielli 2016; Khor and Yu 2016). Again, causality is debatable. The citation has multiple meanings, variously signifying agreement, cognitive debt, legitimation, field identity, status building, and/or mutual support (Patelli et al. 2017, 1230; Tahamtan and Bornmann 2019, 1635). This renders them unsuitable as a universal indicator of quality. That in turn undermines the assumed link between internationalisation and quality (Cimini, Zaccaria, and Gabrielli 2016, 201). However, the primary driver of the citation fetish – and the pathologies it generates, from the multiplication of citations and authors, and performative publishing, to various forms of gaming, and the proscription of unorthodox studies – is not epistemic quality, which is field-specific and impenetrable to nonspecialists, but academic status, which is universal. Global scientific networking is an unambiguous builder of individual status and careers.

To say that the global system is primarily bottom up, with scope for the agency of scientists, modified by structural influences, does not explain it in terms of social relations. The underlying concern of this paper is with such explanation. It approaches this problem by critically reviewing current explanations of global science. It begins by outlining the main data-based tendencies in global science; and then presents and reviews four contrasting narratives of global science prominent in the scholarly literature and/or policy-making and public discussion: global science as a collaborative network, as an arms race between national governments, as a market of ‘World-Class Universities’, and as a world systems hierarchy controlled by a Euro-American global centre. Each of these four narratives embodies a mode of agency, coupled with a spatial lens, in a distinctive set of values.

Though often more than one narrative is used, they are only partly congruent. Arguably, none of these narratives is consistent with the empirical evidence on global science nor suficient to furnish a cogent explanation of it. The conclusion suggests another approach.

Data, explanations and narratives

Any explanation of global science needs to reckon with the data on systemic tendencies, and with the insights generated in ongoing research and scholarship on global science.

Inclusions and exclusions

The empirical resources for explaining science include Web of Science (Wos) and Scopus (for a comparison see Waltman 2016); secondary data from the bibliometric collections, including the US National Science Board (NSB 2020) and the Leiden University (2020) ranking; and Organisation for Economic Cooperation and Development (OECD 2020) data. There is also a burgeoning literature in scientometric papers that rests on the bibliometric collections, and does not look beyond them. Reviews of scientometrics include Mingers and Leydesdorf (2015), Patelli et al. (2017). For Mingers and Leydesdorf (2015) scientometrics is ‘the study of the quantitative aspects of science and technology seen as a process of communication’ (1). It tracks data from ‘a positivist perspective’ on publications, web usage, authorship and citation. Scientometrics has achieved ‘major’ importance in ‘the academic community’ because of ‘the drive of governments and oficial bodies to monitor, record and evaluate research performance’ (2). Studies in this genre include social network analyses (Scott 2017) of cross-border science, in all disciplines or selected disciplines, or between cities or individual universities; comparisons of national scientific output, citation quality or co-authored papers in which each nation is treated as a meta-node in the network, or as a separated data silo; studies of science within single nations; and studies comparing scientific output in the global, regional, national and local scales (Marginson 2021. For an overview of diferent spatial takes on science to that point see Frenken, Hardeman, and Hoekman (2009).

The global science data repository by no means includes all of science and still less the sum of knowledge. Much scientific output is circulated in pre-print and unreviewed forms. WoS and Scopus lag behind the explosion of online grey matter online, which does not always find a home in bibliometrics. Other matter is more consciously excluded. First, many English language social science journals and nearly all scholarship in the humanities are outside WoS and Scopus. Books, which carry a large part of knowledge in these disciplines, are almost wholly excluded. Second, and most importantly, the overwhelming majority of papers in languages other than English are outside the two global bibliometric collections. With only that knowledge made clearly visible, the bibliometric industry, and the scientometric studies that feeds of it, grossly exaggerate the extent to which intellectual work is concentrated in Anglophone countries and Western Europe. Other nations, scholars and scientists are positioned as in deficit, regardless of the work they produce (Beigel 2014, 620). Endogenous (indigenous) knowledge everywhere is excluded (Connell 2014; Mbembe 2016; Nyamnjoh 2019; Posholi 2020).

In his theorisation of relations of power Lukes (2005) focuses on the ‘mobilisation of bias’ (20–21) and control over agendas, ‘the power to decide what is decided’ (25). Global science is Anglo-European centric and partly closed, structured by an inclusion/exclusion binary (Santos 2007) operating at two levels: first, the selection of global knowledge, and second, the ordering of value inside it. Scientists from the leading countries, especially the US, set epistemological agendas, determine standards and produce nearly all highly cited papers, as defined by citation counts within the same skewed system. The Anglo-European hegemony in knowledge is consistent with the 1990s Anglo-American globalisation of trade and geo-politics. Yet that era is fading, with the rise of China, East Asia and a range of middle powers, and the momentum for decolonisation (Macaes 2018; Pieterse 2018). This paper is largely focused on the inner dynamics of global science but the outer dynamics, hegemony and the knowledge left outside the global system, will return.

Transparent empirical tendencies

There are four transparent empirical tendencies in global science: the rapid expansion in papers; the spread of scientific capacity and output to a growing number of countries and cities; the expansion of networked flows of knowledge as evidenced by citations and growth in the number and proportion of papers authored from two or more countries; and the changing geo-politics of science, its multi-polar evolution beyond the Euro-American world

Between 2000 and 2018, while world GDP grew by 3.5% per annum (World Bank 2020), papers in Scopus grew by 4.9% per annum (NSB 2020, Table S5A-2), driven by increases in the number of journals, in journals included in Scopus, and in papers per journal. As Figure 1 shows the number of internationally collaborative and nation-only collaborative papers grew more rapidly than papers from one institution. The pattern of growth was common to almost every national science system and was often resourced by an increase in the GDP share allocated to research in universities and specialist institutes (OECD 2020) where most published science is produced. The Leiden University (2020) ranking shows that between 2006–09 and 2015–18, the number of universities with at least 5000 papers in Web in Science rose from 131 to 233, and in China alone from 10 to 54.

At the same time, national capacity in research and doctoral education has spread from the traditional science nations in the English-speaking world, Europe, Japan and Russia to many middle-income and poorer countries. Whereas in 2000 scientists from 30 nations, eight outside Anglo-Europe, authored over 5000 Scopus papers a year, by 2018 there were 52 countries with 24 outside Anglo-Europe (NSB 2020, Table S5A-2). As Table 1 shows, in the thirty years after 1987 the number of countries authoring half of all recognised papers rose from three to six and the countries with 90% of papers rose from 20 to 32. In most countries, the share of papers produced outside the leading cities has increased (Grossetti et al. 2014). The diversification of capacity in science is of a piece with the global radiation of modernisation via urbanisation, industrialisation, education, communications, publishing and governance (Powell, Baker, and Fernandez 2017; Packalen 2019).

In 2018, 22.5% of all Scopus papers were co-authored at the international level with 44.4% coauthored nationally only. The international share has grown slightly faster (NSB 2020, Table S5A-32). In 2016 it varied by discipline, from 54.0% in astronomy and astrophysics papers in Scopus to 15.4% in social science papers (NSB 2020, 122). The proportion of papers internationally co-authored was relatively high in Europe (e.g. UK 61.7%, Germany 53.2 %), where cross-country collaboration is fostered by EU funding schemes, and in many small systems (e.g. Singapore 71.8 %), including those emerging countries that use internationalisation as the primary means of building capacity (Olechnicka, Ploszaj, and Celinska-Janowicz 2019). International co-authorship is lower in certain larger emerging systems that emphasise endogenous capacity (e.g. Iran 24.4 %, China 21.7 %, India 17.8 %). In the US, in 2018, 39.2 % of papers involved international collaboration (NSB 2020, Table S5A-32). This is a high rate given that in the US there are many potential domestic partners and confirms the statement by Olechnicka, Ploszaj, and Celinska-Janowicz (2019) that ‘the contemporary global scientific network is woven around the US’ (92). There are intensive cross-border collaborations at Harvard, with 52.0 % of papers in 2015–18, and other leading US universities (Leiden University 2020). There are also unmistakeable signs of multi-polarity. Since 2016 China has produced more annual papers than the US and India recently passed UK and Germany in total paper numbers to move to third in the world (NSB 2020). High citation science is increasing more rapidly in China and Singapore than elsewhere (Leiden University 2020).

Interpretive resources and narratives

However, the global science system is more than a field of data. As critical realism argues (Sayer 2000) it is a field of historically grounded social relations, which are only partly structured and are open to contextual variation, contingency and the ever-living potentials of agency. While data regularities in science are significant, they are always bounded and provisional. Social relations are only partly captured by empirical research. Some aspects are hidden from direct observation. There is an irreducible need for interpretation. Yet interpretive frameworks and theorisations are also always provisional. Theories are not prescriptions of the possible, they are tools for enhancing understanding that should be chosen, matched, mixed and discarded as appropriate. This suggests the needs for methodological and conceptual pluralism (Pieterse 2018, 179–195), and for the continuous testing of interpretations and observations against each other.

It must be said that in scientometrics, and related policy-oriented discussion of science, few theories as such are used. The most cited constructions are those of world-systems theory, with its centre-periphery hierarchy between nations (Wallerstein 1974, 2006), to be discussed; and the sociology of preferential attachment. These ideas are cited more often than they are used systematically. In the absence of a developed culture of theorisation, interpretation in the largely positivist field of global science studies mostly consists of apparently plausible ‘common sense’ speculations on data. Common sense in scholarship, as in daily life, is typically patterned by narratives and myths (Henriques et al. 1984). The following four narratives are used to fill the gap in understandings of globa science:

  1. Global science as an open and expanding pan-national network, as in the social network-based analyses often used in studies of science;

  2. Global science as an arms race in innovation between competing nations, the interpretation most favoured by policy makers and in public discussion

  3. Global science as a world market of competing institutions (‘World-Class Universities’), as in university rankings, often the focus of leaders’ strategies;

  4. Global science as a centre-periphery hierarchy, derived world systems theory that positions Euro-American countries as dominant, also used in studies of science.

These four narratives are instantly familiar. They are commonly used in either popular and policy usage (narratives 2 and 3) and/or the scholarly literature (1, 2 and 4). Further, each of the narratives gains purchase from its correspondence to a spatially-defined agent position in science, as discussed below in Table 2. The four narratives are never rigorously tested. Essentially they are reifications, easily comprehended caricatures of a complex reality, but this does not rob them of efectiveness, either as explanations or in shaping the worlds of policy and politics, science and higher education. Each narrative fosters difering goals and behaviours in agents, nudging the world of science closer to the world imagined in the narrative. The paper now reviews each narrative in the light of scholarship and empirical data. The underlying question is: ‘How explanatory of global science is this narrative?

Narrative 1: pan-national network of scientists

The material foundations of the network narrative lie in the social logic of the network form in information systems. Castells explains this in The Network Society (2000). As the network grows each successive agent (‘node’) is added at negligible cost. It adds value to the existing nodes by increasing the potentially fruitful connections and cheapening the average unit cost of each connection across the network. When operating freely any node can forge connections with any other node and gains by doing so. Networks continually call new agents and connections (‘links’) into being, expanding naturally towards complete inclusion of every possible node, while intensifying the connections between existing nodes. As the network grows the costs of exclusion increase faster than the benefits of inclusion (Castells 2009, 42). Networks are prima facie disposed to expansion, openness, inclusion, horizontality, bottom-up agency and a binary inclusion/exclusion dynamic. Science is an information system, and once the conditions for synchronous electronic communication were established, global science expanded in the manner of a Castellian network. ‘Patterns in international collaboration in science can be considered as network efects, since there is no political institution mediating relationships at that level’ aside from the European Commission (Leydesdorf and Wagner 2008, 317). In fact national governments assisted the trajectory: few blocked the Internet and many built the capacity of national science so as to source the global science pool. There was no brake to the process of global growth.

The network idea lends itself to imaginings of science as global, collegial, open, ever growing and more flat than the other narratives suggest. Some scientometric analyses wholly dispense with national categories when investigating aggregate science or connections in specific fields or topics (e.g. Cabanac, Hubert, and Milard 2015; Larregue, Larivière, and Mongeon 2020). Citations indicate that successive papers by Wagner, Leydesdorf and colleagues have influenced network studies, though these authors veer between conceptions of global science as one system (e.g. Wagner and Leydesdorf 2005), and studies that combine the single global network with its zerosum division on the basis of author nation, as if nations are super-nodes of the network (e.g. Wagner, Whetsell, and Mukherjee 2019). In reviewing the evolution of global science Wagner et al. (2015) note the ‘enhanced capacity in diferent parts of the world’ (7) and wider distribution of output and connections. Countries in the ‘dense centre’ numbered 35 in 1990, 64 in 2005 and 114 in 2011. Network density has increased sharply but ‘fewer nodes dominate the network, or, in other words, power is more difused throughout the network in 2011’ than in 1990. ‘The drop in betweenness measures suggests that many nodes operate efectively in the network, and that influence is not accruing to centralised nodes’. The network seems to encourage new participants and enable free movement inside. Strong players do not gate keep: ‘new entrants are not clustering around the scientific “leaders”’ (6). Graf and Kalthaus’s (2018) study of photovoltaics research reaches similar conclusions. Likewise, Choi (2012) finds that collaborative links between scientists in nations on the ‘periphery’ have grown faster than centre-periphery links and centre-centre links (39).

Network-based approaches capture the rapid expansion and spreading of global science – though as Cabanac, Hubert, and Milard (2015) and Citron and Way (2018, 187) show, network diagrams can overstate connectedness, as many past linkages have lapsed. Network models explain why global co-authorship is expanding in almost every country while national co-authorship exhibits more mixed patterns. NSB (2020) data for the 42 leading science countries (Table S5A32) show that between 2006 and 2018 the least growth in the number of internationally co-authored papers was the 42 % in Japan. At the same time, in 23 of 42 countries, nationally co-authored papers increased more slowly than 42 % and in five countries they declined. In collaboration within the nation, the logic of Castellian expansion is mediated by nation-state funding, policies, infrastructure and incentives, and limited by national boundaries.

However, where the network imaginary falters is in identifying vertical as distinct from horizontal relations – external gaps and exclusions, where its methods have little to ofer, and inequalities inside the network.

Castells (2001) notes that the network form ‘allows metropolitan concentration and global networking to proceed simultaneously’ (225). Other studies note this double efect of extension and concentration (e.g. Bergé, Scherngell, and Wanzenböck 2017; Graf and Kalthaus 2018, 12). Network-based studies do not fully explore this. In network diagrams intersecting lines at the major nodes present as black boxes, or rather, black spheres. The quantity of connections does not capture geo-political and cultural meanings. Links are not necessarily equivalent, but network analysis does not distinguish between links embodying expressive and innovative power, links based in reciprocal relations, and links signifying mimetics and dependence. Chinchilla-Rodríguez et al. (2018) struggle to investigate global inequality and asymmetric agency using network analysis. They equate national agency in turn with lead authorship in collaborative papers (power of initiative) and low rates of international collaboration (independence), but these indicators are contrary and ambiguous. As they state, subaltern science is subordinated by ‘the selection of research topics, agenda, or criteria behind publication and evaluation’ (1486), yet social network analysis cannot pinpoint the hegemonic role of English in Anglo-American science. As Conrad (2016) remarks: ‘If hierarchy is neglected, preoccupation with connections may blur an accurate understanding (70) … the network is embedded in structural inequalities, but the impression arises that it operates in a vacuum’ (127).

Narrative 2: arms race in innovation between nations

In the nation-centred narrative, science is a weapon in the endless multilateral competition between states. The material foundations of the narrative are the political economy of nations and the resources, infrastructures, agencies, policies and rules they bring to bear in science. Nations do not delegate their strategies to the voluntary actions of global scientists. They try to shape priorities, incentivise innovation and tap global science to their advantage. Their objective is not science for its own sake but national security, military strength, geo-political weight, income-earning intellectua property, industry innovation, prosperity and employment; though at times they find themselves using the output of basic science (and even the number of ‘World-Class Universities’) as a proxy for these other goals. As noted, they find it hard to direct thinking from a distance, and to render the nation more competitive they depend on those same global scientists. This is the ambiguity of ‘national science’.

Nevertheless, nations matter. Government determine broad priorities in the global science on national soil, and a significant body of national science, especially applied and commercial work, does not figure in global paper counts but articulates the passage of global science into the industry, civil society and public order. National funding is correlated to total output and citation recognition in global science, as indicated in OECD (2020) data, Chinchilla-Rodríguez et al. (2019) and others. Large national system size brings a greater number of global collaborative possibilities close to hand. The nation is especially potent in regulating people movement. Policy on visits, immigration, security blocks and eligibility rules for funding can all afect output, as shown in both the collaboration and tension between science in the US and China (Lee and Haupt 2020).

‘The sciences develop internationally, but the funding is mainly national’ (Bornmann, Adams, and Leydesdorf 2018, 931). Because nations fund science they also shape the study of performance in science. Much of scientometrics consists of studies in which the nation is the primary category and global network data an arena for the play of national interest. Within that literature, the main component is cross-national comparisons of performance (for a review see Abramo and D’Angelo 2020). The genre begins with seminal papers by May (1997) and King (2004), in which nations are stand-alone silos, and data on joint papers prepared within networks are arbitrarily split and assigned to one nation or another. The focus is alternately on pure scientific performance as measured by output, citation and collaboration indicators, or economic eficiency: performance relative to resources (e.g. Cimini, Zaccaria, and Gabrielli 2016). May (1997) notes two problems of method: ‘comparisons are to a degree confounded because a large and growing fraction of scientific work involves international collaboration’; and ‘there is an English language bias in the ISI database, both in the journals included and in patterns of citation’ (795). Neither problem is solved in later studies, although some repeat May’s two points (e.g. Cimini, Gabrielli, and Labini 2014, 8). Social network analyses define nations as meta-nodes and map connectivity and centrality between them (e.g. Choi 2012). Other papers extract single nation data from bibliometric collections (e.g. Kwiek 2018 on Poland; Avanesova and Shamliyan 2018 on Russia; Basu et al. 2018 on China).

To what extent is the part of global science that is captured in national performance measures, also national science? Like May (1997) some scholars who conduct national comparisons are uneasy with the narrative, including the category ‘nation’. Adams and Gurney (2018) note that as scientists in each country become ‘inextricably intertwined with other research economies, it wil become increasingly dificult for any comparative analysis of a country’s research performance to identify a separate national component’ (3). Many researchers have plural country afiliations. Bornmann, Adams, and Leydesdorf (2018) state that ‘it is increasingly dificult in bibliometric analysis to separate clear country efects’ (942) though ‘tensions and trade-ofs between international and national perspectives … difer among disciplines’: the nation is more of a factor in sociology than in physics (931–932). Ambivalence in the literature reflects the ambiguity in the real world. National identity has two possible meanings. Are scientists agents of the nation-state pursuing national interests within a multilateral global order, or are they nationally-based persons driven by bottom-up disciplinary relations in autonomous global science? The decisive question, albeit one hard to answer, is the extent to which the nation-state controls ‘its’ part of global science as an instrument of will. Though the answer varies by country, it likely boils down to ‘not much’. Epistemology is an inner art. Scientists might be proud to serve the nation, and the nation provides necessary coonditions for science, but how often is the nation in the room when new ideas come? Open knowledge flows and protocols allow lead scientists to step outside the framing of science as a primarily national enterprise, except in military-security matters; though junior scientists and scientists in emerging countries (Muriithi et al. 2018) have less freedom.

A further problem for the arms race narrative is that studies of the link between investment in science, scientific performance and innovation performance are inconclusive. There are less such studies than might be expected. Patelli et al. (2017) and Gazni and Ghaseminik (2019) use patents as the proxy for innovation, while Frenken, Heimeriks, and Hoekman (2017) use Leiden ranking data on university-industry co-publication. These indicators are at some distance from product markets. The science-to-innovation chain is long and difuse, and there is a lack of causal alignment between its two halves: because science enters a global pool, many innovations by national industry are sourced in foreign science, while national science might be first exploited by foreign rather than the national capital. In scientometrics is easier to stick to bibliometrics and studying the comparative efectiveness of national science as a quasi-economy in itself (Abramo, D’Angelo, and Carloni 2019) but this begs the question about economic innovation. A more directly challenging point is made by Klavans, Boyack, and Hernandez Montoya (2017). Most university science is decoupled from industry innovation. It is ‘altruistic’ in purpose, for example, medical research, much of life science and ecological science, and social science.

As Anderson (2006) famously stated, nations are ‘imagined communities’. They work strenuously and continually to sustain themselves. Part of that work is ideological. The narrative about nationa competition shapes the common sense about science more than do the other narratives, yet it struggles for traction. National factors like language, culture and resources always matter in global science but their efects are refracted through the global system. National governments afect that system, yes, but episodically and from outside it. Nations do not control collegial networks in science.

Framing science as a multilateral zero-sum competition, as a mosaic of separated nationa systems, perpetuates the fiction that nations order their own destinies and everything else. It blocks from view the global science system and the collaboration intrinsic to its expansion. Inquiries into specific cognitive knowledge flows – disciplines, cross-disciplinary links, topics – are a more sensitive use of network analysis than top-down studies of aggregated nation-nodes grappling with each other, like great clunking beasts at the waterhole. Melin (2000) and Jeong, Choi, and Kim (2011) indicate that collegial cooperation across borders predated the accelerated national policies designed to encourage it. While national competition drives investment in science worldwide, and states have played a great role in most of the fastest-growing systems, national factors alone cannot explain the upward shift in the growth of science after 1990; nor its open, unbordered and collaborative form; nor the marked fecundity of global networking in science when compared to nationa networks.

Narrative 3: Global market of ‘World-Class Universities

The material foundations of science as a global market of ‘World-Class Universities’ (WCUs) (Salmi 2009) are science-oriented higher education institutions; their perpetual competition for prestige, talent, and in some countries, international students; the corporate freedoms of many universities when outside their national borders; and the primary role of scientific research in securing university visibility and status (Docampo and Cram 2017, 740). However, what has given the narrative its form is the key role of research in the indicators used to comprise the three prominent global university rankings (ARWU 2020; QS 2020; THE 2020; for comparisons see Marginson 2014; Moed 2017). The indicators used by the global rankings are consistent with measurable characteristics of Anglo-American science universities: paper volume, high citations, Nobel Prizes, research income, university reputation in surveys, the proportion of personnel who are international: ‘a highly international university … demonstrates an ability to attract faculty and students from across the world, which in turn suggests that it possess a strong international brand’ (QS 2020). Rankings standardise the chosen indicators into a single number for each university, calibrating a highly visible and coherent global order.

This narrative imagines a world of stand-alone context-free universities, responsible for their own fate, whose performance is separated from governments, resources and collegial links – as if universities are homogenous business firms contesting a level playing field. In reality, they are highly contextualised, nested in states and with varying autonomy, and their performance rests not solely on their corporate leadership but on the stability of government support, their language of use, their mostly socially selective character and their resources for bottom-up science, and faculty activity independent of institutional strategy. Yet rankings are normatively compelling. The Times Higher Education states it is ‘trusted worldwide by students, teachers, governments and industry experts’ (THE 2020). Perhaps ‘trust’ is not the right word, but as Hazelkorn (2015) shows, the three leading rankings are widely used in decisions by students, faculty, governments when allocating scholarships and selecting skilled immigrants, and private investors and donors; and they are a primary performance indicator for university leaders and governing bodies. Striving for higher rankings, universities use monetary and career incentives to encourage research cooperation and papers in global English.

Given the potency of rankings, and the scope to search bibliometric data on the basis of the institution, this narrative has a surprisingly modest impact in scientometrics. Few papers use the institution as the main unit of analysis. Prathap (2017) and Piro (2019) use institutional data to study national not institutional outcomes, finding that high-performance systems are characterised by relatively high stratification, as in the US and UK, and China and Singapore which pursue WCU policies. However, Docampo and Cram (2017) find that the positive correlation holds in only some systems, and Muriithi et al. (2018) state that in Kenya competition retards science by inhibiting university cooperation. Studies also correlate the size of the university and performance (Moed 2017). Benito, Gil, and Romera (2019) correlate institutional funding and performance. In contrast, Bornmann, Mutz, and Daniel (2013) conclude that most of the statistical variation between universities in the incidence of high citation papers is due to country location, not the university, with the remaining variation is largely driven by specific disciplines not institution qua institution. For Kwiek (2018) institutional factors have ‘zero impact … Intrinsic motivations rather than institutional incentive structures stimulate research productivity’ (448). Both Bornmann, Mutz, and Daniel (2013) and Frenken, Heimeriks, and Hoekman (2017) critique the ‘questionable implicit assumption of a single global system’ in the institutional ranking (860).

The narrative of science as a global competition of WCUs became popularised less to explain science than to order universities as a neoliberal global student market (QS, Times Higher rankings) or to benchmark their global competitive position (ARWU). Subsequently, it was retrofitted to institutional science policy and management. Nevertheless, this grand parade of status, reworked in the instantly familiar form of a football premiership, makes its own story. As with the national arms race narrative, the narrative of the global university market, and the focus on the institution as a unit of analysis, conceal the work of collegial drivers and networks in science (Winkler et al. 2015, 139). Again, the narrative is unable to fully explain the processes of growth and diversification in science. Like nations, universities provide favourable conditions for growth, but WCU policy is more a symptom than a cause in science. Knowledge-based relations are more motivating than is institutional location. Scientists are much more likely to retain their field and change university than vice versa.

Narrative 4: centre-periphery hierarchy of nations

Science is a site of relations of power. It is moulded by the geo-political and economic distribution of activity, resources and status between countries, cities and universities; it is afected by hierarchies of disciplines; and in turn, it constitutes hierarchy and inequality of agents, sites and knowledge. While for Wagner and colleagues global science is a vast open quasi-market of scientists pursuing selfinterested partnerships, King (2011) tempers this utopia with a realist’s appreciation of hierarchy and oligopoly. Science is ‘characterised by networks of enclosure’ as well as openness, and by ‘network power’ (360). Language requirements, journal hierarchies, citation counts and rankings entrench inequalities. Chinchilla-Rodríguez et al. (2019) argue, with only moderate exaggeration, that ‘despite increasing global participation in science, most international collaborations are asymmetrical … The research system remains structured around a few dominant nations’ (1). Global inequality is the starting point for the fourth narrative, from world-systems theory, in which global science is a hierarchy structured by centre-periphery logic. Centre-periphery and the similar North–South binary are widely referenced in scientometrics (e.g. Chinchilla-Rodríguez et al. 2018; Choi 2012; Helibron 2014; Olechnicka, Ploszaj, and Celinska-Janowicz 2019; Schott 1998).

In Wallerstein’s (1974, 2006) world systems theory applied to science, the countries of the centre in the US, UK and parts of Western Europe reproduce their dominance by maintaining the permanent subordination of science on the periphery. Between the centre and the periphery is an intermediate zone of countries in the ‘semi-periphery’. Wallerstein (1974) argues that the position of individual countries can be understood only in terms of the ‘totality’ of worldwide capitalist economic relations (387). As he puts it: ‘There is no such thing as ‘national development’’ (390). There is limited ‘surplus’ at the world level and for one country to rise another must decline (Wallerstein 1976, 466). Wallerstein repeatedly states that countries can only rarely move out of the periphery or the semi-periphery. He is a critic of Eurocentrism, but sees it as inevitable unless or until capitalism is abolished.

Hence for advocates of Wallerstein’s model in science, global relations in science are determined by the fixed structure of the political economy (Olechnicka, Ploszaj, and Celinska-Janowicz 2019, 102 and 105). Knowledge and science, including collegial relations between scientists, have negligible social autonomy in their own right. In an early critique Smith (1979) argues that world-systems theory underplays the potential of individual states on the ‘periphery’ to lift the trajectory of nationa development. This has proven prophetic.

Centre-periphery ofers a model of global hierarchy in science that at first sight looks consistent with the binary efects of inclusion/exclusion and Anglo-American hegemony. However, worldsystems theory was closer to observable realities in the 1970s, before the growth of world scientific output, its diversification and the flourishing of international collaboration including ‘periphery to periphery’ networking. The last two decades of global science are inexplicable in world systems terms. It has become evident that a significant part of the scientific ‘periphery’ is not confined by permanent under-development. Figure 2 lists the 26 countries where the 2000–2018 annual rate of growth in science papers exceeded the world average of 4.94 %, and scientists authored more than 5000 papers in 2018. In ten of those countries per capita PPP incomes were below the world country average.

The collapse of the centre-periphery model is confirmed by the breakthrough of China to a leading global role, without networking intensively across the Euro-American ‘centre’, and the rise of autonomous systems in India, Iran, South Korea and Brazil where national collaboration has often been more generative than global collaboration. China outperforms the US in high citation science in mathematics, and is closing the gap in computing, engineering and chemistry, though not in biomedicine and life science (NSB 2020, Tables S5A-2 and S5A-36 to S5A-49). The Leiden University (2020) ranking finds that in 2015–18 Tsinghua University passed MIT, Stanford, Harvard and Berkeley in its number of papers in physical sciences and engineering in the top 5 % by citation – and in mathematics and computer science the leading seven universities were all in China. As Pieterse (2018) notes, the global North–South polarity is being supplemented in the multi-polar setting by East-South, East–West and China–India axes. However, China’s success is success in Western not Chinese science. The rising systems have yet to modify the Anglo-American domination of professional language, conventions, standards and, often, topics. Science is not a neo-imperial hierarchy enforced by nation-state power as Wallerstein suggests. It is more like a Gramscian hegemony that operates at the level of cultural consent. This may take longer to shift.

Scale and agency in the four narratives

The four narratives of global science gain purchase because they are authentic for agents. Table 2 matches each narrative to diferent agent positions. As column one shows, global science is practised by three diferent kinds of agents, operating in the individual, institutional and national scales respectively and joined relationally to their fellow agents in established modes of interaction: (a) collegial scientists, (b) universities and research institutes, and (c) nation-states. The four narratives condition the actions expected of scientists, universities and governments. However, agents have a choice of ways of seeing which again involves scale: they can perceive global science either through a global lens, which allows all of science to come fully into view, or a national lens. Scientometric papers are split between the two perspectives, though not equally. The global lens fits better with free-flowing knowledge from anywhere, and because it is not pre-structured by a normative centre, in column 2 the diferent agents pursue their own projects and agendas (Marginson

2008). However, nation-state politics dominate thought. In daily life and in scientometrics the default is ‘methodological nationalism’ (Shahjahan and Kezar 2013) not global openness.

The four narratives are not the only possible narratives that could be aligned to each agent/lens position, but arguably, they have become dominant, to an extent that might be surprising. Note that the three global lens narratives in the middle column embody contrasting values not always compatible with each other. Narrative 1 is science centred, positive sum and, though scientists compete for status and position, the network narrative foregrounds their collaboration. In narrative 3, the university is an end in itself: this is the neo-liberal vision of institutions in zero-sum competition for status and resources, though like business firms often in temporary alliances with each other, and operating globally outside government, as in Anglo-American corporate universities. Narrative 4 is a birds-eye global view of states locked into fixed zero-sum relations. In the final column, through the solely national lens, only narrative 2 is in play and values are constant. As in narrative 4 nations are seen as zero-sum. At all three scales of agency in column 3 nations compete for advantage via strategic innovations in industry and pursue autarkic national security. Individuals and institutions are judged in terms of their contributions to national interests.

In the practical world, no single narrative is always supreme, and the same agents may work with either a global or a national lens, swapping between columns 2 and 3. Again, this has implications in difering values and behaviours. As Conrad (2016) remarks, ‘the choice of scale always has normative implications’ (156). Scientists move between their collegial hat and their national hat, though some wear both together. Universities oscillate between a global public good mission in research and their role in the national knowledge economy. After interviewing in elite universities in the US and UK Friedman (2018) concludes that the pragmatic commitment of university leaders to ‘everyday nationalism’ was more fundamental than their globalist talk. However, many individual scientists are primarily loyal to their associations in disciplinary networks within the global system (Adams and Gurney 2018). National governments never stop being national governments. Yet in column two the nation is determined by the ‘totality’ (Wallerstein 1974), with little scope for ‘peripheral states to move up; whereas in the column three narrative, the nation determines its own destiny, wholly and autonomously, the ‘internalist’ fallacy noted by Conrad (2016, 88). It seems that in the imagined community of the nation, agency is all or nothing. This stark ideological polarity with no middle ground exposes the reification in narratives 2 and 4.

Conclusions

Few human activities are more globally integrated than science. Castells (2009) argues that in a more global setting the challenge is ‘to conceptualise a new form of society … made up of specific configurations of global, national and local networks in a multidimensional space of social interaction’ (199). Global science is a partly autonomous system with distinctive relational characteristics that must be accounted for. Global science connects to agents at the national, institutional and collegial-local scales but is not subsumed in those scales. It is dificult to know whether the present system – Anglo-European centric, exclusive of much human knowledge, yet fecund and expanding rapidly – will continue its present trajectory. Greater plurality of knowledge is possible. China–US tensions in science and technology suggest another possibility: the forceful disruption of autonomous global collaboration by national agendas.

A fuller picture of science would explore its resourcing and infrastructure country by country, trace the growth and reproduction of the science professions, and investigate worldwide variations in the extent to which national and local science are globally engaged. It would also discuss in detail the patterns of local, regional and nation-bound research, hidden from bibliometrics and devalued by the focus on global citations and World-Class Universities, but a large part of total scientific activity and its applications. As with any study that gives attention to one part of the social fabric, one set of strands, the focus on the global science system exaggerates its stand-alone character. The system is connected at many points to political, economic and social agents; immersed in cultures, shaped in discourses, and never isolated from the rhythms of economy and ecology. But its autonomy is nevertheless important. It is a defining feature and a key part of the explanation.

Table 3 summarises the explanatory qualities of the four narratives of global science reviewed in this paper. A ‘Yes’ is recorded when a narrative has what appears to be a plausible explanation for the particular characteristic of the system in question, whether or not that explanation is agreed. In many areas, these narratives fail the test.

It is apparent that policy-makers, media and public discussion are especially poorly served by the two narratives in popular use: arms race of nations and WCU market. Neither narrative adequately explains the rapid growth and spread of science to many more countries, and the assumptions about intrinsic zero-sum competition for national strategic advantage and for best university status are undermined by the great growth of collegial collaboration. The centre-periphery model has been exploded by global system evolution and it is surprising that it still figures in the literature at all. The network narrative is the only one that acknowledges collegial communities as central to global science, and the only one that captures the developmental dynamics of the system. However, in contrast with the other three narratives, it has no explanation for inequality and hierarchy, a major weakness. Scholars of science mostly divide between the network narrative and the national arms race narrative, but there is unease about each, and in any case, this is an unsatisfactory either/or choice. It implies that to analyse science one must either focus on global autonomy and flat network forms, or on nations and hierarchy. Yet all these elements are in play.

Each narrative on its own constitutes a stereotype that conceals as much as it reveals. Social science and in particular, the field of global studies, can do better. The way forward in explaining global science is to acknowledge the (partial) autonomy of the global system and within that, its horizontal and vertical dimensions – to combine network theory with a theorisation of the hegemonic power that stratifies and excludes – while keeping open the potentials of multipolarity. Science has only a partial autonomy because it is impacted by the arbitrary interventions of governments, especially through funding, and by market forces; though up to this point, neither politics nor economics have permanently suborned the expanding collegial conversations. Like all information, science spreads like quicksilver in a connected world and its structures are porous to newcomers. However, its recognised global contents are gripped by a firm Anglo-American hegemony, and the fact must be faced that it is the professional science communities themselves that, together with the commercial bibliometric companies, exercise the homogenising role, locking out more diverse knowledge, agendas and agents. If linguistic plurality and reciprocal translation were normalised, and the bibliometric repositories began to open up to a fuller range of disciplines and forms of output, much would change in global science.
