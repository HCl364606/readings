CHAPTER 7

WORK-GAMES IN THE GIG-ECONOMY: A CASE STUDY OF UBER DRIVERS IN THE CITY OF MONTERREY, MEXICO

Mariana Manriquez

ABSTRACT

Uber, the virtual service that connects drivers to passenger, presents a novel form of work-organization in which managerial functions are transposed into a virtual platform. This ethnographic study documents how Uber drivers in the city of Monterrey, Mexico navigate and come to make sense of the Uber model of work. Employing the conceptual device of the work-game, this study argues that engagement in the game of “earning coins” coupled the interest of drivers in generating the most-possible income with the interest of management in maintaining a readily available labor pool. Reinforcing this coupling was Uber’s deployment of an entrepreneurial ideology of “being your own boss,” which was especially important given the company’s lack of a physical manage ment structure. However, as Uber takes advantage of the deindustrialization that has gripped Monterey, it attracts drivers exhibiting varied employmen trajectories. This in turn creates different modes of playing the work-game and thus generates sharply divergent subjective understandings of the work, whose nature this chapter explores.

Keywords: Gig-economy; labor process; service work; post-Fordism; Mexican labor market: Uber

INTRODUCTION

Emerging from the technocracy of Silicon Valley, Uber is the virtual service that connects driver to passenger, and it has generated several puzzles among scholars in various fields. For law scholars, Uber presents a case in which the legal classifications of “employee” and “independent contractor” that determine entitlement to employment and labor law protections become blurred (Dubal, 2017). For organizational scholars, Uber significantly departs from traditional organizational forms: the company does not own the majority of its productive infra structure; its drivers are not employees; and management often has to establish itself vis-á-vis local laws (Jordan, 2017). For sociologists of labor, Uber exemplifies a “worksite” par excellence of the so-called “bad jobs” that characterize post-Fordism (Kalleberg, 2011). It offers no employment protections or rewards, and it does not offer a fixed salary. As configurations in occupational structures constitute a global process, the social contract between the worker and the firm or the state has eroded, and Uber has migrated into various national contexts, from Pittsburgh, United States (Malin & Chandler, 2017) to Bengaluru, India (Surie & Koduganti, 2016) to Monterrey, Mexico, my concern here.

Not solely an example of a “bad” job, Uber presents new challenges for labor scholars insofar as it constitutes a new model of work that transposes its management structure into a virtualized platform, further amplifying labor flexibility. Studies that situate Uber as representative of post-capitalism, similar to how the Ford Motor Company became representative of Fordism, refer to the implications of the Uber model of work for the intensification of labor precarity. Yet precisely how Uber exercises control over the labor it deploys has remained obscure. Scholars such as Rosenblat and Stark (2016) emphasize the structural features of the Uber platform, whose algorithm is designed to establish an informational asymmetry between the company and its drivers, involving blind passenger acceptance, algorithmic pricing, rating systems, and partial disclosure of demand patterns, all of which serve to produce “indirect control” over workers. Others, such as Sheiber (2017), emphasize the more cultural features of the Uber app, which they see as invested with features – appealing graphics and sounds, motivational notifications, and “heat wave” maps of consumer demand – that make the Uber app feel like a video game. The latter view is consistent with scholars who have stressed the emergence of entrepreneurial ideologies that serve to re-cast the uncertainties that come with precarious labor as being “fun” and “emancipatory” (Vallas & Prener, 2012).

Drawing on three months of ethnographic work in which I rode along 50 Uber drivers in the city of Monterrey, Mexico. I attempt to bridge these structural and cultural accounts, thereby showing how Uber manages to elicit a sustained interest from the drivers it deploys. Following the line of labor process theories that uses the construct of “work games” as a conceptual device (Burawoy, 1979; Sallaz, 2014; Sharone, 2007), I develop a meso-level analysis of the work routines and subjective found among Uber drivers in Monterrey, providing what is to the best of my knowledge the first ethnographic study of Uber drivers in Mexico.

My findings suggest that in the absence of any wage provisions, during the course of the working day, drivers tend to become engrossed in the game of earning coins. Playing this work game requires developing particular strategies, often combining the careful management of time with an extensive knowledge of the spatio-temporal rhythms of urban life in Monterrey, all with an eye toward generating the most possible revenue. In this way, the game succeeds in coupling the interests of drivers with the interest of management in maintaining a readilyavailable labor pool. Where the game can be played in its more pleasurable and engaging form, the daily experience of driving for Uber acts to reproduce the entrepreneurial ideology of “being your own boss” (which the company actively propounds). At the same time, because there are few barriers to entry into the Uber workforce, the company often absorbs drivers coming from many different employment trajectories. This means that different participants experience the earning coins game in sharply divergent ways. Because drivers coming from the professional sector enjoyed a high degree of control as to when to log in and off, they tended to experience the game in “fun mode,” which largely sustained the firm’s entrepreneurial ideology and encouraged them to align their interests with those of the firm. By contrast, drivers who came from the more precarious sector enjoyed few such choices: they often had to work extensive hours in order to earn revenue at the end of the day (e.g., to pay off the rental car charges they incurred). They could thus be said to play the game in survival mode. For them, the appeal of the game was fractured, leading some drivers to question the rules of the game, perceiving their interests as sharply diverging from those of the firm.

CHANGES IN THE LABOR MARKET OF MONTERREY

Monterrey is a theoretically generative site in which to investigate the deploy ment of Uber. During the 1940s throughout the early 1980s, Monterrey was an important site of industrialization for the development of the import substitution industrialization (ISI) model (Toledo, 2015). The ISI was a state-driven economic model that aimed to promote rapid industrialization and urbanization and achieve integration in the global political economy. Under the ISI model, there was a redistributive state–citizen relationship in which national economic growth was accompanied by a productive absorption of the labor force (Bayón, 2006). This model, in parallel with Fordism, was accompanied by formal employment and corporatist industrial relations that aided in establishing a consistent labor supply that was faithful to the state. For Monterrey, the occupational structure of the decades of 1940s to 1986 was heavily concentrated in the manufacturing sector, and it was characterized by formal employment and an opportunity for social mobility (Solis, 2005).

The city of Monterrey became an emblematic site in which the structural transformations of the ISI model were palpably evident. During the 1940s toward the end of the 1970s, Monterrey was a prominent site for state-led industrialization: there was a limited number of firms that specialized in intermediary good such as glass, cement, and steel. The creation of these industries was accompanied by an overall increase in the economic prosperity for the city. During the ISI period, the manufacturing sector dominated the labor market of Monterrey. Employment in the state-led industries such as Fundidora, a steel and metal factory, or other private industrial corporations such as Vitro, a glass manufacturer, offered industrial workers employment stability and wages that were significantly higher than elsewhere in Mexico (Martinez, 2016; Solis, 2005). As a result of the economic crisis of the early 1980s, the economic prosperity that industrialization of ISI model sparked was interrupted. Many industries, including Vitro, were involved in heavy downsizing as a strategy to accommodate to the global external market. Fundidora, which had given the city an industrial identity, closed its doors in 1986, resulting in the termination of employment of thousands of industrial workers.

The transition from the ISI model to the neoliberal model was accompanied by a series of significant changes in the structure of the labor market in Monterrey, including a transition of employment from the manufacturing to the service sec tors (Garcia & De Oliveria, 2001; Martinez, 2006). Furthermore, there was an increase in the sectors that had traditionally been the most precarious: microenterprises, domestic work, and unskilled self-employment. Accompanying these transitions was an overall decrease in real wages and an increase in poverty levels (Solis, 2005). In summary, the structural-transformations occurring since the 1970s at a global scale were significant for the city of Monterrey, particularly in the transformation of its labor market.

Cultural shifts, too, accompanied the dismantling of the ISI model. The Import Substitution Model, and the cultural inheritance of the Mexican Revolution, had fomented a discursive ethos that promoted loyalty to the state (Gonzalez Navarro, 1961; Martinez, 2016). For those employed by the state, the syndicalist relations present in the work place achieved material concessions and helped sustain a social identity among workers based on a form of solidarity uniting workers, the factory, and the state (Gonsalez-Navarro, 1961; Martinez, 2016). The aforementioned structural transformations in the labor market occurring in the 1970s were accompanied by a change in the dominant discourse. The previous discursive and symbolic representations of work relations no longer had institutional resonance and a new imported entrepreneurial ideology emerged to legitimize the uncertainties that come with labor precarity (Alonso & Fernandez, 2013; Vallas & Prener, 2012). The entrepreneurial ideology attempts to promote an ethos that fervently rejects the bureaucratic structures that previously had given labor security; dependency to wage labor is seen as an outdated impediment to creativity and self-determination.

When Uber arrives in Monterrey in 2012, well into the transition to the neoliberal model, it manifested an example par excellence of novel forms of work organization: there is no fixed-salary, it does not offer employment protections or health insurance, and there is even the absence of a flesh-and-bones management figure. Furthermore, the Uber corporation deliberately engages in promoting the entrepreneurial ideology for labor recruitment. During my stay in Monterrey, I observed Uber recruitment publicity in high billboards, radio-ads, and pamphlets that were given out in subway stations: they all displayed in bold letters a slogantriad of emancipatory resonance: Be your Own Boss, Set your own Schedule and Earn Instant Money. To understand how Monterey workers responded to such discourse, one must develop a deep understanding of the nature of the work they perform and the subjective understanding their working lives foster. In the following sections, I present an ethnographic study of Uber drivers in the city of Monterrey. I employ the conceptual device of work-games as introduced by Michael Burawoy (1979), in an attempt to situate the linkages between subjectiv ity and structure.

RESEARCH DESIGN

I ventured to the city of Monterrey, Mexico, in 2017, during the hot summer months of June, July, and August. Unlike the labor-process theorists before me, my ethnographic site was not confined to a fixed physical location. In part, this was a consequence of the raison d’etre of the service. However, Uber differs from its direct competitors: taxis, who objectively performed the same service of transporting people from point A to point B. Taxi drivers in Monterrey are attached to different bases controlled by their respective union leader comprising a quasi-fixed location where drivers are gathered and then depart to different locations when the customer arrives. For Uber, consumer and driver are joined together via the algorithmic process of the Uber application. In this vein, my smartphone with my downloaded consumer-specific Uber application became my field “localizer.” Furthermore, unlike labor theorists of the ethnographic tradition who go into their field site in accordance with the established working hours, Uber drivers can operate at any time of the day. In this vein, and to achieve variation among drivers, I solicited the Uber service at various points of the day: mornings, afternoons and late at nights. In addition, I solicited the ride service from various municipalities of the city of Monterrey, including San Pedro, Santa Catarina, San Nicolas, Guadalupe, and Monterrey. These municipalities vary tremendously in their level of economic prosperity, with San Pedro being the richest and Guadalupe being the poorest (Fisher & Taub, 2017). It was often the case that drivers resided in Guadalupe, Monterrey, or San Nicolas and would travel to San Pedro to work.

During my stay in Monterrey, I requested rides from 60 Uber drivers. When I entered each car, I was attentive, and took notes on how drivers engaged with the Uber application, the manner in which they adorned their car, and how they interacted with me as a consumer. The hectic traffic of Monterrey, and the fact that I was often traveling across municipalities, allowed me to have rides of approximately 30–40 minutes each. Once I was in the car, I greeted drivers, and then began with a conversation. I mimicked the common parlance one has when conversing with a transportation service provider: How’s your day going? How’s work going? How long have you been an Uber driver? Do you have another employment besides Uber? Where did you work before being an Uber driver? Do you like being an Uber driver? What schedule do you set up for yourself?

These questions were employed merely to provide a basic guideline, as often drivers dwelled on and directed the conversation toward those questions that sparked their interests the most. I listened carefully to their answers, annotating basic points in my fieldnotes notebook during the ride. Immediately after arriving at my destination, I reconstructed in entirety the interview and complemented my notes with other relevant observations.

When conversing with the drivers, I maintained my status as a covert researcher.1 I was situated in the field as a consumer; this with the purpose of further morphing the questions I asked as everyday parlance, as opposed to embedding the questions in a researcher-worker context. My position as a Mexican national, as well as my past residency in the city allowed me to be perceived as a local consumer.

However, my account remains partial. Drivers interacted with me in accordance with my identity as a consumer as well as my gender, race, and perceived socioeconomic status (a light-skinned Mexican in her 20s perceived as a university student). My positionality was particularly salient when drivers would promote the Uber service as something beneficial for “someone like me”: a young women who is expected to constantly request the Uber service to attend leisure activities. I used my perceived position and driver’s engagement with me as the basis on which to foster rich conversations about their perceptions of Uber, their interactions with passengers, and especially their experience of their daily activi ties on the road.

My research strategy departs from other labor-process theorists in the ethnographic tradition (Burawoy, 1979; Sallaz, 2015), as I was not employed as an Uber driver. Whereas I lost potential insight that might be generated through direct experience in the jobsite, I gained knowledge into the experiences of Uber work for different drivers.2 My observations were notable in at least two respects. First, of the Uber drivers, all but one (59 out of 60) were male, suggesting that despite the situation of labor precarity expanding to both men and women workers in Monterrey, Uber has not become a common labor option for women. This is consistent with the gendered stratification of the transportation sector of Monterrey: in which 86.7% are men, and 13.3% are women (INEGI, 2018). Second and not surprisingly, given the low barriers to entry into the ride-hailing industry, the labor market for Uber accommodated a wide variety of drivers exhibiting differ ent employment trajectories. Those coming from the professional sector included drivers who had a college degree and worked or had worked as accountants in offices, salesman in corporations or as low-level managers. Those coming from the industrial sector included drivers who had worked as operators in manufacturing factories. Those coming from the precarious sectors were ex-taxi-drivers or waiters and bartenders. Finally, there were students who had no prior record of employment or who had worked in the precarious sectors (Table 1).

I translated all interviews and fieldnotes from Spanish to English. I then trans ported my data to ATLAS.ti, and inductively developed my coding scheme to examine variation between the subjects in regards to their experience as Uber drivers. Consistent with the extended case method (Burawoy, 2009), which encourages the researcher to enter the field armed with a theoretical tradition, my analytical framework focused on the manners in which drivers experienced their everyday work-life.

THE CONSTITUTIVE ELEMENTS OF THE WORK-GAMES

The theoretical tool of work-games, as proposed by Burawoy (1979) aims to pro vide a dialectical solution to the contradictions of the agent/structure dichotomy. The ethnographer, embedded in a specific worksite, documents the lived experience of work. It employs the metaphor of work-games to note the strategies workers use to achieve outcomes and infuse meaning into their work routines. Studying the Geer Allied Corporation, a Fordist manufacturing firm, Burawoy (1979) found that workers’ daily lives were structured by the company’s piecerate system and daily production quotas. To adapt to what amounted to harsh and unrewarding jobs, workers constructed a shop-floor culture that construed their jobs in terms of a game (“making out”). Playing this game that workers enabled workers to position themselves in relation to one another, usually by demonstrating their skill at overcoming even tight piece rates. Yet playing the game required that workers engage in what were essentially productivity contests that tended to obscure the profit-driven interests of the firm. Being absorbed in the work-game of making-out coupled the interests of workers in acquiring monetary rewards and job security with the interests of management in extracting effort from workers.

Sallaz (2015) later extended the metaphor of the work-game to the context of a post-Fordist call center. Sallaz notes that being left in the workplace unprepared, and without explicit or formal “rules,” stimulated workers to engage in different strategies to manage the computer system while interacting successfully with consumers. Being absorbed in these strategies, a work-game emerged that construed work in terms of an “autonomous learning game,” in which work ers had to fuse everyday conversational norms with the demands of their computer systems. Playing this work-game had results that were similar to those Burawoy identified: they coupled the interest of workers in portraying themselves as capable workers with the interest of the firm in extracting effort from workers. Similarly, Sharone (2007) contextualizes the process of job searching as work. In an American support organization for unemployed white-collar workers. Sharone finds a “job-searching” game: “job-searchers” are engaged in strategic decision-making to find employment: creating a carefully crafted resume, fostering a positive attitude, developing and time-organization skills. As “job searchers” fuel their time and energy into individualized decision-making strategies the absorbing “job-searching” game serves to impede a critique toward structural conditions of unemployment.

Games at times help ensure the reproduction of the social structure, as to when the game serves to successfully couple the interests of management with those of the workers. At such times, involvement in the game aligns everyday practices with hegemonic ideologies. At other times, however, the changing dynamics of games can have different effects, and understanding these can help serve as a point of departure for the study of resistance. For Burawoy (1979), a game cease to be absorbing when the uncertainty of outcomes either becomes too great or beyond the control of the players, or when the game becomes too predictable (thus producing indifference to the outcomes). When a game loses its allure, it prompts the players to question the rules of the game. In his ethnographic study, Sallaz (2015) demonstrates that the learning game was successful in extracting effort from workers. However, this effort was only momentary: given the lack of monetary rewards (which were constant regardless of the workers’ efforts), workers soon found the game too taxing to endure, and frequently left the firm in spite of their need for subsistence. Sharone (2007, 2013) too notes instances in which the work-game fails to serve as a tool of reproduction, as when a discrepancy arises between lived experience and culturally predominant discourses in the wider society.

In the following segments, I introduce the working-games that the Uber managerial structure foments. In this context, the “rules of the game” have been transposed to a virtualized platform.

FROM THE FACTORY TO THE APP: THE UBER VIRTUALIZED MANAGEMENT SYSTEM

When Burawoy (1979) wrote Manufacturing Consent, he was writing in the context of Fordism. The Allied manufacturing corporation was guided by a logic of reciprocity – in which the firm was interested in consolidating a stable and loyal workforce, thus offered the workers a good salary and decent working conditions. Unlike the firms of the early twentieth century, which were involved in despotic and direct techniques of control of workers, the Allied corporation operated under indirect control: at the micro-level, it established elementary guidelines such as specific quotas, but for the most part allowed workers a high degree of discretion in the shop-floor. From this allowed discretion, an autonomous work-culture emerges, which is nevertheless coupled with the interests of management.

Thirty-six years later, and in the post-Fordist context, I am writing not of a manufacturing firm located in the United States, but of an Internet-based service corporation that has expanded to cities across the globe. Aside from the staff in the register office, once Uber drivers were “signed-in” to operate as Uber drivers, they did not see a flesh-and-bones management figure again, or any fellow drivers. All the managerial structure, or the “rules of the game” are transposed into the Internet-based application. Not only does Uber establish primary guidelines for drivers, but it a priori and strategically constructs a game-like experience. In this manner, the work-game does not arise from workers in the everyday shop floor experience, but rather is a priori proposed by management. Using variou design techniques: logarithmic match to passengers, heat-wave maps of consumer demand, alluring visuals, and an accelerating sound notifications, Uber replicates an individualized video-game experience (Sheiber, 2017). However, even if the working-game in Uber is individualized, it is not surprising that similar and pat terned strategies to navigate the game arise. In the following segment, I refer to those strategies that Uber drivers employ to earn the most-possible revenue when they are logged into the application. The game, which is proposed by management and then further perpetuated by drivers is successful in extracting effor from drivers, and maintaining and always available labor-pool. Furthermore, given that the drivers solely interact with management in the form of a virtualized, Internet-based application it constitutes a form of indirect control, that foments a faux notion that Uber lacks management structure. However, in later segments, I refer to moments when the game ceases to be absorbing, and thu there is a rupture in the appeal of the game.

LOGGING-IN: THE VIRTUAL WORK-GAME OF EARNING COINS

Unlike the jobs found in Monterrey in the industrial sector during the Import Substitution Model, which offered a fixed salary, adequate working conditions, and employment protections, laboring for Uber does neither offer any protection nor a fixed salary. Rather, for each ride, Uber keeps 25% of the price, and drivers keep 75%.3 The interest of Uber drivers is to be able to earn the most possible revenue in the time that they are logged-in in the application. The interest of Uber is to be able to maintain a readily available labor-pool that can offer the service to consumers in the areas that are the most needed. During my stay in Monterrey, it did not matter if it was day or night or my city location, I never waited longer than 10 minutes for a ride to arrive. The hyper-fast delivery of the service provided me with a clue that Uber was successful in extracting continuous effort from drivers despite the low price of the ride and the high-commission that Uber asks for. Given that Uber operates in the form of indirect control, in which the figure of management is hidden in a virtual application. A question arises: How exactly did Uber extract effort from drivers? Unlike the Allied corporation, where management establishes a specific quota, Uber drivers are theoretically allowed to log-in or log-off of the application when they desire. However, without the protections of the working-parameters of the jornada laboral,4 coupled with the motivation to earn a decent revenue, Uber drivers become engrossed in the game of earning coins: that is in finding strategies to earn the most possible revenue when they logged-in into the application. At the end, given that there was not a physical management figure exerting control over workers as to the rides to accept, or when to log-in or log-off the application, the work-game helped hide the presence of management and thus aid to reinforce the entrepreneurial ideology.

Strategy I: Accept All Rides

As previously established, laboring for Uber does not offer a fixed salary or a fixed schedule. For this matter, Uber drivers engage in a series of engrossing strategies, including accepting all possible rides, to be able to earn an adequate earning, after car-related expenses such as gas, insurance, and in some cases the monthly rent of the car. When drivers log-in into the application they get matched through a mathematical logarithm to the nearest consumer who is requesting a ride at the moment. The driver has 30 seconds to accept or decline the ride in case of rejection, the ride is passed to another nearby driver. This limited-time incentive, prompted the drivers to click “accept ride” fast enough as to secure the ride. Many times, I was still in the car with an Uber ride, when I will hear the sound-notification announcing another ride request: each time, I observed the drivers “accept” the next ride. When riding with Carlos, a retired factory worker, the sound-notification came out, he turned to me and said: “Hitting that accept button becomes instinct.” Similarly, Francisco a 60-year-old salesman and Uber driver, described feeling addicted to clicking the “accept button”:

I am telling you, five minutes don’t pass when I have another request for a ride, to be honest it becomes addicting. My wife tells me to come home to eat but then I have another customer in the line, and I can’t stop working. I see it as a videogame and you see the rides as coin symbols.

Further, aside from always clicking “accept,” drivers developed strategies to mold their daily working routines in the application to minimize wasting time, and be able to accept rides at all possible moments. Jorgue-Luis, a former officeworker avoided drinking any water when he was logged-in in the Uber application as to avoid going to the restroom and losing potential rides. He told me: “If you really have to go, then you stop quickly in the convenience store and then sign-in back immediately. Gabriel’s strategy was to “accept” those rides that were the closest, and to deny any rides that were too far of his current location:

Sometimes, I take too long to pick up the passenger, the map tells me is going to be four minutes, but it ends up being ten or twelve, and that’s not worth it because you waste time and money for gas. Traffic is real killer. The key is to get locations that are close to each other as possible.

Former charismatic taxi driver, Carlos was grateful for the logarithmic feature of Uber, he used the term “kicking bottles” to refer to the act of waiting in a taxi-base for passengers to arrive:

Look, in this Uber thing, you have to leave your house having a good big breakfast, and then later have lunch out, it doesn’t matter if you spend some money on food. The interesting thing about this [Uber] is that you have to eat out because if you go home you end up wasting a lot of time, because you end up watching television, that’s when you are really fucking up. This [Uber] is all about being in the car, quiet and sitting down for your ten hours, if you don’t do that you are not going to earn any money. You also have to love [emphasis on the love] driving otherwise you’re fucked.

Strategy II: Locate the “Movimiento”

Drivers not only engaged in the micro-managing of their own time to “accept” all possible rides. In addition, drivers became absorbed in developing a precise knowledge of the patterns of demand among consumers in the city of Monterrey, or movimiento (movement) as was often referred by Uber drivers. When drivers log-in into the application they are provided with a heat map, which display the hot-zones of the city; that is, the parts of the city in which there is more demand for Uber. Furthermore, to incentivize drivers to log-in to the application when there was more consumer-demand, Uber send e-mails to register drivers informing them that dynamic pricing was in place. Dynamic pricing refers to when the price of a ride is momentarily algorithmically elevated given that there is more consumer demand than drivers operating. These incentives served merely as an elementary roadmap to the game, as drivers developed a more complex knowledge of the tempo of the city: the movement of people to places concentrated in specific spatial and temporal intersections. Uber drivers acquired an organized knowledge of the social and cultural dynamics of the city, which I shall organize at two levels.

At the highest level was the knowledge of the proximate big cultural events that would attract a high volume of audiences and thus potential Uber costumers who will be requesting a ride to arrive and to leave the event. I arrived at Monterrey at the beginning of June and I could not avoid seeing the numerous advertisements of a music festival with prestigious international and national musicians that was going to transpire in the month of July. The ads were present in the airport, in highway billboards, and in pamphlets distributed in the subway. I even received an e-mail from Uber inviting me to request the Uber service if I was going to attend the festival. For Uber drivers, the music festival was the ultimate jackpot for the “earning coins” game. A sort of brief period of work ing frenzy where “winning” at the game was a given. The thousands of festival attendees would probably avoid the high prices of the parking lot, and/or be too intoxicated to drive. A week prior to the concert, Uber drivers communicated to me the anticipation they felt for the music festival not because they themselves were attending but because they knew that there would be a high demand for Uber. Even better, dynamic pricing would be set as well. After the festival weekend was over, the music festival was at the center of conversation with the drivers. I asked Uber driver Antonio how work was going. He responded: “Movement is bad right now, but you know what that’s ok the festival last week gave me enough money to take it easy.”

Aside from being attentive to the city’s big cultural events, Uber drivers were also engaged in strategies to partition the week into the most lucrative working days that will exploit the movimiento of the residents of Monterrey. I too, by soliciting Uber rides in all days of the week learned to locate the patterns with the highest moments of movimiento that were directly linked to the social activities of the citizens of Monterrey. On Mondays and Tuesdays, there was more unhappy drivers reporting losing at the “earning coins” game, meaning that they were not making an adequate revenue. On Wednesdays and Thursdays, the movement was starting to pick up, culminating in the weekend days of Friday, Saturday, and

Sunday. This was further intensified during quincena weekends – Fridays of coordinated paydays, when costumers had money in their pockets to go out. Consider the strategic working schedule given to me by Daniel, an approximately 30-yearold Uber driver who was formerly a manager of a 7-Eleven:

There are times where I don’t work on Mondays, or Tuesdays, and then Wednesday comes and I am all rushed up on the weekend to make up for lost time. But to be honest, you do earn it back on the weekends, cause there are just so many things going on, I leave my personal cell phone at my house, because my friends always want me to take me up partying and stuff. Look, I have it all figured out: Wednesdays, people go to the movies. Thursday’s, you start seeing movement from parties and night clubs. And then Friday to Sunday is just ride after ride, people in Monterrey never get tired of partying.

The working game of earning coins persisted even outside the crafted instructions of the Uber application. This was the case with Enrique, who operated outside the application and took advantage of the lack of transportation in areas outside the city.

I like going to the outskirts of the city because there is no good public transportation, so people don’t have another option to get around. Sometimes, I even go to Apodaca [municipality in Monterrey]to the bus stops and then I stop and ask the workers where are they going, and if they are going to the same place I am going I pick them up, and I charge like 20 pesos to each one [1 dollar]. The bus charges them [50 cents] but they are happy to spend 50 cents more for some air conditioner.

Strategies III: Earning Stars

Most of the time when I get inside a drivers’ car, I was often greeted with the flowing phrases with an accentuated over-polite tone: Good Morning m’am, Do you want me to change this music? Is the air conditioner ok for you? In one occasion, I was even offered chocolates and a water bottle. It did not take long for me to understand that not only did drivers had to “catch” the most possible rides, but in addition has to carefully mange interactions with passengers. Seconds after arriving at my destination, I receive a sound-notification through my smartphone reminding me to rate my ride experience: I had to make the decision whether to give my driver one up to five stars. According to several drivers, and then later confirmed by the Uber website, drivers could be denied access to the application, or in other words: get fired if they fellow below a 4.5 star average. Drivers engaged in several strategies to ensure that they maintained an adequate star average in order to maintain earning coins, for if they fell in their average it was game-over for them.

It was frequently the case that after conversing for several minutes with drivers, they abandon their previous over-polite tone for a more casual nonchalant tone. I realized that service-work in Uber was not routinized, but rather I was given a tailored service in accordance with my demeanor as perceived by Uber drivers. This was confirmed by Valentin, a 40-year old former office worker, and now Uber driver. Valentin gave me an insight into his self-crafted typology to identify different types of passengers. His idea was to locate different types of passengers, and direct his behavior in accordance to each type. The first type, was the “talkative type.” Not surprisingly he placed me under this category. He said, “People like you are the talkative type, you know people who love to talk so you have to maintain conversation the whole trajectory of the ride.” The second type was the “polite but not talkative,” for this type of passenger, Valentin advise to greet politely but then remain quiet for the rest of the trip and give them their space. The third was type was the “rude but not talkative,” those passengers never responded to Valentin’s greetings, his strategy was again to not talk for the rest of the trip. Then finally, the fourth type of passenger, and according to Valentin “the worst kind of passenger” was the “talkative and rude,” he said: “You can imagine how those are like, with those ones you have to pray not to lose your temper.” I took the liberty, to illustrate Valentin’s 2 x 2 table (Fig. 1):

Other drivers expressed similar strategies to deal with difficult passengers; mainly to suppress the urge to talk back when a customer was being rude to not risk getting a low-star rating. Miguel, a former ex-factory worker told me the fol lowing anecdote:

Sometimes people can be really mean. Like the other day, I picked a lady from Escobedo (a county in Monterrey) and she started yelling at me like crazy. She said I was a lazy scum that she had to be in the sun all day, while I was here in my car that had air conditioner. I didn’t say anything, I stayed quiet, and just answered “ok” and “ok” to everything she said.

Other drivers like Julian, a former engineer who had become an Uber driver after failing to find employment, went out of their way to please rude costumers:

The other day, this woman with a baby got into my car, the women looked like she was having a hard time, she was being real rude to me, then I heard say on the phone that she had a major headache, so I offer her a pill that I had, and water too. So later she thanked me, and she even apologized for being rude to me. I like keeping bottles of water in my car, I don’t have any at the moment, I have to go to the store and supply myself.

A Hidden Management Structure

The absorbing game of earning coins – which was suggested by Uber and then further developed by the drivers – was successful in coupling the interest of

Uber in maintaining a readily available labor supply with the interest of drivers in generating an adequate revenue in a non-fixed salary working arraignment. Furthermore, the transposition of the management structure to a set of carefully designed virtualized instructions and email-incentives gave the impression that Uber was merely an “objective” mediator between driver and consumer, and not an employer. In other words, the “rule makers” and “rule enforcers” of the game became virtualized and thus hidden. Former 7-Eleven manager and now Uber driver had thought about returning to a manager job:

I started applying for jobs, polishing my resume and everything. And then they called me to set up an interview, and I didn’t go, I regretted even applying. Just the thought of having a boss again makes want to vomit. I prefer to stay here in Uber, here I am the one in control.

This “hidden” managerial structure further reinforced the “entrepreneurial ideology” that drivers were emancipated from the boss, and serve the purpose of redirecting the grievances and uncertainties emerging from precarious employ ment to individualized critiques of work-ethic. As told by Manuel, an approximately 30–40-year-old driver and waiter,

The cool thing about being an Uber driver is that I set my own, schedule, and I am my own boss. Here you discipline yourself. And those who work earn the money.

Furthermore, the embedded rating system of the Uber application reproduces a power asymmetry in which the consumer is given direct labor control. Specifically – in its ability to punish and reward drivers. This is expressed by Jorge- Luis who recounted me an experience he had when he first started laboring for Uber:

At the beginning, I would get lost with the GPS thing because I did not know to use it. In one my first rides, the customer was getting frustrated with me because we kept going the wrong way, I was terrified, when we arrived he told me that he was going to fill in a report. If a customer fills up a report, it can get ugly. They can fire you.

In this vein, part of the surveillance component of the managerial structure is outsourced to consumers, which in addition serves to redirect concerns arising in the worksite to consumers, rather than to a management figure.

Despite the common strategies shared by drivers to navigate Uber-work, there were significant differences in the manner in which Uber was experienced and understood by different drivers. Below I show how differences in experience stem primarily from the different employment trajectories of the Uber drivers.

DIFFERENT MODES OF PLAYING FOR DIFFERENT PLAYERS

When Burawoy (1979) conducted his ethnographic study, he observed a reliance on an internal labor market in the Allied Corporation. This meant that workers were drawn from within the labor pool of the firm, allowing management to establish an incentive structure of internal mobility. In the long run, the possibil ity of upward mobility within the firm, consolidated worker’s loyalty to the firm as they opted to remain in the firm rather than to seek employment elsewhere.

Thirty-six years later, in the context of the Uber corporation, I observed a total reliance on an external labor market for worker’s recruitment. In fact, Uber was dependent upon a situation of unemployment and underemployment in the city of Monterrey. In this vein, Uber was successful in absorbing drivers from a variety of employment trajectories. For instance, there were drivers coming from the professional sector, such as accountants and store-managers who were terminated from their previous employment, and were signing-in to labor for Uber while waiting to transition to another employment. Other drivers coming from the industrial sector were recently fired from manufacturing factories and opted to labor for Uber, without the certainty of returning to an industrial-related job. As I conducted my ethnographic study, it became evident that there were distinctive working-experiences in Uber depending on the employment trajectory- and labor situation of the drivers. Employing the metaphor of the work-game: the reliance on an external labor market created different players and different modes of playing, either playing in “fun” mode, as in the case of drivers from the professional sector who still held their professional job and labor for Uber to supple ment their income; or, alternatively, playing in survival mode, as was the case for drivers who relied solely on the income they generated for Uber, and without a certain promise of returning to a “good” job. A third type was waiting mode, as with drivers who were working for Uber while in transition to a “good job.” These different modes of playing constituted different functions for the earning coins game in regards to the subjective understandings of work it produced: either a reproduction of the entrepreneurial ideology or a discursive resistance to Uber. To give more empirical resonance to the manner in which Uber creates different working experiences, in the following segment I present different players coming from different employment trajectories (Table 2).

Professional Players

Jose is an approximately 60-year old man, and has held a position as a manager in the subway control unit for the last 30 years. He expressed pride in his work, as he said: “I been doing this for years, it is pretty much my life, this is pretty much who I am.” He began laboring for Uber for the past three months, as a way to supplement his manager income:

I just log into Uber on my way to work, so I usually leave to work an hour or so, and pick up passengers on my way to downtown, where I work.

For Jose, he only engaged in the earning coins game for a brief period of his day. In other words, he had the control to log in and log off the application when he pleased. As he expressed:

I am really happy with this Uber thing because I am just going to work and earning some extramoney while am at it. It’s like the car gets paid for automatically

As Jose enjoyed a stable position as a manager, it allowed him to play the earn ing coins game in “fun mode.” Playing in these manner, allowed him to maintain a degree of control in the Uber-work game, and thus prompted him to conceive the rules of the game as fair. Furthermore, the game played in this mode also fostered ideological reproduction as there was a resonance between lived experience in the game and the entrepreneurial discourse that Uber promoted of “earning great money, and your own schedule.”

Roberto, young and charismatic also came from the professional sector. However, he no longer held his position as a sales manager in TELCEL, a monop olistic telecommunication corporation in Mexico. He told me he was in the mid dle of a lawsuit against his past employer, given that they had fired him without a finiquito: established legal pay that employers had to give at the moment of termination. His lawyer had advised him not to start employment in another site, and so he logged-in to labor for Uber while he was waiting for the lawsuit to end. Roberto, was playing the earning coins game in waiting mode, he did not conceive of Uber as his employment site, rather it was a manner to productively wait in this moment of transition. He spoke with bold certainty of returning to a job related to his professional career in business and administration. Furthermore, his previous work experience in a “good job,” where he previously enjoyed a fixed salary, and monetary compensations lead him to judge his experience in Uber merley as transitory, and not as an adequate work-situation. In fact, he had sued TELCEL precisely because they had stopped caring and protecting the worker. As he told me:

They(TELCEL) did not give a damn about us (workers) they would not defend us or protect us. To tell you the truth, I used to be very critical of the trade union but then I realized, that they were the ones that truly care for their workers. I also realized that we have to demand our right for collective bargaining.

Roberto, had inherited a cultural repertoire in his previous work organiza tion, which prompted him to value security and stability for the worker. This cultural repertoire was in direct opposition with the entrepreneurial ideology that Uber promoted. Although inevitably, while he logged-in into the Uber, Roberto became engaged in the earning coins’ game, the game in his case, was not successful in reproducing the entrepreneurial ideology. As the imported cultural understanding of the employer as someone who is supposed to protect and care for workers, and his credentials as a professional worker, prompted him to reject continuing laboring for Uber.

Industrial Players

Aside from professional workers, Uber in addition absorbed workers coming from the industrial sector. I observed the presence of two generations of industrial workers. Older factory workers who had worked in the domestic manufacturing corporations of the Import Substitution Model. These workers, like workers of the Fordist era, had enjoyed a lifetime of stability, and adequate salaries and were now retired. The younger generation were workers who were currently working or were recently fired from the transnational maquilas of the neoliberal period. Julio, represented the older generation of factory workers. He had worked for Vitro, a domestic glass manufacturing, for the past 40 years. He was now retired, and he had begun to log into Uber to productively spend his retirement.

When I retired, I started to work here [ as Uber driver] so that the car could live, and to have something to do, but to be honest I am just in deep nostalgia all the time. There in Vitro (glass manufacturing firm), management did everything to form a family with the workers you know? Most of the time, I find myself going to the factory and offering workers rides, or asking them if I can help in any way.

Similar to Roberto, Julio was playing the earning coins game in a waiting mode. While logged-in into the application, Julio was engaged in the work-game, and regarded laboring for Uber as a productive and “fun” past-time. For him, earning revenue in Uber was not an urgent concern as he only used his earnings to pay for car expenses. In his case, the working-game was successful in extracting effort from him, and to some extent in reproducing the entrepreneurial ideology. Julio could have spent his retirement in another manner: watching TV, or playing chess with his friends for instance. However, he decided to drive for Uber, as to not be sitting down dioquis as he said (Mexican slang to refer to the act of being of “no-use”). Further, the degree of control he enjoyed – being able to log in or log off when he wanted, prompted him to experience Uber as a productive and fun way to spent retirement. However, his long-term commitment was to the factory, even though he no longer worked there.

The situation of younger industrial workers was different from that of the retired workers. Often times, younger industrial workers were recently fired from factories. They had opted to sign into work for Uber as they had had trouble finding another employment in the industrial sector. Unlike the professional workers or the retired factory workers, driving for Uber was the only manner in which they could earn some revenue. For them, the earning-coins game was no longer a “fun” game, as drivers had to play in survival mode. Drivers did not enjoy control of when to log-in and log-off the application, and often had to labor for extensive hours (from 8 to 16 hours) in order to earn a decent revenue at the end of the day. As expressed by Juan, who was in his mid-thirties and who had recently been terminated from a steel factory:

I work Monday through Friday 10 or 12 hours, on Saturdays I try to work less, and then on Sunday’s that when it is my rest day to charge my energies for Monday. I try not to go out during the weekends, unless it is a family event on Saturday or something. But I keep calm, I cannot drink beer because I know I am going to be driving.

This lack of control made the outcome of the game – in this case earning a decent amount of revenue – too uncertain. As Burawoy (1979) had observed, when the outcomes of a game became too uncertain, the work-game ceases to be absorbing, and thus prompted the workers to question the rules of the game. For these drivers, laboring for Uber was not “fun”, as the world of leisure, and the world of work could no longer be mashed. Juan, later confessed to me with a face of anger and disappointment:

Well to be honest, Uber is not convenient for the worker, maybe it is for the consumer, but not for the worker. Because a price for a ride is so low, so for us between commission that Uber takes, and the gas, you end up with virtually nothing. We must be working lots of hours to be able to earn some revenue out of this. Uber does not invest nothing on you, you must invest everything, here who ends up winning really is Uber. To be honest, I do miss having a secured salary. In this business you earn what you put on, and well you do have to have self-discipline.

It is really interesting this new system, for the customer it is all good news cause they don’t have to be chasing the taxi anymore. You can even program from the day before when and where you want an Uber to pick you up.

However, it is important to note that as Juan was in a situation of unemploy ment he had no choice as to sign into Uber to earn a living. As he was obligated to engage in the previously outlined strategies to earn the most possible revenue, the working-game, as played in survival mode was still successful in extracting effort from him, even if it no longer achieved ideological reproduction, as there was an incongruence between the entrepreneurial ideology and lived experience in Uber.

Precarious Players

Workers who were coming from the precarious sector, such as waiters and bartenders, experienced the Uber working-game similar to the young industrial workers. They too were playing in a survival mode. However, the uncertainty of being successful at the game was further intensified for drivers coming from thi sector. I observed two groups of precarious workers: those who owned their own car, and those who did not own their cars. Drivers coming from the precarious sector who owned their cars were still playing in survival mode, as they too had to drive for extensive hours to earn an adequate revenue (10–12 hours) even if they maintained another employment in another precarious job. As fired industrial workers, precarious workers who owned their cars experienced great uncertainty while laboring for Uber, and often lamented the absolute dependency on consumer demand. As expressed by Jaime, a former taxi driver who had transferred to Uber after experiencing a decrease in demand for taxis:

I think the commission that Uber charges is really a lot. They invest nothing on you, you have to pay for everything yourself: gas, car maintenance insurance. Right now the commission is at 25%, a buddy was telling me that they [Uber] are thinking of making it 28%, I shut him up: I did not want to hear horror stories.

Again, playing the game in survival mode, fractured the appeal of the game. Working extensive hours without significant rewards prompted Jaime to question the “rules of the game.” And thus, to denounce Uber as an unfair employer, Jaime, recognized that his situation was better than those drivers who did not own their cars. As he told me: “I only work 10 hours, I’ve heard horrible stories from people who do not own a car, that they have to be working for 16 hours a day to be able to earn some revenue.” In Monterrey, a third-party rental business had emerged adjacent to the entrance of Uber. Those who did not own their cars5 rented cars from a socio (or associate). A socio owns a fleet of cars and then charges a monthly fee to drivers. Fees varied from socio to socio. According to drivers, the rental commission varied between 10,000 to 12,000 pesos (580–780 dollars). Drivers who had to resort to renting a car had a significant added expense to the already outsourced investments drivers had to make to operate as Uber drivers: gas, car repairs, and specialized insurance. For drivers without their own car, playing in survival mode was further intensified. Pedro, a 30-something young man used to work in a convenience store, decided to quit and join Uber.

Given that he does not own a car, he has to pay 10,000 pesos monthly rent to a socio. Pedro labors for Uber around 12 hours a weekday, and during the weekend had to increase his working-hours up to 16 hours, returning home around 2 to 3 in the morning. He confessed that since he had to pay for rent, commission and of course earn a viable living, he was extremely dependent upon the hours of movimiento. However, the strategies in which he engaged to locate consumer demand – particularly driving in the places that Uber notified as being the most profitable and where demand pricing was activated – proved to be useless. The high level of uncertainty in the game, lead Pedro to question its rules:

That whole deal about the dynamic pricing is a fraud, they charge you guys [ the costumers] more but they don’t actually pay us workers more. Look, when the dynamic pricing is in place they usually send us a notification so I get going but then I realize they are the same prices. I’ve tried complaining, you know sending them reports via email you know, but you never win! They answer you back saying that they are right, and you can’t make them back off that stance, and then we don’t have a physical space were we can voice our complaints, Is really easy to hide yourself behind a screen you know?

The high level of uncertainty in the game, lead Pedro to question the rules of the game. Furthermore, his experience of working significant hours a day without extracting significant rewards, prompted Pedro to reject the dominant entrepreneurial ideology of being your own, and being in control. For Pedro, Uber was an employer, and a very unjust employer who hid behind a screen and thus, was not held accountable.

Students

Aside from absorbing people coming from a variety of employment trajecto ries, Uber also absorbed those had not yet had an employment history: students. Given that I conducted my fieldwork during the summer, I encountered several students who joined Uber to take advantage of their time-off at their respective universities. Similar to drivers coming from the precarious sector, within students, stratification also occurred along the lines of assets: those who owned their cars (or their parents cars), and those who had to pay a monthly rent. Consider, the experience of two students: Alan and Saul. Alan, was an 18-yearold charismatic young man, two years prior in his sixteenth birthday, he had received a car from his parents. Alan, had decided to join Uber to pass the time in his summer vacation:

I own my own car and I drive it around when I don’t have a lot of stuff to do. I am currently an aviation student, I signed in to Uber because I did not want to be at home you know.. not doing anything. And more than anything, I do it for fun, because I have fun driving around the city and meeting new people.

For Alan, having a car, as well as having a high degree of control as to when to log-in and log-off the application allowed him to play the earning coins game in a fun mode. Furthermore, Alan’s daily experience laboring for Uber was congruent with the entrepreneurial ideology that Uber promoted, and in thus, prompted him to adopt celebratory understandings toward Uber work. Saul’s experience was different than Alan’s. Saul, was a student in engineering, and had signed-in to work for Uber during his summer vacation in an attempt to earn some income. Unlike Alan, Saul did not own his car, and had to pay car rent. Saul, worked around 12–16 hours a day:

I do enjoy driving a lot. But after a while, it gets old. You get tired of being trapped in the car, you back starts to hurt, your neck starts to hurt. It’s not pretty. In order to gain some money, you must be working about 12 to 16 hours. Because you have to be working those hours in order to be paying for gas, the Uber commission, and the rent. You can’t be a lazy person, if you want to do this.

For Saul, working extensive hours a day prompted him to reject the notion of Uber as being fun. The appeal of the earning coins game had fractured; driving, once an enjoyable activity, had lost its appeal: laboring for Uber caused physical pains. However, the fractured appeal of the game did not lead Saul to ques tion Uber as an employer. He relegated an unfavorable experience in Uber to a questionable work-ethic. Saul, despite being disappointed in his labor experience, continued to frame his understandings of work in terms of the entrepreneurial ideology.

I don’t think forming a union or anything like that is really relevant for Uber drivers, I mean I am thinking of this just as a temporary thing, there is no point of taking it too seriously. I am in a Facebook group of Uber drivers though, but we mostly talk about the best routes, and how to deal with annoying customers.

After Playing the Game

For drivers coming from the professional sector, there was a high expectation that their stay in Uber was going to be momentary; many had better jobs complementing their driving and enabling them to play the earning coins game in fun mode. For the university students, there was an expectation that they will hopefully find a job in their careers, inclining them to play the game in waiting mode. Thus for both students and professionals, the interest in reforming the conditions of Uber work was simply irrelevant. There was no rupturing of the work game, and hence little impetus for resistance. For other drivers, however, where the nature of the game stood at odds with entrepreneurial ideology, the future remained uncertain and there seemed little reason for hope. For young drivers from the industrial sector, for example, given the deindustrialization of the city after the collapse of ISI, the expectancy of returning to a job in manufacturing was low. For drivers coming from the precarious sector, playing the earning coins game in survival mode provided no sense that an alternative was possible. Leaving Uber was likely to mean only moving to yet another precarious job. For these latter drivers, I saw little evidence that the rupturing of the work game was likely to provoke acts of resistance. That is, even for precarious drivers, Uber was seen as a momentary source of income, even for those who had driven for Uber for a lengthy period of time. As expressed by Daniel, a bartender who has been an Uber driver for the last years, and who was considering exiting Uber because he did not earn enough money after rent and commission.

CONCLUSION

Consistent with labor-process studies in the ethnographic tradition that employ work-games as a theoretical framework (Burawoy, 1979; Sallaz, 2015; Sharone, 2007), this study of Uber drivers in Monterrey attempts to show how management structures foment subjective understandings of work. However, this study differs from previous studies in that in the context of Uber, the management structure is now virtualized. The transition from a physical worksite to a virtual Internet-based application presents several methodological and theoretical challenges for researchers who are interested in documenting the labor-process in the current historical and economic context. In this study, I address some of these challenges, and thus hope to contribute to the emergent and on-going knowledge of the labor-process in the gig-economy.

Given, the nature of virtual job-sites, workplace ethnographers need to develop creative strategies to gain access to the de-collectivized settings that platform work often entails. In this study, I adopted the role of a consumer, and solicited rides from different parts of the city in order to gain access to the compartmentalized work-site and thereby interact with drivers. Distributing my data collection work throughout a 24-hour period, I engaged drivers in social interaction, conducting semi- and unstructured interviews as a way to identify drivers’ work strategies and subjective experiences of the work. Doing so revealed that, much as Burawoy and his followers expect, driver often experience Uber driving as a game (much as the firm itself hopes). Yet I also found a pronounced heterogeneity in the work trajectories of drivers. As firms become more depended upon an external labor market for recruitment it becomes increasingly important to depart from a homogeneous treatment of workers and locate different experiences of precarity within the same worksite. While Sallaz (2017) found differences among the call center workers based on class, gender, and race, the differences I found stemmed from varying employment trajectories, which shaped how workers experienced the earning coins game. For many – and perhaps for the most influential group, on whom Uber mostly relied – the presence of the game largely obscured the presence of the managerial structures that extracted effort from their labor. The result was a sense of “being one’s own boss” in spite of the many condition that exerted control over their work.

Other studies will likely find other source of difference among gig-workers that differentially shape their different working experiences and which may further exacerbate social inequalities among their ranks. A final consideration is that in the current neoliberal period, we are no longer dealing with strictly domestic firms. Uber and other forms of gig-work emerge in the United States and then are transported to economies located all across the globe. Researchers need to address how app-based corporations exploit labor-market transformations across different national economies.
