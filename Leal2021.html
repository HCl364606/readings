Network Inequalities and International Migration in the Americas1

Diego F. Leal

University of South Carolina

Based on predictions derived from migration systems theory, this study argues that chain-like international migratory exchanges tend to be organized as transitive structures patterned along an intelligible hierarchy of attractiveness between countries. To test this idea, harmonized bilateral migrant stock data are used to estimate country-to-country migration flows in the Americas in the second half of the 20th century. Then, temporal exponential random graph models are used to explain the emergence and structure of these estimated flows. Results show that triadic migratory chains are patterned as (nearly) perfect linear hierarchies. These hierarchical structural patterns are shown to be important to understand the structure and evolution of migration flows across the continent from both a statistical and a historical perspective.

INTRODUCTION

After centuries of being a pole of attraction for international migrants, in the late 1960s Latin America became for the first time a net exporter of emigrants (Pellegrino 1995; Cohen 2010). Massive emigration emerged, among other things, because of the large levels of inequality and slow economic growth observed in the region (Pellegrino 1995; Durand and Massey 2010). Difficult economic times became even more pressing during the decades to come, especially during the 1980s (Kliksberg 2001). In the last two decades of the 20th century, Latin America witnessed generalized poor economic growth due to the implementation of U.S.-sponsored neoliberal reforms and several economic crises in countries like Mexico, Brazil, Argentina, Colombia, and Ecuador (Portes and Roberts 2005). The increasing levels of economic inequality between countries in the Americas in general, and within Latin America in particular, further stimulated migration across the continent (Pellegrino 1995, 2003; Cerrutti and Parrado 2015). During this same period, many Latin American countries also endured dictatorships, revolutions, and civil wars that forced people to emigrate. Migration from countries such as Mexico, El Salvador, Haiti, Nicaragua, Colombia, Chile, Cuba, and Peru was triggered by political instability (Shellman and Stewart 2007; Eckstein 2009; Hiskey and Orces 2010; Silva and Massey 2015). A report from the United Nations’s Latin American and Caribbean Demographic Centre succinctly summarized all these changes by pointing out that the number of interna tional migrants as a proportion of the region’s total population grew from 0.7% in the 1960s to 2.5% in the early 1990s, which represented an increase from approximately 1.5 million to about 11 million migrants (CELADE 2000).

The literature on international migration in the Americas has not suffi ciently examined the structure and evolution of the system of migration flows across the continent during the years of this extensive influx of migrants. The paucity of holistic, system-level analyses is a long-standing shortcoming of this literature. In fact, prominent scholars noted decades ago that migration in the Americas was overwhelmingly analyzed using studies of selective flows, typically involving only one or two pairs ofcountries at a time, such as the flow of Mexicans to the United States (Massey et al. 1994). Repeated calls have continued to be made over the years regarding the necessity to develop sweeping studies of the Americas’ migration system in its entirety (Donato et al. 2010; Massey and Aysa-Lastra 2011; Cerrutti and Parrado 2015; DeWaard, Nobles, and Donato 2018). Yet, this literature is still dominated by research that focuses on only a handful of countries at a time and on the migration of Mexicans to the United States in particular (Garip 2016; Carrillo 2017; Andrews 2018; Asad and Hwang 2019; Bastia 2019).

This is a study of migratory dynamics across the Americas that builds on, and extends, migration systems theory (MST; Mabogunje 1970; Fawcett 1989; Zlotnik 1992; Jennissen 2007; DeWaard, Kim, and Raymer 2012; Bakewell 2014; DeWaard and Ha 2019; Windzio, Teney, and Lenkewitz 2019). MST often fails to be tested empirically in the context of large migration systems because of insufficient—and most of the time plainly inexistent—standardized and longitudinal international migration flow data (Zlotnik 1992; DeWaard et al. 2012), something especially true for regions outside the Global North such as Latin America (Cerrutti and Parrado 2015). This article focuses on testing and extending a key prediction of MST, namely, the expectation that migration systems are highly hierarchical structures (Fawcett 1989; Zlotnik 1992) typically clustered around a core receiving region (DeWaard et al. 2012; Bakewell 2014; see also Massey et al. 1993). This system-level prediction is formalized here in novel ways, by developing a theory of triadic closure in migration flows, which is then tested in the network of country-to-country migration flows in the Americas during the second half of the 20th century. By doing so, this study provides an encompassing analysis of the structural dynamics exhibited by migration flows across the Americas and, more specifically, of a set of network mechanisms that can help explain the structure and evolution of the Americas’ migration system at large. To conduct this analysis, the article uses state-of-the-art techniques developed to estimate place-to-place migration flows between countries on the basis of standardized migrant stock data (Abel 2013). Then, temporal exponential random graph (TERG) models are employed to characterize the structure and evolution of the America’s migration system in terms of the “diversity” of its flows (Czaika and de Haas 2014; DeWaard and Ha 2019), that is, in terms of which countries do—and do not—exchange flows of people, or migrants. Extensive sensitivity analyses corroborate the robustness of the results pre sented here.

THEORY

Abstract Systems MST: Coupling MST and Network Analysis

There is a consensus in the migration literature that Mabogunje (1970) is the foundational paper behind the MST tradition.2 Following Bakewell (2014), the work of Mabogunje (1970) still represents a solid base to characterize a migration system, which can be defined in two steps. First, as a set of three interacting “elements”: (1) a collection offlows of migrants, goods, and information between places (e.g., counties, countries); (2) a collection of strategies (e.g., migration decisions by households, migration policies); and (3) a collection of institutions (e.g., transnational communities, social networks). Second, as the dynamic structure that emerges from both the endogenous interactions between the three elements mentioned above (i.e., “feedback,” in MST parlance) as well as from the exogenous interactions between these elements and their larger environment (e.g., wars).

The concept of feedback plays a key role in Mabogunje’s theoretical con tribution and in MST more broadly (de Haas 2010; Bakewell 2014; Bakewell,

Kubal, and Peréira 2016; DeWaard and Ha 2019). Mabogunje (1970) distinguished between positive feedback, understood as endogenous or within-thesystem mechanisms that can potentially stimulate migration (e.g., flow of information about the types of jobs available in prospective host societies), and negative feedback, seen as endogenous mechanisms that can potentially halt migration (e.g., flow of information about the hostility of locals in prospective host societies). The interaction between different sources of feedback is expected to lead to the clustering of flows around relatively attractive places, thus ultimately making migration systems likely to be geographically patterned or “organized” (p. 13). These two insights, namely, the role of feedback and the geographic identifiability of migration systems, have fueled the MST literature to this day in its quest to identify how the changes that migration systems— and their respective elements—undergo are subsequently expressed in the geographic structure ofmigration flows (DeWaard et al. 2012; Fussell, Curtis, and DeWaard 2014; Willekens et al. 2016; Hauer 2017; DeWaard and Ha 2019).

Conceptualizing migration systems as “organized” structures with feedback and clustering has deep connections with network analysis. This study follows a renovated strain of MST that explicitly takes advantage of the fact that migration flows in a migration system are expected to be geographically patterned, which naturally renders them to be studied as migration networks defined as a set of places (e.g., countries) connected through flows of migrants (Nogle 1994; DeWaard et al. 2012; Hauer 2017; DeWaard and Ha 2019; Windzio et al. 2019). Making migration networks the main object of study directly locates this article in the “abstract systems” variant of MST (Bakewell 2014, p. 305), a variant that squarely focuses on studying the structure of migration systems modeled as “a network of places exchanging populations” (p. 305).

There are variants of MST that focus on aspects of migration systems different from migration networks. Take, for instance, the MST work that fo cuses on mesolevel elements such as migrant networks (e.g., Gurak and Caces 1992), which can be defined as “sets of impersonal ties that connect migrants, former migrants, and nonmigrants to one another through relations of kin ship, friendship, and shared community origin” (Palloni et al. 2001, pp. 1263– 64). As put by DeWaard and Ha (2019), the body of work within MST that focuses on migrant networks draws heavily on the interpersonal nature of migration. In this type of work, factors such as the agency of pioneer migrants and their migration-specific social capital (i.e., resources such as information and connections that migrants acquire through the act of migrating itself) are used to understand when and how certain migratory corridors are established (or not) in a migration system in the first place. This type of work also focuses on how the social capital within migrant networks can sometimes make migration a self-sustaining process by substantially lowering the migrating costs faced by future migrants (Faist 2004; de Haas 2010; Bakewell, de Haas, and Kubal 2012). This study focuses on the analysis of macrolevel migratory structures (e.g., migration networks), not on mesolevel migratory structures (e.g., migrant networks).3

Over the years, Bakewell et al. (Bakewell 2010, 2014; Bakewell et al. 2012, 2016) have provided several important insights regarding the strengths and weaknesses of MST. In particular, Bakewell (2014) called for a “relaunch ing” of MST centered on the careful specification of the feedback mecha nisms through which migratory corridors tend to emerge, decay, or intensify. Bakewell et al. (Bakewell 2014; Bakewell et al. 2016) have made explicit their growing skepticism of the possibility of specifying such mechanisms at the level of migration networks, where the agency of migrants is not di rectly taken into account. This article agrees with Bakewell et al. about the importance of better specifying the mechanisms through which migratory corridors emerge (or not; see also DiMaggio and Garip 2011). Yet, this article also agrees with DeWaard and Ha’s (2019) theoretical and empirical critique of Bakewell’s (2014) call for relaunching MST because of its dismissal of decades of fruitful research on migration networks as an avenue to advance migration theory in general and MST in particular (Zlotnik 1992; Nogle 1994; Jennissen 2007; DeWaard et al. 2012; Fussell et al. 2014; DeWaard, Curtis, and Fussell 2016; Hauer 2017; Windzio et al. 2019). This study aims to advance the empirical and theoretical insights of the abstract systems variant of MST by leveraging well-established advances in social network theory aimed at identifying, and testing, the role of generative network mechanisms, which can then be fruitfully used to explain the emergence and structural dy namics of migration networks. This will help advance the abstract systems variant of MST beyond Bakewell’s (2014) main critique of this line of work: a typically vague and static specification of the endogenous (or in network theory terms, “local”) mechanisms that can explain the emergence of the dis tinctive empirical patterns exhibited by migration networks.

Delineating MST and the Relevance of Supradyadic Migratory Structures

After Mabogunje’s foundational contribution, a new wave of theoretical and empirical work advancing MST developed during the late 1980s and the early

1990s. On the one hand, this new wave of work attempted to empirically identify and analyze international migration networks with the scarce data at hand at the time (Salt 1992; Zlotnik 1992; Nogle 1994). On the other hand, this new wave of work also attempted to better specify the principles of MST as a theoretical framework to study international migration. This included the development of a typology of “nonpeople linkages” (Fawcett 1989, p. 673) that are deemed key to understand the functioning of migration systems. These linkages, which will be explained in detail below, are considered to be “nonpeople” correlates of the dynamics of migration systems because they are conceptually different from migration networks per se; that is, they go beyond the exchange of people (i.e., migrants) between countries (Fawcett 1989; see also Boyd 1989; Kritz and Zlotnik 1992). Within this new wave of work, Fawcett (1989, p. 672) delineated the following core analytical principles of MST:

A. Migration is a dynamic process unfolding over time;

B. Places (e.g., countries) are connected by different “linkages”;

C. Attention should be given to both stability and mobility in each place in the system;

D. Any given flow should be studied in the context of all other flows in the system;

E. Flows are sensitive to changes in other flows because of their interconnected nature;

F. Comparing places is key to stress “the disparities and imbalances” that are a source of energy (i.e., migratory movements) in the system.

Expanding on MST principle B above, Fawcett (1989) pointed out that the dynamics of migration systems are influenced by three types of (nonpeople) linkages: regulatory linkages, which are related to both geographic proximity between places (DeWaard et al. 2012) and comembership in supranational organizations (e.g., Mercado Común del Sur [MERCOSUR] in the Southern Cone); tangible linkages, which are related to the flow of money and goods between places (Greenwood and McDowell 1991); and relational linkages, which are related to shared culture and history between places (Kim and Cohen 2010).4 Other prominent MST theorists summarized the three sets of linkages proposed by Fawcett (1989) as linkages pertaining to geography, comparative development, and cultural affinity, respectively (Zlotnik 1992). Any rigorous test of MST thus presumes three distinct elements: incorporating time into the analysis (analytical principles A and C), taking into account the role of linkages between places (analytical principle B), and having and analyzing migration flow data (analytical principles D, E, and F). The analyses in this article fulfill all these requirements.

The relational nature of MST as a theoretical framework is most explicit in principles C, D, and E above. On the one hand, the theorization of dyadic or bilateral relations (i.e., ties/flows) between countries is, of course, core not only to MST but to the broader international migration literature, including the literature on migration in the Americas (e.g., Kim and Cohen 2010; Özden et al. 2011; DeWaard et al. 2012; Cerrutti and Parrado 2015). On the other hand, the theorization of supradyadic (chain-like) relations is substantially less common in both the MST literature and the international migration lit erature more broadly (but see Paul 2011). This constitutes a missed opportunity for theorization and research since MST explicitly encourages relational analyses based on the interconnected nature of all migration flows in a given migration system (MST principles D and E). This missed opportunity for theorizing supradyadic migratory patterns seems particularly acute since the prevalence of chain-like migratory patterns is empirically documented inside and outside the MST literature (Segal 1987; McElroy and Albuquerque 1990; Plane, Henrie, and Perry 2005; Durand and Massey 2010; Paul 2011; DeWaard et al. 2016).

Unlike migration theory, and MST in particular, the theorization ofsupradyadic relationships, and more specifically, triadic relationships, has been a core area of research in social network theory at least since the original work of Simmel ([1922] 1955). Network theorists have proved that any social system in which ties have direction (e.g., a network of exchanges such as a migration network) can be formally reduced to a finite set of 16 types of triads (Holland and Leinhardt 1970) and that fundamental emergent (global) prop erties of social systems can be derived (locally) by investigating the distribution of the triads in them (Davis and Leinhardt 1972). In that context, one of the most robust findings in social network analysis is related to the nontrivia implications of shifting the analysis of social relations from dyads to triad (Simmel 1955; Cartwright and Harary 1956; Holland and Leinhardt 1970; Davis and Leinhardt 1972), especially when trying to model hierarchical social systems (Davis 1970; Davis and Leinhardt 1972; Chase 1980; Hallinan and Kubitschek 1988; Papachristos, Hureau, Braga 2013; McFarland et al. 2014; Block 2015; Kitts et al. 2017). There is no a priori reason to expect that migration networks are an exception to this empirical regularity.

Theoretically, a powerful bridge to draw connections between the largely unexplored link between MST and the study of supradyadic (e.g., triadic) migratory structures can be traced back to the original work of Mabogunje (1970). This theoretical connection is palpable when Mabogunje discussed the existence and relevance of “migration subsystems [that] operate to per petuate and reinforce the systematic nature of international flows by encouraging migration along certain pathways, and discouraging it along others” (Mabogunje 1970, p. 4, emphasis added; see also in Bakewell et al. 2012, p. 417).

Empirically, the existence of geographically identifiable “migration subsystems” in the Americas has a long-standing history inside and outside the MST literature, going from studies ofthe North American migration subsystem composed ofthe triad Canada, United States, and Mexico (Massey et al. 1994) to studies of the Southern Cone and the Greater Colombia migration subsystems in southern and northern South America, respectively (Cerrutti and Parrado 2015; see also Zlotnik 1992). Following network theory, a key argument of this study is that the concept of a migration subsystem can be parsimoniously theorized and operationalized by carefully analyzing the simplest, and most fundamental, supradyadic configuration there is, namely, the triad.

In network theory a “triad” is any set of three nodes (e.g., countries). Similarly, a “triangle” is a triad in which all nodes are connected. Two types of triangles are critical here. First, a “cyclic triangle” is a horizontal-like triangle in which migration flows exhibit a circular or nonhierarchical exchange pattern in which each one of the three countries in the triad sends a flow out and receives one back (graphically: ⇔\Leftrightarrow . This structure is known as the 030C triad in the standard classification of Davis and Leinhardt (1972).6 Following Goodreau, Kitts, and Morris (2009), this article distinguishes between process and pattern by the terms cyclic triadic closure and cyclicality, respectively. Using the language of Windzio (2018, p. 20), a cyclic triangle in the context of international migration dynamics connotes a scenario in which there is no structural evidence of an underlaying “hierarchy of attractiveness between countries” because the migratory exchanges among them are balanced in the sense that each country sends and receives one flow. This type of horizontal exchange is sometimes called generalized exchange in the social networks literature.

Second, a “transitive triangle” is a vertical-like triangle in which migration flows exhibit a hierarchical exchange pattern as follows: the least attractive country in the triangle sends two flows out and receives none back, the most attractive country sends no flows out and receives two back, and the country with middle attractiveness sends one flow out and gets one back (graphically: ↷\curvearrowright . This structure is known as the 030T triad. Again, this article makes a distinction between process and pattern by using the terms “transitive triadic closure” and “transitivity,” respectively. Building on this vocabulary, migratory chains are defined here as any (supradyadic) migratory pattern between three or more countries and the migration flows among them.7 As explained earlier, following a long-standing tradition of network theory, this study focuses on migratory chains among triads as a principled way to effectively and parsimoniously model the supradyadic structure of migration systems and, more specifically, of the migration subsystems therein.

Drivers of Migratory Chains: From Microfactors to Macropatterns

Chain-like migratory patterns have been reported in the migration literature for over a century. In what is typically considered a foundational piece of the modern study of human migration, Ravenstein (1885) explicitly mentioned the existence of stepwise (i.e., chain-like) migratory patterns driven by the relative attractiveness of places in the British Islands, where: “The inhabi tants of the country immediately surrounding a town of rapid growth, flock into it; the gaps thus left in the rural population are filled up by migrants from more remote districts, until the attractive force of one of our rapidly growing cities makes its influence felt, step by step, to the most remote corner of the kingdom” (p. 199, emphasis added).

The idea of a hierarchy of attractiveness between places has been repeatedly suggested as a key reason why migratory chains might develop (Ravenstein 1885; Segal 1987; McElroy and Albuquerque 1990; Plane et al. 2005; Durand and Massey 2010; Paul 2011; Windzio 2018). This idea directly connects with MST analytical principle F, according to which comparing the “disparities and imbalances” between all places in a migration system (e.g., the relative attractiveness of countries) is necessary to understand when migration flows emerge (Fawcett 1989). Windzio (2018) further argues that the notion of a hierarchy of attractiveness suggests that international migration flows will tend to be structured in a vertical way, that is, following transitive patterns. In short, the idea of a hierarchy of attractiveness represents a system-level expectation regarding the tendency of supradyadic (e.g., triadic) migratory exchanges ob served in migration systems to be organized as transitive structures.

Why specifically are migratory chains expected to be transitive? A key mechanism put forward here is based on the fact that, at the aggregate level, migration flows typically imply the relocation of valuable (economic, demographic, cultural, etc.) resources. Indeed, robust evidence from the Americas suggests that participating in the exchange of migrant populations can bring important country-level benefits to the host society in terms of (a) economic gains, for example, decreasing prices of immigrant-intensive services such as gardening or babysitting, increasing levels ofpurchasing power among high skilled native workers, higher wages for college- and non-college-educated natives, or increasing levels in productivity (Cortes 2008; Peri 2012; Peri, Shih, and Sparber 2015); (b) demographic gains, for example, the rejuvenation of the population due to migrants’ relatively high fertility rate when compared to that of natives (Jonsson and Rendall 2004); or (c) cultural gains, for example, a “transition to diversity” as the host society becomes more heterogeneous in ethnoracial and religious terms (Alba and Barbosa 2016).8 Thinking about migration flows as vehicles for the relocation of valuable resources is important because, in competitive contexts, the transferring of resources from a sending unit to a receiving unit is an act of deference (Kitts et al. 2017). Two related ideas are worth highlighting. First, it is known that status hierarchies (e.g., a hierarchy of attractiveness) emerge from the ability of actors to amass acts of deference (Sauder, Lynn, and Podolny 2012). Second, evidence sug gests that the global “migration market” (Czaika and de Haas 2015, p. 316) did become highly competitive during the years under analysis here since it was progressively dominated by an increasingly small set ofimmigration countries receiving migrants from an increasingly large pool of emigration countries (Czaika and de Haas 2015). Given the deferential and competitive nature of the migratory exchanges described above, migratory chains can thus be expected to be patterned as structures in which transitive (i.e., hierarchical) ex change patterns should be observed above chance levels, and, concomitantly, intransitive (i.e., nonhierarchical) exchange patterns should be observed below chance levels.

As explained in detail below, while these status-driven dynamics are presumed to be constantly at play in migration systems, there are also individual (e.g., migrants’ preferences), country (e.g., migration policies), and exogenous (e.g., natural disasters) factors that undeniably and dynamically shape the concrete configuration of the hierarchy of attractiveness in a migration system at any point in time (e.g., which specific countries are considered prime destinations). At the individual level, Paul (2011) provides rich qualitative evidence based on the migration ofFilipino workers showing how chain-like migratory patterns can emerge when migrants move across borders because of their perceived destination hierarchy between places.9 Specifically, Paul shows that the migrants she studies explicitly “attempt to move up a personally and socially constructed destination hierarchy” (p. 1864, emphasis added). This allows Paul to make sense of empirical chain-like migratory patterns, such as what she labels “Route B” in her study (p. 1868), a sequential migratory pattern described by migratory movements along the triad the Philippines (minimally attractive country) → Saudi Arabia (mildly attractive country) → Italy (highly attractive country).

Systematic differences and changes in the preferences (i.e., perceived des tination hierarchy) of migrants through their life course might help explain the emergence and, perhaps more importantly, the heterogeneity of migratory chains. For example, in a contemporary test of Ravenstein’s (1885) original ideas, Plane et al. (2005) provide strong evidence showing that relatively young internal migrants in the United States seek to move up across a seven-step urban hierarchy—where megametropolitan areas of more than 2.5 million people are at the top of the hierarchy—whereas, at the same time, relatively old migrants tend to move down this same urban hierarchy, specially to coun ties with no urban cluster. Life course factors might also help explain why emi gration from countries generally considered to be at the top of the hierarchy of attractiveness in a given international migration system emerge. Take, for instance, the sizable number of relatively old, retired or close to retirement migrants from the United States and Canada who actively seek to live more comfortable lives in much less expensive societies in the Americas like Mex ico (Croucher 2009), Ecuador (Hayes 2014), the Bahamas (McElroy and Al buquerque 1990), or Panama (Benson 2013). More generally, the relational, tangible, and regulatory linkages explained in detail earlier can also create or reinforce differences in the perceived destination hierarchy of concrete migrants—for example, by making places more accessible or by signaling dif ferences in the quality of life between places (again, see MST principle F).

Quasi-experimental evidence from Latin America also shows how countries can be chosen as intermediary destinations in chain-like migratory movements when opportunities suddenly open up at the country level. More specifi cally, Ecuador’s policy of visa freedom between 2008 and 2010 was capitalized by migrants from inside and outside the Americas such as migrants from Haiti (minimally attractive country) who made Ecuador (mildly attractive country) a stepping-stone to later gaining entry into Brazil (highly attractive country; Freier and Holloway 2019). A relatively similar situation took place in the 1980s when some of the so-called Mariel migrants from Cuba (minimally attractive country) migrated, under the auspices of Costa Rican

President Rodrigo Carazo, to Costa Rica (mildly attractive country) in order to ultimately emigrate as political refugees to the United States (highly attractive country; Fernández and Narváez 1987). These examples suggest that individuallevel perceived destination hierarchies are constrained and enabled by policies developed in both sending and receiving countries (Zolberg 1999; Waldinger 2015). In general, it is known that immigration policies in receiving countries are increasingly important to understand migration flows within and outside the Americas (Hollifield, Martin, and Orrenius 2014), even though sometimes such policies can have largely unanticipated consequences—and sometimes plainly opposite results—when compared to what policy makers intend on paper (Massey and Pren 2012).

Beyond migrants’ decisions, preferences, and personal circumstances, work in the MST tradition has also shown the potential for the emergence of chain like (internal) migratory patterns between counties in the United States in response to exogenous shocks affecting migration systems such as natural disasters like Hurricane Katrina (DeWaard et al. 2016). In this context, the evidence suggests that people with the resources to emigrate from the affected area slowly return to it. Yet, they did not seem to do it directly but through intermediary counties in a process labeled as “stage migration” (DeWaard et al. 2016).

In sum, the aggregate place-to-place migration flows that comprise a given migration network, and the migratory chains in it, are the result of discrete migration decisions made by individuals at a given point in time (Bakewell 2014). These individuals make their decisions and develop their migratory strategies based on their preferences (e.g., their perceived destination hierarchy). These strategies and preferences are constrained and enabled not only by migrants’ individual changing characteristics and circumstances (e.g., their stage in the life course); they are also constrained and enabled by larger structures such as migration policies (e.g., visa regimes), differences in costs of living between places (i.e., tangible linkages), or changes in the environment of the system (e.g., natural disasters). Seeking to advance the abstract systems variant of MST does mean that the analyses in this article focus on describing and analyzing aggregate-level structural dynamics only (e.g., the emergence and structure of migratory chains), which under no circumstance means that the micro- and mesolevel substrate of such dynamics (e.g., perceived destination hierarchy, migration policies) is deemed immaterial in this study or in MST more generally.

Migratory Chains in the Americas

Empirically, some migratory chains in the Americas are known to originate in the core of the migration system, where the countries with the highest overall levels of attractiveness are located (i.e., the United States or Canada in the case of the Americas; Zlotnik 1992). These migratory chains can be illustrated by the well-documented chain-like migratory pattern known as relay migration (Segal 1987; McElroy and Albuquerque 1990; Durand and Massey 2010). Probably the best example ofa relay migration pattern in the Americas is Durand and Massey’s (2010, pp. 40–41) account of the labor migration stream that connected the United States to Haiti during the years under anal ysis in this article:

The process began at the upper part of the labor ladder, in the United States, and resulted from deliberate labor recruitment. After World War II, labor markets in the northeastern United States required new workers to replace aging European immigrants who had arrived earlier in the century. . . . The departure of [Puerto Rican] workers to the United States from rural areas created job openings that were then filled by immigrants from the Dominican Republic, a short boat ride away, who arrived in significant numbers to cut sugar cane and harvest coffee. In this case there was no active labor recruitment because news about the availability of seasonal jobs filtered back to the neighboring island, where salaries were lower, initiating voluntary undocumented migration. In turn, rural areas of the Dominican Republic began to experience a shortage of workers owing to the departure of workers for Puerto Rico as well as the capital city and, after 1965, the United States. The shortage of workers in the coffee and sugar industries was then met by recruiting workers from Haiti, the poorest country in the Western Hemisphere, whose inhabitants viewed seasonal labo in the Dominican Republic as an economic opportunity.

The mobility of international labor migrants described above can be analyzed through the notion of vacancy chains (White 1970), understood as “the sequence of moves a vacancy makes from initial entry into the system to final termination” (Chase 1991, p. 135). Two key scope conditions of vacancy chains— which are directly related to their hierarchical nature—are that individuals typically want or need newer or “better” resource units (p. 135; e.g., jobs) and that these “better” units should be available from time to time. In the eyes of migrants, as suggested by Durand and Massey’s (2010) account, these are better jobs in the sense that they represent employment opportunities abroad that will likely translate into important income gains for migrants. Vacancy chains are “top down” by definition since they emerge when relatively attractive or better resource units become available. The hierarchy of attractiveness logic of the resulting migratory chains is simple: from the point of view of individ uals occupying mildly attractive resource units (e.g., workers in the Dominican Republic, DR), they can be expected to be drawn to the newly available highly attractive resource units (e.g., jobs in Puerto Rico, PR). Once the individuals who used to occupy mildly attractive resource units leave them to occupy new highly attractive resource units (DR → PR), individuals occupying minimally attractive resource units (e.g., workers in Haiti, HI) can be expected to be drawn to the newly vacant mildly attractive resource units (e.g., HI → DR). A migratory chain would thus emerge in this context ([DR → PR], [HI → DR]; graphically: ). This structural pattern is known as the 021C triad.

Critically, the hierarchy of attractiveness between places not only offers a rationale for the emergence of migratory chains; it also provides a plausible explanation for their (triadic) closure, that is, for the emergence of supradyadic migration subsystems structurally shaped as triangles. This process involves two main tendencies. On the one hand, the hierarchy of attractiveness in the particular migration subsystem analyzed above (i.e., [DR → PR], [HI → DR], a subsystem structured as a 021C triad) entails that individuals occupying minimally attractive resource units in Haiti are likely to be drawn to any available highly attractive resource units in Puerto Rico, which would increase the likelihood of observing a transitive triangle or 030T triad ([DR → PR], [HI → DR], [HI → PR]). On the other hand, the hierarchy of attractiveness in this particular migration subsystem also entails that individuals in highly attractive resource units in Puerto Rico are unlikely to be drawn to the new minimally attractive resource units left open by the Haitian workers who moved to the Dominican Republic, which would decrease the likelihood of observing a cyclic triangle or 030C triad ([DR → PR], [HI → DR], [PR → HI]). It should be noted that applying the lenses of vacancy chains to the analysis of labor migration streams is compatible with the conceptualization of migration flows as signals of deference derived from the relocation of valuable resources (workers) from the sending country to the receiving country.

This study argues that (triadic) closure patterns will also emerge in “bottom up” migratory chains, that is, migratory chains that originate outside the core of the migration system. An example of this is related to the migratory movements following political or economic crises taking place in the periphery of the system, where countries with low overall levels of attractiveness tend to be located. Take the example of the migration subsystem between El Salvador (SV), Belize (BZ), and the United States ([SV → BZ], [BZ → US]; again, a subsystem structured as a 021C triad). Here, the civil war that started in the 1970s in El Salvador meant that the economically driven middle-class Salvadorian migrants that had been emigrating in previous years to Belize were joined by increasingly large numbers of politically driven migrants (Asoman 1982; Everitt 1984). This flow of emigrants from El Salvador to Belize hap pened in the context of a relatively large flow of Belizeans going to the United States (Stavrakis and Marshall 1978; Everitt 1984). Over time, however, the economic and political forces behind the existence of this migratory chain became stronger. On the one hand, this resulted in a “Latinization” of Belize (Everitt 1984) as evidenced by the fact that the formerly English-speaking Belize became a country in which around 50% of the population spoke Span ish as their mother tongue by the year 2000 (Moreno and Otero 2006).10 On the other hand, the large number of Central Americans in Belize in and of itself became a strong push factor for Belizeans to leave for the United States (Everitt 1984; Moberg 1997; Cunin and Hoffmann 2013), thus reinforcing the bottom-up nature of the migration subsystem between El Salvador, Belize, and the United States.11

As with the top-down relay migration pattern described earlier, bottomup migratory chains are also expected to exhibit hierarchical (triadic) closure tendencies since the hierarchy of attractiveness between countries is assumed to be a force that shapes migratory chains across the migration system, irrespective of where in the system the chains initiate (i.e., in the core or the pe riphery of the migration system). Here, therefore, it is expected that migratory movements are likely to take place from El Salvador (minimally attractive country) to the United States (highly attractive country), thus increasing the chances that this particular migration subsystem will be structured as a transitive triangle or 030T triad ([SV → BZ], [BZ → US], [SV → US]). Sim ilarly, it is expected that migratory movements are unlikely to take place from the United States (highly attractive country) to El Salvador (minimally at tractive country), thus reducing the chances that this particular migration subsystem will be structured as a cyclic triangle or 030C triad ([SV → BZ], [BZ → US], [US → SV]).

HYPOTHESES

This article is designed to study one of the two key analytical dimensions of migration networks, that is, the diversity of flows, as opposed to the intensity of flows (Bell et al. 2002; Czaika and de Haas 2014; DeWaard and Ha 2019). Diversity maps the structure of migration networks in terms of which countries do and do not exchange migrants. In network theory terms, this means modeling migration networks as binary directed graphs (see fig. 1A). In tensity maps the structure of migration networks in terms of the size or volume of the migratory exchanges between countries. In network theory terms, this means modeling migration networks as weighted directed graphs (see fig. 1B).

Using MST as their guiding framework, DeWaard and Ha (2019) employ European flow data to formally show how diversity and intensity are analyt ically different dimensions that uniquely contribute to explaining how migration networks work (see also Czaika and de Haas 2014). To study the diversity described by supradyadic structures in migration systems, the analysis here proposes to focus on two fundamental triadic closure processes observable in migratory chains. Cyclic triadic closure denotes a (nondeferential) closure process in which an “open” triple—that is, a triangle that lacks one of its sides—becomes a cyclic triangle or 030C triad. Still following the triad census notation, only one open triple can give rise to a cyclic triangle, the 021C triad. Analysis of cyclic triadic closure should control for other endogenous (e.g., dyadic reciprocity) and exogenous (e.g., geography) network influences. For instance, assume a hypothetical migratory chain between Colombia (CO), Peru (PE), and Venezuela (VE) is initially structured as a 021C triad: [PE → CO], [CO → VE]. Then, a new tie/flow from Venezuela to Peru [VE → PE] would be a potential correlate of network endogenous and exogenous changes. On the one hand, the new [VE → PE] flow would change the (endogenous) network structure of the triad in different ways, such as increasing the count of observed cyclic triangles or 030C triads ([PE → CO], [CO → VE], [VE → PE]) or the number of unreciprocated or one-way dyads ([VE → PE], [PE no tie to VE]). On the other hand, the [VE → PE] flow would also increase the count of within-category triangles along key (exogenous) dimensions such as official language or geographic subregion since these are all Spanish speaking countries located in the Andean Region in South America. A correct estimation of each generative mechanism (cyclic triadic closure, dyadic reciprocity, assortative mixing by geographic region or language) must therefore be controlled for the other (Hunter et al. 2008; Goodreau et al. 2009; Cranmer and Desmarais 2011). The methods used in this article are specifically de signed to take these dependencies into account.

Since the foundational study on hierarchy and triads by Davis (1970), network theorists have known that the combination of both transitivity and (anti)cyclicality is a robust structural signal that (local) hierarchy—as opposed to generalized exchange—underlies the emergence of a given (global) social network (Davis 1970; Robins 2015; Kitts et al. 2017; Windzio 2018), a tendency sometimes referred to as “hierarchization” (Pál et al. 2015, p. 815). In that context, the following hypotheses are proposed to assess the (supradyadic) hi erarchical closure tendencies of migration systems through the often-neglected angle of the (triadic) migratory chains in them:

HYPOTHESIS 1a.—Migratory chains mapping the diversity ofinternational migrationflows should exhibit a positive tendency toward transitive triadic closure.

HYPOTHESIS 1b.—Migratory chains mapping the diversity ofinternational migration flows should exhibit a negative tendency toward cyclic triadic closure.

Examples of the hierarchical nature of chain-like migratory patterns exist in the literature: from the emergence of global care chains, to the rise of stepwise, stage, or relay migratory chains (Durand and Massey 2010; Hondagneu-Sotelo 2011; Paul 2011; Parreñas 2012; DeWaard et al. 2016). The current study offers a unique opportunity to explore key triadic closure patterns in all migratory chains in the Western Hemisphere across four decades. Critically, without robust empirical evidence that the tendencies in hypotheses 1a and 1b exist, the very same notion that migration theories should systematically consider supradyadic (e.g., triadic) processes may be ignored, as it indeed has been. For instance, Fagiolo and Mastrorillo (2013) report that modeling migration networks does not require supradyadic elements, only dyadic ones (e.g., shared geographic region or shared official language between pairs of countries). Similarly, Nogle (1994) advanced an empirical test of MST using network theories and tools, yet they did not explicitly model relational dependencies beyond dyads.

This study also argues that as the analysis of migration networks focuses on the diversity of the largest flows in the system, that is, on the migration networks that map which countries are (and are not) connected by large migratory exchanges, the hierarchical configuration ofmigratory chains should be more palpable. This prediction is indirectly derived from—and fully consistent with—MST since proponents of this theoretical framework expect the largest flows in a given migration system to be the ones more likely to be directed to core countries (Fawcett 1989; Zlotnik 1992; see also DeWaard et al. 2012; Windzio et al. 2019).

Indeed, in their classic overview of migration theories, Massey et al. mentioned that from the perspective of MST: “An international migration system generally includes a core receiving region, which may be a country or group of countries, and a set of specific sending countries linked to it by unusually largeflows of immigrants” (1993, p. 454, emphasis added; see also Kritz and

Zlotnik 1992). This expectation suggests that migratory chains composed ofparticularly large flows would disproportionately have both core (i.e., highly attractive) countries positioned as recipients of flows and noncore (i.e., minimally or mildly attractive) countries positioned as the senders of flows. Therefore, by virtue of the hierarchy of attractiveness between places, migration networks that only map relatively large migratory exchanges should exhibit stronger hierarchical closure tendencies than the ones observed under hypotheses 1a and 1b. This should be the case because these two initial hypotheses (1a and 1b) are in fact agnostic about the type of countries (i.e., core vs. noncore countries) present in a given migratory chain. All this should be true above and beyond factors that are known to facilitate the existence of large flows between countries such as geographic or cultural proximity (Karemera, Oguledo, and Davis 2000; Kim and Cohen 2010). A second set of hypotheses is thus proposed:

HYPOTHESIS 2a.—The positive tendency toward transitive triadic closure exhibited by migratory chains should be stronger in migration networks tha map the diversity of relatively largeflows.

HYPOTHESIS 2b.—The negative tendency toward cyclic triadic closure exhibited by migratory chains should be stronger in migration networks tha map the diversity of relatively largeflows

The hypotheses proposed so far engage with, and extend, previous find ings reported in the MST literature. As mentioned above, Nogle (1994) developed an empirical test of MST using network theories and tools focusing on Europe but did not explicitly model supradyadic structures. DeWaard et al. (2012) and DeWaard and Ha (2019) recently advanced important empirical tests of MST also focusing on Europe, but they did not explicitly model the network structure of the system. Windzio et al. (2019) provide a remarkable cross-sectional test of MST in which network processes were explicitly modeled using European flow data. Windzio et al. (2019) do provide important indirect evidence supporting the hypotheses introduced above regarding the diversity of flows. However, the evidence provided by Windzio et al. (2019) is cross-sectional, and it is circumscribed to Europe. Moreover, and perhaps more importantly, Windzio et al. only studied diversity among the largest migration flows in the (European) system and did not include an explicit cyclic closure measure (2019, table 1). Windzio (2018) also models diversity among large migratory corridors only. Moreover, the work in Windzio (2018) uses stock (not flow) data, and it is not meant to be a test of MST.

Finally, focusing specifically on transitive migratory chains, this article argues that a hierarchy in structural attractiveness can be measured via transitive triangles as follows: “highly attractive structural positions” are those occupied by countries that receive two inflows and send no outflows, “mildly attractive structural positions” are those occupied by countries that receive one inflow and send one outflow, and “minimally attractive structural positions” are those occupied by countries that receive no inflows and send two outflows (see fig. 2). Following the hierarchy of attractiveness argument, here it is posited that structural attractiveness, that is, the specific positions countries tend to occupy in transitive migratory chains, is not random but patterned along an underlaying hierarchy in substantive attractiveness measurable in terms of countries’ overall levels of prosperity and well-being.

The role of substantive inequalities between places has been explicitly highlighted by classic MST theorists as a key driver behind the emergence of migration flows (Mabogunje 1970; Fawcett 1989). In the case of the Americas in particular, the literature suggests that increasing levels of inequality in economic well-being between countries in the Americas in general, and within Latin America in particular, stimulated migration across the continent during the years under analysis here (Pellegrino 1995, 2003; Durand and Massey 2010; Cerrutti and Parrado 2015). Assuming that substantive inequalities between countries are indeed predictive of the emergence of both migration flows and migratory chains, and following the argument in hypotheses 2a and 2b regarding how hierarchical tendencies should be more palpable among large migratory corridors, here it is argued that countries’ level of substantive attractiveness would map onto countries’ level of structural attractiveness according to the following final hypothesis:

HYPOTHESIS 3.—Countries’ well-being (substantive attractiveness) should be positively associated with the number of large inflows they exhibit in transitive migratory chains (structural attractiveness).

DATA AND METHODS

Estimation of Migration Flows

The main data set used here consists of four directed (socio)matrices that contain estimated migration flows between 192 countries around the globe across four decades: 1960s, 1970s, 1980s, and 1990s. The names of the 192 countries can be found in Leal, Malhotra, and Misra (2019). The raw data came from the World Bank Global Bilateral Migrant Stock Database (Öz den et al. 2011). These data detail the origin and number of immigrants living in a given destination country using official demographic data. This i the only truly global migrant stock data set that covers the period under analysis in this article (Özden et al. 2011; Czaika and de Haas 2014). These data allowed for the use of the flows-from-stocks (FFS) method—a method recently developed, and empirically validated, by formal demographer Guy Abel—which allows one to estimate flow-like data from stock data (Abel 2013, 2018; Abel and Sander 2014). This method, implemented in the migest R package version 1.7.4 (Abel 2016), produces “state-of-the-art estimates” that led to the “first complete global estimates of bilateral migration flows” (Azose and Raftery 2019, p. 116). The FFS method has been successfully used previously to estimate international migration flows from stock data in order to conduct empirical tests of MST in Europe (DeWaard and Ha 2019). All the data sources and code to replicate the estimation of flows conducted here can be found in Leal et al. (2019) and in table O.1.1² Formal details about the estimation procedure and the assumptions therein can be found in appendix B. Thorough discussions of the strengths and weaknesses of the FFS method and the Global Bilateral Migrant Stock Database can be found elsewhere (Abel 2013, 2018; Czaika and de Haas 2014; Azose and Raftery 2019; DeWaard and Ha 2019).

Once global migration flows were estimated, only flows in the Americas were analyzed. Although all countries can be considered part of a global migration system, focusing on the Americas is warranted because the vast majority of Latin American migrants migrate within the Americas (Pellegrino 1995; Cohen 2010; Spörlein 2015). A total of 38 countries and dependent territories had flow data, which represents all cases in the Americas. Unfortunately, complete data on gross domestic product (GDP) per capita and polit ical conflict, which are key MST covariates, were available for only 32 out of these 38 countries. Measures

Dependent variables.—Given that the migration networks produced by the FFS method naturally reflect the intensity or size of flows between countries, to test hypotheses 1a and 1b any flow from country i to countryj at time t with size 5 0 was kept as a 0 (i.e., as a nonexisting flow), while all present flows, that is, flows of size ≥ 1, were coded 1 (i.e., as existing flows). This allows anal ysis of the diversity of the system (i.e., which countries exchange migrants) regardless of the size of the existing flows. To test hypotheses 2a and 2b, the four original migration networks produced by the FFS method were binarized again. This time the binarization was based on a set of gradually increasing thresholds that capture flows of growing size, thereby allowing for the study of the diversity of flows while focusing on the largest, most relevant, migratory corridors in the system—a strategy required to test hypotheses 2a and 2b and supported by the broader MST literature. This article uses a comprehensive set of robustness checks that firmly suggests the results reported here regarding hypotheses 2a and 2b are not a mere by-product of a particular binarization value. The robustness checks are based on:

  1. Two different binarization rules (more on this below);

  2. Three different threshold values within each binarization rule;

  3. Two different methods to estimate flows from stock data, the FFS method described earlier, and a “pseudo-Bayes” method proposed by Azose and Raftery (2019, p. 116).

As mentioned in item a above, two different binarization rules were used to transform migration flows into binary ties to test hypotheses 2a and 2b. The first rule coded as 1, as an existing flow, all migration flows between a given origin country (i) and its top 15 destination countries (j) at time t (0 was assigned otherwise). Zlotnik (1992) suggested that migration systems could indeed be identified using outflows (see Windzio et al. [2019] for a practical application). Of course, the selection of this threshold is somewhat arbitrary, and thus other threshold values were used in sensitivity analyses. More specifically, increasing or decreasing the threshold value by a third (±5 top destination countries) produced robust model estimates, while making it possible to study migration networks that contain between 40% and 80% of all existing flows. Information about the graphical representation of the effect of these three different threshold values (i.e., top 10, top 15, and top 20 destination countries) on the number of flows present in the migration networks under analysis can be found in the online appendix.

To further evaluate the robustness of the results, a different binarization rule was used. This rule was purely based on absolute flow intensity or size irrespective of the particular sending and receiving countries involved in a given flow. Three different thresholds were used in the context of the second binarization rule: (1) all flows comprising at least 10 migrants were coded 1 (0 was assigned otherwise), (2) all flows comprising at least 25 migrants were coded 1 (0 was assigned otherwise), and (3) all flows comprising at least 50 migrants were coded 1 (0 was assigned otherwise). Finally, a completely different set of migration flow estimates produced by Azose and Raftery (2019; see app. B for details) was also analyzed using this second binarization rule. These estimates cover the period 1990–2015 by five-year intervals, for a total of five waves (1990–95, 1995–2000, 2000–5, 2005–10, 2010–15). All robustness checks, which are discussed in the results section, produce consistent findings.

Key endogenous dependencies.—As explained earlier, in order to measure hierarchical relational patterns at the level of triads, two variables were used: (1) transitive triangles, which captures the number of 030T triads in the network (see table 1, panel B, for details), and (2) cyclic triangles, which measures the number of 030C triads in the network (see table 1, panel A, for details).

Endogenous controls.—(3) Mutual dyads capture the number of pairs of countries i and j for which the following relational pattern holds: [i →j], [j → i]. The existence of mutuality in migration flows in the Americas is doc umented in the literature. For instance, it is known that Mexicans tend to migrate to the United States in large numbers (Garip 2016) and that Mexico is also a top destination for people from the United States (Croucher 2009). The same goes for cases like Venezuela and Colombia (Pellegrino 1992, 2003) or Brazil and Paraguay (Rochcau 1983). Mutual dyads have also been used as an endogenous network control inside and outside the MST literature (Desmarais and Cranmer 2012; Windzio et al. 2019). (4) Reciprocation captures whether a flow from country i to country j leads to a flow from country j to country i in the next time period ([i→j]timet,[j→i]timet+1).( \left[ i \longrightarrow j \right] _ { \mathrm { t i m e } ~ t } , ~ [ j \longrightarrow i ] _ { \mathrm { t i m e } ~ t + 1 } ) . . Kitts et al. (2017) have shown that status hierarchies between aggregate actors (e.g., organizations) emerge not only through triadic closure processes but also through (dyadic) reciprocation. This suggests the importance of accounting for reciprocation when modeling hierarchical social systems. (5) Dyadic stability is a “memory term” that captures the number of dyads that are stable (persistent flows and persistent nonflows) between two consecutive time periods. This term aims to account for a well-known regularity in networks research, the inertia or persistence of ties (Gulati and Gargiulo 1999; Rivera, Soderstrom, and Uzzi 2010). In a nutshell, this literature shows that a current tie is likely to exist if it existed in the past. In the migration literature, the notion of cumulative causation (Massey 1990) is closely related to the idea of tie inertia since cumulative causation highlights the tendency of flows to persist over time once they are established (for a critical perspective on cumulative causation, see Faist [2004]). (6) In-degree popularity measures the tendency of a given country to receive inflows as a function of the inflows it already has; it is used to control for inequalities in the distribution of inflows (i.e., in degree). In the literature, this term is also known as attractiveness by in de gree (Snijders, van de Bunt, and Steglich 2010). Effectively, this term represents success-breeds-success dynamics in which popular countries (i.e., countries with a large number of inflows) tend to receive more inflows as a function of their high popularity (i.e., high in degree). (7) Out-degree popularity measures the tendency of a given country to receive inflows as a function of its outflows; it is used to control for inequalities in the distribution of outflows (i.e., out degree). This term is also known as attractiveness by out degree (Snijders et al. 2010). It represents the mirror image of in-degree popularity in the sense that it measures the tendency of a given country to receive more inflows (i.e., to be popular) because of its high out degree.14 (8) Absolute difference in out degree captures homophily based on outflows, that is, the tendency for two countries to be connected as a function of how similar their out degrees are. This term is especially useful when testing hypotheses 2a and 2b given that, by definition, a binarization rule based on top destination countries puts a cap on countries’ out degree (e.g., top 15 destination countries).

Finally, MST theory also mentions tangible linkages. As DeWaard et al. (2012, p. 1324) put it, “these [i.e., tangible linkages] include features of rel ative economic well-being and standards of living in sending and receiving countries,” which is why DeWaard et al. operationalized tangible linkages using data on GDP per capita (see also Windzio et al. 2019).16 In this context, to proxy well-being and quality of life, information about GDP per capita and political instability and conflict was used as follows: (11) income in de gree captures the main effect of income per capita on in degree (i.e., inflows).17 Here, the Adjusted Income per Capita Index developed by Padros de la Es cosura (2015) was used as the data source. For instance, in the 1980s there were only 3 cyclic triangles while there were 338 transitive triangles. To better understand these numbers, it is useful to note that a perfect linear hierarchy is a pecking order in which the topmost node in the hierarchy receives ties (e.g., flows) from all other nodes and sends no ties back, the second topmost node in the hierarchy receives ties from all but the topmost node (and only sends a tie to the topmost node), and so forth. In that context, a “tournament” (Holland and Leinhardt 1970; Wasserman and Faust 1994) is a network structure in which each dyad is connected by only one asymmetric or one-way tie that goes from the lower status actor in the dyad to the higher status actor in the dyad. Since cyclic triangles and transitive triangles are the only triples in the triad census in which all dyads are asymmetric, that also makes them the only two types of triads needed to exhaustively analyze the structure of tournaments between triples in a given social system (Davis 1970). Formal and simulation work in network theory has shown that tournaments in random networks can be expected to be transitive triangles 75% of the time and cyclic triangles 25% of the time (Rapoport 1949; Davis 1970; Chase 1980).20 A network is thus said to be closer to a perfect linear hierarchy, the more the percentage of transitive triangles is above the expected value of 75% (Davis 1970).

To provide evidence in favor of the existence (or not) of a perfect linear hierarchy in the migration networks under analysis, the bottom panel in table 2 reports counts of transitive and cyclic triangles—as well as of all the relevant triads: 021U, 021C, and 021D—for 500 random simulated networks per decade, holding constant the number of nodes (countries) and ties (flows), as well as the proportion of null, symmetric, and asymmetric dyads in the ob served migration network in each decade. Results show that the random networks that emerge from this simulation exercise do reflect the distribution of transitive and cyclic triangles expected by chance. For instance, the simulation show that there are, on average, 173.15 transitive triangles and 57.60 cyclic triangles in the random version of the 1980s migration network. This means that, on average, in random networks that share key structural properties with the 1980s observed migration network (i.e., size; number of ties; proportion of null, symmetric, and asymmetric dyads), 75.04% of the tournaments are transitive and 24.96% are cyclic. Similar results are true for all decades under analysis.

The situation is radically different in the empirically observed migration networks. Take, again, the 1980s migration network as an example. In this network, 99.12% of the tournaments are transitive and 0.88% are cyclic, which is a large deviation from the values expected by chance. In general, for each one of the decades under analysis, the transitive triangles represent at least 94.40% of all triadic tournaments (i.e., of the sum of transitive and cyclic triangles). This evidence strongly conforms to the main intuition be hind this article since it suggests that the empirical migration networks under analysis are (nearly) perfect linear hierarchies at the level of triads. This implies the existence of nonrandom and highly deferential relational processes operating within the migratory chains observed in the Americas.

The results in table 3 thus strongly suggest that local hierarchy is a blueprint of the network structure of (triadic) migratory chains in the Americas. These effects are not a mere nuance. They are empirically robust and also make sense theoretically given what it is known about the highly unequal political economy of migratory exchanges (Fawcett 1989; Massey et al. 1993; Misra, Woodring, and Merz 2006; Paul 2011; DeWaard et al. 2012; Bakewell 2014; Windzio et al. 2019). These results also make sense in the context of broader literatures that have shown the hierarchical nature of exchanges between countries, within and outside the Americas, along an ample range ofdimensions beyond migratory exchanges per se, such as military interventions, diplomatic relations, treaty memberships, trade relations, and associations through intergovernmental organizations (Frank 1969; Cardoso and Faletto 1979; Snyder and Kick 1979; Alderson and Beckfield 2004; Beckfield 2010;2 0 1 0 ; Escobar 2011). Here it is important to note that the structural positions the United States and Canada occupy in the transitive migratory chains in the Americas clearly suggest the existence of a highly stratified pecking order. The historical evidence discussed so far suggests how the substantive attractiveness of countries (e.g., economic and political stability) is linked to the structural attractiveness of countries as captured by their positions in transitive migratory chains. CONCLUSION

A necessary step to further develop MST, and the abstract systems variant of MST more specifically, is to better specify the endogenous (or in MST terms, “feedback”) mechanisms through which migration flows in a migration system emerge, intensify, or dissipate (Bakewell 2014; Bakewell et al. 2016). Social network theory is equipped to address this challenge because of its core orientation toward theorizing and modeling the endogenous (or in networks terms, “local”) mechanisms through which relational structures emerge and change. This article is an effort to explain how network closure is useful to understanding the structure of triadic migration subsystems; subsystems in which certain chain-like migratory patterns are systematically encouraged (transitive migratory chains), while others are systematically discouraged (cyclic migratory chains). The combination of (anti)cyclicality and transitivity has been long recognized in network theory as a signature of a hierarchical social system.

MST conceives the structure of migration flows as hierarchically and geo graphically patterned. On the one hand, going back to the foundational work of Akin Mabogunje, a rich MST tradition focuses on studying migration systems as adaptive structures that are ultimately expressed geographically. Thi study reports evidence of the geographic identifiability of migration networks. On the other hand, the hierarchical organization of flows has also been docu mented in the MST literature, although not nearly as comprehensively as their geographic identifiability has. Indeed, a detailed analysis of how the hierarchical organization of flows is expressed through migratory chains is new to the MST literature.

To be sure, the broader migration literature has pointed out the existence of hierarchical chain-like migratory patterns such as stepwise or stage migration, relay migration, or global care chains. However, the lack ofboth comprehensive longitudinal flow data and appropriate modeling tools has been a major impediment to test for the endogenous mechanisms behind the emergence of (hierarchical) network patterns in migratory chains. This article starts filling this gap by theorizing and modeling triadic closure patterns in migratory chains in the Western hemisphere. This is also important because recent network-based MST analyses of the structure of migration flows have focused on the Global North only.

Theoretically, this article puts forward the idea that triadic closure in migration networks is fueled, among other things, by a hierarchy of attractiveness between localities. This conjecture goes back to Ernst G. Ravenstein’s foundational studies and has found some empirical support in contemporary studies of internal and international chain-like migration patterns. Empirically, this study examines triadic closure patterns in migratory chains in the Americas between 1960 and 2000. Strong support is found regarding the notion that the diversity of flows in the system (i.e., which countries are and are not connected through migratory corridors) is structured as a (nearly) perfect linear hierarchy of deferential population exchanges, a sign of the existence of strong network inequalities, at the level of triadic migratory chains. More specifically, empirical support in the Americas is found regarding (a) the hierarchical nature of triadic closure in migratory chains as evidenced by the existence of strong tendencies toward both transitivity and (anti)cyclicality among all flows in the system (hypotheses 1a and 1b), (b) the amplification of the hierarchical nature of triadic closure patterns when only the diversity among the largest flows in the system is considered (hypotheses 2a and 2b), and (c) the fact that from both a historical and a statistical perspective transitive migratory chains in particular are shown to be status-driven structures patterned along countries’ underlying levels of attractiveness (hypothesis 3).

In the context of these findings, a first key question for future work emerges: How do transitivity and (anti)cyclicality operate across different types of chain-like migratory patterns? Unlike, say, relay migration or global care chains, other forms of chain-like migratory patterns such as stepwise or stage migration describe place-to-place migration flows of people from the same origin moving through a set of intermediary destinations. Developing a unified theoretical framework to understand the heterogeneity of chain-like migratory patterns (e.g., relay migration vs. stage migration) is thus an important task for future work. To be sure, there is new important work based on MST that theorizes and analyzes stage migration patterns (DeWaard et al. 2016), but no unified framework regarding chain-like migration patterns has been developed yet.

It should be mentioned that even within the migratory chains studied here— that is, chain-like migratory patterns that, like relay migration or global care chains, emerge from place-to-place migration flows from different origins (e.g., Colombia →Venezuela → USA)—several aspects related to the dynamic of closure in these migratory chains need deeper theorization. For instance, are top-down migratory chains that originate at the core of migration systems more or less likely to exhibit stronger tendencies toward transitivity and (anti)cyclicality than either bottom-up migratory chains that originate in the periphery of the system or homogenous migratory chains that only involve countries of the same strata/status (e.g., only peripheral countries)? Furthermore, analyzing triadic closure within specific migration subsystems (e.g., South America) that exclude core destination countries (e.g., the United States) would be helpful to evaluate the extent to which the hierarchical patterns described here depend on the existence of these top system-level (core) destinations. Providing answers to these questions will be key to developing a full-fledged theoretical framework regarding closure patterns across different types of chains (e.g., relay vs. stepwise) and different kinds of countries (e.g., core vs. peripheral).

When thinking about core-periphery dynamics, it should also be noted that MST does not preclude the possibility that countries may be part of more than one migration system (Zlotnik 1992; Jennissen 2007). For instance, countries in the European migration system such as Spain were, and continue to be, key destinations for Latin American migrants (Leal 2014). This scenario cre ates a set of interesting theoretical and empirical questions about the structure of migratory chains across different migration systems. Answering questions like these, however, necessarily presupposes the use of longitudinal global data on migration flows.

Another set of important questions for future studies are related to the identification of migration subsystems beyond triads. The empirical identification of migration systems is a key endeavor of MST (Zlotnik 1992). Thinking about the case of the Americas in particular, the most likely scenario is that migration subsystems in the Americas are correlated to geographic subregions (e.g., Southern Cone, Andean Region). Another possible scenario is that the Americas is better characterized as a two-tiered system with the United States and Canada, on the one hand, and the rest of the countries, on the other. Network techniques for community detection might prove very useful in this context (Wasserman and Faust 1994). Exploring the similari ties and differences in the dynamics of triadic closure within and across each of these subsystems is also an interesting question for future research. Perhaps an identification strategy to understand the boundaries of migration systems is triadic closure itself. For instance, according to the evidence pro vided in this article, transitive triads tend to emerge between countries with different levels of attractiveness. In that context, it is possible to see how the rates at which countries occupy the highly, mildly, or minimally attractive structural positions in transitive chains might be useful to characterize the dif ferent strata (i.e., the core, semiperiphery, and periphery, respectively) along which migration systems could be patterned and thus empirically identified.

Questions regarding the robustness ofthe results presented here not only along different geographies but also along different time periods or for gender-based analysis are also highly relevant. Corroborating the findings reported in this article in a different continental system (e.g., Asia or Africa), at a global scale, or by analyzing gender-based chain-like flows is critical in this regard. When thinking about time specifically, questions about decade-by-decade time heterogeneity or questions about the different time frames (e.g., long term vs. short term) in which the processes described in this article take place are particularly relevant. Using aggregate-level data on migration flows based on shorter intervals of time is a necessary step in this regard. The migration stock database recently produced by the United Nations based on five-year intervals starting in the year 1990 is very promising in this regard. A detailed explanation of the timing (e.g., long term vs. short term) of the processes described in this article can be helpful to start answering questions about causality in the context of MST (Jennissen 2007).

The fact that the migratory chains that contain most of the migrants in the system are highly hierarchical, as shown in table 4, also generates important questions for future research. For instance, table 4 suggests that migratory chains based on flows of different sizes might represent substantively different types of chains (and flows). Here it is worth mentioning that stochastic actor–oriented models (Snijders et al. 2010) have the ability to simultaneously model the coevolution of different networks over time using the same set of predictors. Thus, such analyses could start providing answers to questions regarding how the same processes (e.g., triadic closure, assortative mixing) might play out differently across distinct types of flows (e.g., small flows vs. large flows or south-south flows vs. north-north flows).

Finally, what are the theoretical and empirical implications of modeling migratory chains as weighted networks? In other words, what are the implications of modeling the intensity, not the diversity, of flows? Modeling the evolution over time of migration flows as weighted networks is, without a doubt, an important and exciting frontier in the expansion of both the abstract systems variant of MST and migration studies more generally. Important cross-sectional evidence regarding the intensity of flows supports the conclusions of this article (Windzio et al. 2019). From an applied point of view, focusing on how a network-based approach can be leveraged to serve as a predictive tool of the intensity of flows would be an important input for both academic and policy-oriented audiences interested in the difficult task of forecasting international migration flows.

The main purpose of the FFS method is to consider the array of all origin destination-birthplace tables simultaneously to estimate their (unknown) off-diagonal values. Once these parameter values are estimated, the unknown off-diagonal elements of the arrayed origin-birthplace (flow) tables can be imputed/calculated. Maximum likelihood estimates of yijky _ { i j k } are obtained via an iterative proportional fitting type algorithm. This algorithm is implemented in the cm2 routine in the migest package in R, version 1.7.4 (see Abel [2013] for technical details).

The FFS method is based on two key assumptions. First, since the (unknown) off-diagonal values in the matrices under analysis can contain people who do not move (stayers) and those who do move during the period of analysis (migrants), an assumption must be made about stayers to estimate the number of migrants. Stayers are represented in the diagonal cells of the origin-destination-birthplace tables. The FFS method is based on the assumption that the number of stayers should be maximized (conditional on the marginal stock information, of course). This implies that the migrant transitions reflected in the final estimates correspond to the minimum number of movements between countries that is consistent with the known marginal total. Importantly, the method to estimate flows used in tables C5–C7 relaxes this assumption (Azose and Raftery 2019). As noted by Abel (2018), setting the number of stayers to a minimum would imply the implausible scenario in which there are costs associated with staying rather than associated with moving. Since this article considers decades-long migration patterns, international migration is better understood as an unlikely, rather than likely, course of action because of the many barriers that (long-term) international migrants have to navigate. This is, in turn, consistent with maximizing the number of stayers as part of the estimation process.

Second, the offset term mij,m _ { i j } , which in this article is included as the inverse of the geographic distance between dyads, could have taken other forms. Abel (2018) demonstrates that the FFS method produces consistent esti mates if the offset term is set to 1. The same is true if an alternative distance matrix based on sets of a Pythagorean triple (3, 4, 5) is used. Perhaps more important, modeling the costs of migration using geographic distance as a proxy for migration costs—as it is done in the current article—has been done in previous work in the specialized literature on migration in the Americas (Massey and Aysa-Lastra 2011).

All the figures and tables in the main text are based on FFS estimates, and thus they reflect the assumptions made to estimate the flows (as described in detail above) or flaws in the original/raw bilateral migrant stock data fed to the FFS method. The original/raw bilateral migrant stock data are provided by Özden et al. (2011) after they harmonized demographic migratory information published in censuses and demographic registers by (receiving) coun tries in terms of the origin and number of migrants in their territory from more than 230 (sending) countries and territories around the globe. Importantly, as mentioned by Özden et al. (p. 17), countries in the Americas typically define migrants by country of birth (not by citizenship), which work better to determine physical movement.
