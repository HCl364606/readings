Revised Manuscript

ABSTRACT

This study investigates how mental health affects social integration. We develop a theoretical framework to explain how the differential creation and maintenance of network ties produce distinct sociality patterns, that is, specific propensities to exhibit ties over time. We apply this framework to the case of adolescent friendship networks, where prior studies offer contradictory findings. Using a combination of systematic review and meta-analysis, re-analysis of secondary data across five countries, and an agent-based computational experiment, we test for the form of the depression-sociality link. We find a predominant pattern we label churning sociality, wherein higher levels of depressive symptoms are not necessarily associated with smaller networks, as most prior work assumes, but with greater network turnover. We discuss the theoretical implications of our framework for understanding network integration, its consequences for inequality and cohesion, and methodological implications.

Loneliness and social isolation are pressing health concerns (Jaffe 2023), with depression being among the leading causes of illness and disability for adolescents globally (World Health Organization 2023; Shorey et al. 2022; Ghafari et al. 2022). The dangers of social isolation are both the direct risk to mental health and the reduced access to social support provided through networks (Berkman et al. 2000; Thoits 2011). While studies demonstrate a clear pattern linking poor mental health to reduced network size (Reiner and Steinhoff 2024), explanations for this pattern have been more elusive. Various network selection and social support mechanisms have been hypothesized, but empirical tests have produced inconsistent findings.

We aim to offer new insight into the link between networks and mental health by focusing on depression’s impact on sociality, or the depression-sociality link for short. We take our cue from Goodreau et al. (2009: 105) who explain: “there is heterogeneity among individuals in their propensity to establish friendship ties. We refer to this propensity as sociality and to individuals as more or less social” (emphasis in original). The question of whether depression affects sociality has driven much research on adolescent school-based friendship networks. This focus reflects both the heightened salience of peers and mental health at this life-course stage and that schools provide a natural boundary that facilitates testing many of the pathways linking depression with network structure. Despite a strong theoretical basis, studies of the depression-sociality link in adolescent friendship networks have produced mixed results, with some finding a negative effect (e.g., Schaefer et al. 2011; Giletta et al. 2012; Qin et al. 2023) while others find null or even positive effects (e.g., Doucet et al. 2016; Yang et al. 2014; Yu et al. 2023). Here we identify several possible explanations for these mixed findings and evaluate them with multiple analytical tools.

We first consider whether differences in study design, specification of statistical models, or even random variability may explain mixed findings. We do so through a systematic review and meta-analysis of existing studies, supplemented by our own re-analyses of many of these studies’ datasets to make tests more consistent. Finding no satisfactory answer, we revisit the theoretical foundation behind the forms of the depression-sociality link. We reconceptualize depression as separately affecting relationship creation and maintenance, as opposed to prior studies that focus on depression simply leading to fewer ties. Using this framework, we identify various scenarios representing how tie creation and persistence may combine to shape network partner selection. These yield forms of sociality that we label enclosing, churning, and secluding. We use a longitudinal network model to test this dynamic account in adolescent friendship nomination networks across five countries. Our results provide support for a pattern of depression-based churning sociality, defined by individuals with higher levels of depressive symptoms being less likely to maintain existing ties, and just as likely to create new ties, compared to peers with lower symptoms. In a follow-up computational experiment, we demonstrate how churning sociality can lead to inconsistent results, such as those observed in our systematic review and meta-analysis of the literature, when using the conventional approach for testing how depression affects sociality.

By combining multiple methods, we obtain a clearer picture of the link between depression and social structure via the selection of network partners. We show that youth with elevated depressive symptoms dissolve their relationships faster, but are just as likely to form new friendships as less depressed peers. Thus, our results suggest that depression reconfigures supportive network structures not by inhibiting youth from “putting themselves out there” socially, but by shaping which relationships persist and, ultimately, the rewards that sustain friendships. A higher propensity to form ties that tend to be short-lived differs from a lower propensity to establish new ties. The former points to processes internal to the actor (e.g., self-doubt, pursuit of self-reliance, anticipated stigma), while the latter reflects relational processes and their interpretation. This insight deepens our understanding of the connection between social support and mental health and has implications for efforts to improve health through relational interventions.

This study also has broader implications for understanding social integration. Dyadic ties are the building blocks of social structures (Martin 2009), with actor-level tie distributions explaining much of macro social structure (Faust 2007). Hence, our findings speak to long-standing questions about heterogeneity in social integration within a population (Fischer 1982; Marsden 1987; McPherson et al. 2006). Specifically, we examine the selection mechanisms underlying social network structure and show how distinguishing depression-based tie creation and maintenance points to different mechanisms that impede integration. Our substantive focus is mental health, but the distinction matters for understanding variation in social integration based on a wider set of attributes and across different situations, a point we return to in the Discussion.

BACKGROUND

Social integration has been central to the sociology of (mental) health at least since Durkheim’s work on suicide (1951 [1897]) (see also Berkman et al. 2000). Social network analysis has been widely used to examine the link between depression and social integration. Traditionally, the key structural question is why depression is negatively correlated with the number of relationships (e.g., friendships). The social support tradition conventionally holds that certain network structures (e.g., fewer close relationships) increase the likelihood of loneliness or depression (Berkman et al. 2000). Thus, the effect is from network structure to health. By contrast, the network perspective emphasizes that health can also shape network structure (Haas et al. 2010). Network scholars view relationships as their own accomplishment and identify multiple selection mechanisms that can produce observed network patterns (Rivera et al. 2010). For example, someone who becomes depressed may then withdraw from social contacts or be excluded by others (Schaefer et al. 2011), both of which would result in them having fewer ties.

Among the many pathways linking depression to network structure, we focus on its effect on the propensity to exhibit ties, or sociality. This direct pathway offers the clearest implications for prevention and clinical efforts (i.e., targeting individuals rather than broad network contexts). Whether depression lowers sociality has critical implications. If this effect exists, then depression risks limiting access to resources for better health such as social support (Umberson and Montez 2010; Berkman et al. 2000) or social capital (Perry et al. 2023; Cornwell 2009; Song 2011). This may reinforce depression, with cumulative long-term health effects. If an effect of depression on sociality does not exist, research and network intervention efforts—many of which are designed to target adolescents at school (An 2015; Jemmott et al. 2010; McMillan and Schaefer 2021; Paluck et al. 2016)—can focus on other pathways linking depression to sociality (e.g., peer rejection).

Sociality as a Dynamic Relational Process

As an individual-level process, sociality is one of the most fundamental network processes. Hence, heterogeneity in actors’ propensity to exhibit ties (i.e., sociality) is a key factor explaining global network properties (Goodreau et al. 2009; see also Faust 2007; Robins, Pattison, and Woolcock 2005). Probing how actors’ attributes (e.g., depression) are linked to sociality can thus help reveal how those attributes become sources of emergent network structure. Here we focus on establishing the depression-sociality link in adolescent friendship nomination networks, asking whether higher levels of depressive symptoms are associated with the propensity to create ties, as separate from the propensity to maintain them. Our dynamic perspective raises the possibility of several scenarios, including the often-expected scenario marked by tie loss and reduced tie maintenance (e.g., social withdrawal), but also a scenario marked by increased relationship turnover, which includes tie creation as a key ingredient.

Friendship is a rather dynamic relationship (Hartup and Stevens 1997; Hruschka 2010; Kitts and Leal 2021). That is, friendship ties reflect an interaction process with fluctuations that result in social networks that are continuously evolving. Friendship’s dynamism is especially true among adolescents, for whom peer relations are a primary concern (Crosnoe et al. 2008). At its simplest, network change can be separated into the creation and dissolution of individual ties (Snijders 2001; Small et al. 2015). Network scholars have theorized about the distinction between the formation and persistence of ties across time, cautioning against the assumption that the processes behind them are equivalent (Block 2014; Krivitsky and Handcock 2014).

For example, in their monumental review, McPherson et al. (2001) identified selective tie dissolution as one pathway to homophily. They reasoned that even if ties form randomly, greater tie persistence between similar people than between dissimilar people would produce homophily. Thus, homophily could arise through selective tie creation and/or selective tie maintenance. Following this insight, research has found that homophilous ties are more likely to form, but also more likely to dissolve, in the cases of alcohol use (Cheadle et al. 2013) and shyness (Yang et al. 2021); similarity on happiness enhances friendship maintenance, with no effects on tie formation (van Workum et al. 2013); and similarity in relational victimization promotes friendship creation and persistence equally (Sijtsema et al. 2013).

Recent network studies have expanded creation-dissolution analyses from homophily to also encompass sociality. The question here is whether certain attributes make people more likely to develop or maintain ties. Examining first-year college students, Schaefer et al. (2021) investigated the paradox that alcohol use may facilitate socializing while harming friendship quality. They found regular and binge drinkers were more likely to both develop and dissolve friendships. Copeland et al. (2023) theorized that poor overall health may affect friendship creation and persistence if, say, youth with poor overall health are less involved in foci (e.g., schools) where ties arise or persist. They found youth in poorer health were less likely to create new ties, but no less likely to maintain them.

With respect to sociality and mental health, some studies have acknowledged that depressive symptoms may inhibit both friendship creation and maintenance, but without theorizing how these effects may diverge (Schaefer et al. 2011; van Zalk et al. 2010a). Only one prior network study has actually measured tie creation separately from tie maintenance in relation to adolescent depression. In investigating depression homophily, van Zalk et al. (2010a) found support for the argument that similarity among friends arises more from de-selection than from homophilous tie formation. Moreover, although not their focus, their findings suggest that adolescent depression may undermine tie maintenance without inhibiting creation. This study nevertheless differs from ours in important respects: it examines self-reported interactions rather than friendship ties, covers youth in a single small Swedish town, does not account for the possibility of reverse causality (e.g., smaller networks leading to higher levels of depressive symptoms), and differentiates creation and maintenance for only a subset of effects, raising the possibility of specification bias. Their study had the advantage of using sociocentric data (i.e., all ties of a specific type [e.g., interactions] within a bounded setting [e.g., town or school]), which allowed them to separate the effect of depression on one’s own propensity to select others (a depression ego effect) from one’s likelihood of being selected by others (a depression alter effect), as well as measure ego’s propensity to select alters with similar levels of depression. Separating these three effects is key to understanding whose depression matters for friendship dynamics (as we explain in greater detail below). However, given their interest in homophily, they did not unpack how these three effects combined to affect depression sociality. Because of these shortcomings, we regard van Zalk et al.’s (2010a) evidence as suggestive as it remains unclear if their findings imply a depression-sociality link or a more complex selection pattern (e.g., Snijders and Lomi 2019).

Egocentric studies, by contrast, analyze the personal networks of individuals who are not necessarily part of the same bounded context, as often found in population-based samples. Research in this vein provides important longitudinal and qualitative accounts of depression-based network turnover. For instance, stable network size can conceal simultaneous tie losses and additions, with these changes reflecting, among other things, purposive pruning, anticipated stigma, changing support needs, or disruptions in work, residence, and treatment settings (Link et al. 1989; Perry 2012, 2014; Perry and Pescosolido 2012; see also Small et al. 2015; Cornwell et al. 2021). This literature therefore anticipates our distinction between tie volume and the separate processes of tie creation and maintenance.

Taken together, these literatures provide important but incomplete evidence. Egocentric panel and qualitative studies show that stable network size can conceal tie loss and replacement and identify mechanisms (e.g., purposive pruning) behind turnover. Yet their ego-centered perspective cannot readily separate egos’ creation and maintenance propensities from alters’ relational behavior. Sociocentric studies have found that health more generally has differential effects on creation and maintenance (Copeland et al. 2023), but studies of depression specifically have all too often constrained these effects to be equal (Schaefer et al. 2011). van Zalk et al. (2010a; see also 2010b) come closest to our question and offer evidence that depression matters differently for tie creation and maintenance, but their inferences are limited by the issues noted above. Existing work therefore cannot determine whether depression independently affects creation, maintenance, or both; whether apparent effects reflect alternative selection or influence processes; or whether the pattern generalizes. We address these by analyzing harmonized, longitudinal, sociocentric data to distinguish tie creation from maintenance, while accounting for competing pathways and testing whether the pattern recurs across a variety of settings.

Forms of the Depression-Sociality Link

Research on social isolation and withdrawal in general, and on depression and sociality in particular, routinely focuses on reduced tie volume (e.g., fewer friends). While intuitive, this approach collapses two relational processes (i.e., tie creation and maintenance) into a single outcome: degree. Importantly, “sociality is not synonymous with degree, an individual’s number of ties, which is the outcome of a stochastic process shaped by both sociality and other factors” (Goodreau et al. 2009: 105; emphasis in original). Here we distinguish network processes from network outcomes by restricting sociality to denote a process and degree to denote an outcome (Goodreau et al. 2009; Lewis and Kaufman 2018; Leal 2021). In that context, we submit that the deleterious effects of depression on sociality need not manifest as network shrinkage, that is, as low degree (i.e., reduced tie volume). Depending on how depression operates on creation versus maintenance, tie volume may or may not decline. Critically, depression may increase tie dissolution, but if those ties are replaced, tie volume will remain unchanged.

We begin with scenario A in Table 1, which represents the absence of a negative effect of depression on sociality. In this scenario, depression does not inhibit tie creation or persistence. Here individuals with higher levels of depression create and maintain ties at the same (or higher) rate as their less depressed counterparts.1 In this scenario, the lack of depression effects implies that any apparent pattern of lower sociality among more depressed youth is driven by alternative selection pathways, such as depression-based rejection from peers or depression-based homophily (see Schaefer et al. 2011; van Zalk et al. 2010a), or an effect of smaller network size on depression (i.e., isolation-induced depression, that is, reverse causality).

Scenario B represents when depression leads to a lower likelihood of tie creation and either positive or neutral tie maintenance. This is expected to leave youth confined to a smaller circle of peers as time progresses, leading us to characterize this process as enclosing sociality. Among the many possible mechanisms behind this pattern, Modified Labeling Theory suggests that a protective maneuvering for people to overcome the stigma of a “depressed” label is to retreat to small groups to feel “protected from the rejection that might ensue if they ventured out to seek friends, jobs, and the like in the wider social environment” (Link et al. 1989: 403). Another possible mechanism behind enclosing sociality is co-rumination. This is a form of support seeking where individuals selectively discuss problems and negative feelings at length with one or a few close friends (Rose 2002), deepening social enclosure rather than facilitating new connections. Evidence of co-rumination as a coping strategy among depressed adolescents is robust (Stone et al. 2011; Schwartz-Mette and Rose 2012; Spendelow et al. 2017).

In scenario C, depressed adolescents exhibit negative tie maintenance and either positive or neutral tie creation. Here depression disrupts relationships, increasing tie dissolution without necessarily inhibiting new tie formation. Those depressed therefore experience greater network churn: relationships turn over more rapidly but are not necessarily fewer in number. We label this scenario as churning sociality. The Interpersonal Theory of Depression (Coyne 1976; Starr and Davila 2008) identifies what it terms ‘excessive reassurance seeking,’ which here we see as one possible mechanism behind churning. Excessive reassurance seeking is a form of support seeking rooted in feelings of low self-worth that can destabilize ties through a cycle of reassurance seeking, relationship strain and tie dissolution (i.e., tie loss), and seeking reassurance elsewhere (i.e., tie creation). Evidence of excessive reassurance seeking in depressed youth is robust (Joiner and Metalsky 2001; Joiner et al. 2001; Nesi and Prinstein 2015) along with evidence that they replace rather than renominate friends across waves (Chan and Poulin 2010). This pattern also appears in the only prior study of adolescent depression to distinguish tie creation from maintenance (van Zalk et al. 2010a; see also van Zalk et al. 2010b). The concept of ‘shyness’ in the literature on anxious withdrawal in adolescence suggests another mechanism: shy youth strongly desire social contact yet fear it (Bowker et al. 2021). Shyness predicts tie creation, often with similarly shy peers (Yang et al. 2021; see also Bowker et al. 2021). Yet, the social anxiety underlying shyness can also interfere with the processes of self-disclosure that are known to help sustain adolescent friendships (Ladd et al. 2011; Bowker et al. 2021; cf. Hruschka 2010). Yang et al. (2021) report evidence of both (1) shyness-based homophilous peer selection where youth are more likely to create, and less likely to maintain, friendships with similarly shy peers; and (2) shyness having a positive effect on subsequent depression. Finally, Perry (2012) provides evidence that adults with severe mental illness reorganize their ties through ‘social network strategy,’ that is, by purposively cutting ties to avoid burdening others, conflict, or stigma, to pursue self-reliance, or to disengage from unhelpful alters. Over a 5-year period, these adults—half with major depression—dropped and added about five ties on average, thus also matching churning sociality’s aggregate signature.

Lastly, scenario D is the most extreme version of the depression-sociality link, wherein individuals with higher levels of depression exhibit both negative tie creation and maintenance. Here depression leads to the dissolution of existing ties along with a diminished tendency to create new ties. As a result, the negative effects on sociality would be extreme as those depressed progressively isolate over time, leading us to label this process as secluding sociality. Among the many possible mechanisms behind this pattern, we highlight core depressive symptoms like anhedonia (Pizzagalli 2014)—and, more specifically, social anhedonia, which is defined as the loss of interest and lack of pleasure from social contact (Brown et al. 2007). To be sure, secluding sociality may not lead to total isolation. For instance, Perry and Pescosolido (2012) explain how mental health crises can lead to withdrawing from all but the most supportive ties.

In sum, our proposed framework outlines three ideal-type scenarios or configurations where depression negatively affects tie creation and/or maintenance—thus ultimately shaping sociality—while also providing a non-exhaustive list of possible mechanisms for each scenario:2

  • Enclosing sociality: Depression reduces tie creation but leaves maintenance neutral or positive, resulting in a smaller yet non-isolated network.
    • Possible mechanisms: labeling and stigma, co-rumination.
  • Churning sociality: Depression reduces tie maintenance but leaves creation neutral or positive, yielding an unstable network.
    • Possible mechanisms: excessive reassurance seeking, shyness, social network strategy.
  • Secluding sociality: Depression reduces both tie creation and maintenance, yielding intense social isolation.
    • Possible mechanisms: anhedonia and social anhedonia, mental health crises.

These scenarios point toward possible sources of inconsistent estimates in studies of depression and tie volume. First, since churning sociality can generate greater friendship turnover, those depressed may have as many friends as their nondepressed peers at some time points, but fewer at others. Thus, the association between depression and tie volume can vary with the timing of data collection. Second, when churning sociality or enclosing sociality emerges due to countervailing effects (i.e., depression having a positive association with tie creation and a negative one with tie maintenance, or vice versa), its association with outdegree might go undetected unless the two processes are separated. In either case, the conventional specification in the existing literature—which, as explained in detail below, constrains maintenance and creation to be equal—combines these opposite effects, which can contribute to null results.

Current Study

What is the link of depression to sociality? To answer this question, we adopt a novel, multi-method empirical approach. To offer a current best estimate and assess variability across studies, we embark on a systematic review and meta-analysis of prior sociocentric studies that have estimated how depression affects sociality. Methodologically, statistical models for longitudinal sociocentric data can represent reciprocal pathways and discern whether earlier depression predicts tie changes or vice versa. The most advanced such model to date is the Stochastic Actor-Oriented Model (SAOM). SAOMs are designed to estimate how actors’ attributes (e.g., depression) both shape and are shaped by their networks over time (Steglich et al. 2010). Although often used to assess whether homophily reflects peer influence or selection into relationships based on preexisting similarities (van Zalk et al. 2010a), SAOMs can also assess if tie volume (i.e., number of ties) affects the level of an attribute or vice versa (Schaefer et al. 2012). Thus, our systematic review and meta-analysis offers (1) a pooled estimate of the effect of depression on sociality across SAOM studies, and (2) the capacity to test how known study design variations affect estimates. Then, to evaluate the implication of model specification differences, we conduct our own analyses of multiple secondary datasets. We acquired as many studies from the systematic review as possible and estimated SAOMs that adopt a consistent model specification with appropriate controls. This provides (3) a more rigorous estimate of the depression-sociality link by minimizing variation in model specification across existing datasets.

Our final steps move from tie volume, focusing instead on depression’s effect on tie creation and maintenance. We test this by fitting novel SAOMs to the above data. This analysis provides (4) a test of various forms of sociality that operate through tie creation and maintenance as outlined in Table 1. Finding evidence mostly consistent with depression-based churning sociality, we employ a computational experiment to evaluate how churning sociality appears in the estimates from a standard (misspecified) SAOM. This offers (5) evidence that a process of churning sociality can lead to inconsistent estimates of the depression-sociality link, as seen in the existing literature.

METHODS & DATA

Because the SAOM is at the heart of both our systematic review and meta-analysis and our own modeling efforts, we first provide an overview of the model with particular attention to how it represents the link between depression and social relations. After describing the SAOM and providing a schematic representation of our conceptual model, we provide the details behind our systematic review and meta-analysis of existing SAOM studies. We follow this by explaining how we build upon our systematic review and meta-analysis to estimate our own SAOMs.

The Stochastic Actor-Oriented Model (SAOM): Tie Presence, Creation, and Maintenance

The SAOM’s appeal for evaluating the depression-sociality link is its ability to estimate multiple, bidirectional pathways between depression and social networks. SAOMs are specifically designed to estimate network-attribute (e.g., friendship-depression) co-evolution (Steglich et al. 2010). A co-evolution SAOM has two functions: one modeling network change and another modeling attribute change. SAOMs use whole-network data from bounded settings, such as schools (i.e., sociocentric data). In the studies we reviewed, surveys asked students to name friends within that setting, allowing all dyads to be coded as tied or not. In our case, ties must be directed: a student could name someone as a friend whether or not that person reciprocated. Surveys also measured students’ level of depressive symptoms. SAOMs require at least two observation waves, which allows modeling endogenous change in both the network and the attribute of interest.

SAOMs use an agent-based, Markov chain Monte Carlo algorithm to simulate unobserved network and attribute changes between waves (Snijders et al. 2010). The algorithm assumes actors control their outgoing ties and attribute levels. During “microsteps,” whose frequency is dictated by a rate parameter, a randomly selected actor makes a probabilistic change: adjusting their ties (add a tie, delete a tie, or do nothing) or their attribute (e.g., increase or decrease depressive symptoms by one unit, or do nothing). The included effects3 and their current parameter values determine the probability of each decision. The model simulates many microsteps and estimates parameters that reproduce key features of the observed network and attribute distributions (Ripley et al. 2025).

Our central process of interest is depression sociality, the tendency for an ego with higher levels of depression to name (fewer) alters as friends, regardless of alters’ depression levels. In SAOMs this is represented by ego’s depression affecting their likelihood of extending a tie when given the chance during a microstep. Standard SAOMs constrain the parameter weighting this decision to be the same whether ego is considering adding a tie to a new alter or keeping a tie to an existing alter. This is the depression ego ‘evaluation’ effect in SAOM parlance (see Table 2, first row). Our proposed alternative is to allow for separate parameter weights on each of these two types of tie change by replacing the depression ego evaluation effect with distinct depression ego tie maintenance and depression ego tie creation effects (see Table 2, second and third rows).

Figure 1 distinguishes effects of depression on networks (Box A) from reverse effects of networks on depression (Box B). In the latter, people may converge toward their peers’ levels of depression (i.e., depression harmonization [Brashears 2008], or depression-based peer influence; see also Table 2, sixth row). People with fewer friends may also face greater depression risk (i.e., isolation-induced depression). Depression sociality thus cannot be properly evaluated without accounting for isolation-induced depression. Although the social support tradition documents bidirectional links between network size and depression (Berkman et al. 2000), no study in our systematic review explicitly accounted for this possibility, a major limitation we address in our own analysis.

Other controls appear in Boxes C to E in Figure 1. On the selection side, Box C includes effects of third factors on network structure. This includes similarity on sociodemographic background (e.g., sex) and health factors (e.g., body weight) that may shape friend selection (Crosnoe et al. 2008). The SAOM can also control for endogenous network dynamics that “amplify” selection on other factors (Wimmer and Lewis 2010). Specifically, Box D includes tendencies toward reciprocity, triadic closure (i.e., friends of friends becoming friends), and for reciprocity and transitivity to offset one another (Block 2015). To improve fit, SAOMs often model indegree and outdegree dispersion through indegree popularity, outdegree activity, and outdegree popularity effects (Ripley et al. 2025). For depressive symptoms, SAOMs can represent the effects in Boxes C and E in Figure 1. Here, it is important to control for the effect of sex on depressive symptoms, given evidence that depressive symptoms increase during adolescence, especially among girls (Costello et al. 2003; Rottenberg 2022). SAOMs also use linear and quadratic terms to control for the distribution of the attribute of interest, which here would capture any tendency for depression to be self-reinforcing (Ripley et al. 2025).

In sum, although our interest is in depression sociality, empirically isolating this effect requires controlling for other depression-based selection forces in Box A, effects in the opposite causal direction in Box B, potential confounds in Box C, and the various ways that networks and depression reinforce themselves (Boxes D and E). Thus, even though our effect of interest is relatively simple, the competing explanations become rather complex.

Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis: Collecting Data from a Disparate Literature

Although depression is theorized to reduce sociality, SAOM studies of its effect on outdegree report mixed results: negative (e.g., Schaefer et al. 2011; Qin et al. 2023; Giletta et al. 2012), nonsignificant (e.g., DeLay et al. 2017; Pachucki et al. 2015; Yu et al. 2023), or positive (e.g., Doucet et al. 2016). One study found effects shifting between positive and negative across three years (Yang et al. 2014). Studies also found gender differences (Cheadle and Goosby 2012), including a negative association only among girls (Zhao et al. 2020). Lastly, one study found that depression increased tie dissolution but did not affect creation (van Zalk et al. 2010a).

Mixed findings may reflect differences in samples and methods. Prior studies span countries, use different depression measures and numbers of waves, and examine networks ranging from small classrooms to large schools. Studies also pursue different questions: testing peer influence on depression (Doucet et al. 2016; Giletta et al. 2012), discerning selection mechanisms behind depression homophily (Schaefer et al. 2011), jointly assessing several pathways linking depression and networks (Cheadle and Goosby 2012; van Zalk et al. 2010a), and testing moderating effects (Yu et al. 2023; Zhao et al. 2020). Thus, prior model specification and controls vary by inferential goals. This type of heterogeneity, also known as ‘analytical heterogeneity’ (Holzmeister et al. 2024), matters as differentially adjusting for alternative explanations may produce model-induced variability, likely generating inconsistent estimates of depression sociality. Systematic reviews and meta-analyses are the gold standard for synthesizing such disparate evidence and, critically, have proven useful in uncovering consistent effects across SAOM studies on adolescent outcomes such as alcohol use (Ivaniushina and Titkova 2021) and criminal behavior (Gallupe et al. 2019).

The focus of our systematic review was on studies that: (a) used SAOMs and thus were longitudinal and observational in nature; (b) estimated the focal effect of interest, the ‘depression ego’ coefficient (see Table 2); (c) examined adolescents in school-like settings (e.g., schools, summer camps); and (d) employed a sociocentric design. The search spanned 2001–2024 (2001 is the year SAOMs were introduced; Snijders [2001]) across EBSCO, ProQuest, PsycINFO, and PubMed. To reduce publication bias, we included gray literature (e.g., dissertations, conference papers). Appendix A, Table A.1, details the terms and procedures we used to scan the literature.

Screening proceeded in three stages: title, abstract, and full text. At each stage, studies were coded “yes” or “no” for inclusion by two co-authors. We erred toward inclusion in earlier stages (e.g., title), advancing any disagreements to the next step (e.g., abstract). The only two studies (van Zalk et al. 2010a, 2010b) separately estimating depression effects on tie creation and maintenance were excluded because such effects are not comparable to those from standard tie volume models (Ripley et al. 2025).4 Eleven studies met our criteria, yielding 41 depression ego coefficients from 10 datasets in five countries (the United States, Italy, the Netherlands, Taiwan, and the United Kingdom). SAOM Data: Standardizing Samples and Measures Across Five Countries

Samples

To estimate our own SAOMs, we obtained three datasets analyzed by studies covered in our systematic review: the National Longitudinal Study of Adolescent to Adult Health (Add Health; Harris 2013), the Children of Immigrants Longitudinal Survey in Four European Countries (CILS4EU; Kalter et al. 2016), and the Taiwan Youth Project (TYP; Yi 2020). For each dataset, we first attempted to replicate the published analyses. We then estimated additional models using harmonized data and predictors to test both standard (tie volume) and nonstandard (tie creation and maintenance) SAOMs. To ensure rigor, we excluded networks that did not meet response-rate thresholds, sometimes applying stricter criteria than the original studies.

For each dataset, we only kept networks (TYP and CILS4EU classrooms; Add Health schools) with response rates above 80% and, following Yu et al. (2023), excluded respondents missing depression data.13 Additional criteria varied by dataset. For Add Health’s nationally representative U.S. high school sample, we began with the 16 schools in the saturated sample where all students were targeted for network data collection at multiple time points (i.e., Home 1 and Home 2 waves). The survey was designed to obtain nominations of five best female and five best male friends, but an implementation error restricted many wave 2 respondents to only one friend of each sex (Moody 1999; Lakon et al. 2015). We retained only schools where at least 70% of students were unaffected, yielding 980 adolescents in grades 7–12 across three schools.14 CILS4EU covers England, Sweden, the Netherlands, and Germany, with students nominating up to five best friends. We excluded Dutch data because those students were reassigned to new classrooms at wave 2. The analytical sample included 14 classrooms in England (N=289), 32 in Sweden (N=609), and 23 in Germany (N=521), totaling 1,419 adolescents. Finally, TYP followed Taiwanese adolescents in grades 7–9. For consistency, we used waves 2 and 3, which capped friendship nominations at three, and excluded wave 1, which capped nominations at five. The final TYP sample consists of 57 classrooms and 1,863 adolescents.

Measures

For all three datasets, friendship nominations are directed (coded 1 if ego i named alter j as a friend; 0 otherwise). In Add Health, we removed nominations sent outside of ego’s school or outside the saturated sample. In TYP, we removed nominations outside of the classroom and self-nominations. The Jaccard index for network change is greater than 0.2 for all analyzed networks, a requirement to estimate SAOMs (Ripley et al. 2025).

Psychometric research recommends measuring depression on a continuum of symptom severity (Klein 2008; Hankin et al. 2005). Thus, in TYP, depressive symptoms were measured via the validated Symptom Checklist-90-Revised Scale (SCL-90-R; 16 items; Cronbach’s alpha [α] = 0.86 at wave 2 and 0.88 at wave 3) (see Table B1 in the Appendix for details). In Add Health, they were measured via the validated Center for Epidemiologic Studies-Depression Scale (CES-D; 19 items; α = 0.86 at wave 1 and 0.87 at wave 2) (see Table B2 in the Appendix for details). In CILS4EU, depressive symptoms were measured by 5 questions about the frequency of depression-related symptoms (5 items; α = 0.86 at wave 1 and 0.88 at wave 2) (see Table B3 in the Appendix for details).15 To harmonize our data and analyses, we summed each student’s depressive symptoms items by wave and recoded their scores into quintiles: 0 = 0-19th; 1 = 20th-39th; 2 = 40th-59th; 3 = 60th-79th; 4 = 80th-100th, from low to high.16 Robustness checks detailed in the Results section show that our findings hold if deciles are used instead of quintiles.17

SAOM Specification and Fit

The goal of fitting our own SAOMs was to estimate all pathways in Figure 1 under a consistent specification. We thus included all previously described effects, including the effects in Table 2. We estimated a separate SAOM for each network using default method-of-moments estimation. Because TYP and CILS4EU classrooms were small, we assessed robustness via Bayesian estimation with country-level random effects. To summarize depression ego effects across classrooms, we fit country-specific meta-analyses via Snijders and Baerveldt’s (2003) two-stage weighted least-squares estimator, which accounts for differences in parameters’ standard errors. Meta-analysis results were robust to other designs, including multivariate fixed and random effects models (An 2015; Duxbury 2021; Duxbury and Wertsching 2023). Since only three Add Health schools meet our inclusion criteria, there we report estimates by school.18

SAOM convergence is indicated by an overall maximum convergence ratio below 0.25 and parameter convergence t-ratios below |0.1| (Ripley et al. 2025). SAOM goodness-of-fit is adequate when it reproduces observed network and attribute distributions. We assessed fit via Mahalanobis distance tests comparing Monte Carlo-simulated statistics, based on estimated parameters, with observed wave 2 statistics (Lospinoso and Snijders 2019). We use standard fit targets: triad census, indegree, outdegree, and attribute (depressive symptoms) distributions. With 5,000 phase-3 iterations in RSiena,19 the smallest nonzero p-value we could have observed was 0.0002; thus, lower values were treated as 0, indicating poor fit (Ripley et al. 2025). All our models have p-values greater than 0 in all four distributions (see Appendix B, Figures B1–B6).

RESULTS

Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis

Our first step is to summarize prior studies by estimating the overall effect of the depression-sociality link with a pooled estimate from the literature. As shown in the Forest Plot in the top panel of Figure 3, depression ego coefficients from prior studies ranged from negative to positive, with just over one quarter reported as statistically significant. The MLRE meta-analytic model that we estimated provides a pooled estimate that is negative, but not significant (β = -0.004; SE = 0.006; p = 0.521). This result is consistent with our qualitative impression of great variability across tie volume (standard) SAOMs as it shows a null effect.

Prior studies varied in whether nominations covered best friends only or a wider set, whether nominations were capped, version of depression scale, network level (classroom versus school), country, number of waves, and dataset. We especially suspected that nomination caps or network level were likely to have attenuated depressive symptoms’ effect on tie volume. Hence, we tested whether stratifying the analysis by any of the above factors brought clarity. As shown by the nonsignificant estimates in Figure 3’s bottom panel, no moderator changed the conclusion of a null estimate. Additional sensitivity analyses addressed outlier studies, concerns over too few studies, and other potential biases (see Appendix A, Tables A.2 to A.4). These follow-up analyses also suggest a null effect. We conclude that, across existing SAOM studies, depression did not significantly affect tie volume, nor did this effect systematically vary by key study characteristics.

SAOM: Two Nonstandard Specifications

Our fourth step tested our theoretical proposition that depressive symptoms can affect sociality by separately shaping the creation of new ties and the maintenance of existing ones. To this end, we used two nonstandard SAOM specifications that complement each other.

First, we allowed the depression ego effect to vary between tie creation and maintenance while retaining the default tie-volume specification for all other effects. This approach of decomposing only key effects into maintenance and creation components has been used in SAOM studies of adolescent drinking (Cheadle et al. 2013), smoking (An 2015), and psychological factors (Yang et al. 2021). The drawback, as Block (2014) notes, is the possibility of specification bias: if only a subset of effects is split into creation and maintenance, estimates may be distorted. For example, a genuine maintenance effect may be offset by a creation estimate that is inflated due to misspecification of other model effects. We attempted to decompose all model effects into creation and maintenance, but these models did not converge because their demands on the data were too great—which is to be expected (see Block 2014).

We therefore adopted a second nonstandard SAOM specification following Block (2014) by entering depressive symptoms as an actor-level covariate in the network rate function (i.e., a RateX effect in SAOM parlance, which hereafter we refer to as the ‘depression rate effect’), while splitting every retained control in the network function (e.g., reciprocity) into creation and maintenance components. A rate effect governs the relative frequency with which actors exhibit opportunities to reconsider an outgoing tie (Snijders 2001). Because each opportunity may result in tie creation, dissolution, or no change, a positive depression rate effect would indicate more frequent tie changes among youth with higher depressive symptoms, but it would not signal the direction of those tie changes. That is, the sign of the depression rate effect alone does not distinguish among the forms of depression-based sociality in Table 1—that interpretation requires complementary insight from our first nonstandard SAOM that separated tie creation from maintenance. Specifically, the combination of a nonnegative tie creation effect and a negative maintenance effect indicative of churning sociality should coincide with a positive depression rate effect (because depressed youth are making more changes overall). With enclosing sociality, the combination of a reluctance to form new ties while maintaining ties at the same rate should produce a negative depression rate effect. This would become increasingly negative to the extent that depressed youth also have a stronger tendency to maintain their ties. For secluding sociality, the depression rate effect would most likely be negative or null depending upon whether inhibited tie formation (which decreases the change rate) or elevated tie dissolution (which increases the change rate) is stronger, respectively. Note that it is even possible for the depression rate effect to be positive, if depressed youth dropped ties quite rapidly, though this would be short-lived as they would quickly run out of ties to drop. Thus, by considering the results of the two nonstandard model specifications jointly, we can have greater confidence in inferring a specific form of sociality. To be clear, in no case do these analyses establish the specific temporal sequence of tie changes22 or the mechanism linking tie losses to additions (for a non-exhaustive list of mechanisms, see the Theory section).

Fitting the second nonstandard SAOM specification places great demands on the data. We therefore restricted the analysis to CILS4EU and TYP, which contain many comparably sized (classroom) networks, and adopted a “minimal” specification (Block 2014: 111). The minimal model included the depression rate effect and separate creation and maintenance parameters for two sets of controls: effects found to be significant in our standard SAOMs (i.e., outdegree, reciprocity, transitivity, same sex, and same ethnicity) and the depression alter and similarity effects for completeness. We further simplified this model by omitting the effect of outdegree on depressive symptoms (i.e., isolation-induced depression), as we found little empirical evidence that this effect was present in our case.23

One may question whether the churn we observe across these two nonstandard SAOMs is an artifact of the model. Thus, as a further robustness check, we strip away all complexity and examine several bivariate associations between depression and network structure across all five countries combined.27 We find strong descriptive evidence that the churning pattern is visible in the raw data (see Appendix C, Figure C.1, Panels A-E, for measurement and results details). Although these descriptive analyses cannot account for competing selection pathways, endogenous network processes, or reverse causality, their convergence with the SAOM estimates strengthens our inference of depression-based churning sociality.

Implications of Network Churn for Modeling Networks via Standard SAOMs

Having found that the depression-sociality link is driven by churning, the question remains as to whether model misspecification is responsible for contradictory findings in the literature. Our fifth and final step is to test whether modeling network change driven by churning sociality using the standard SAOM specification could have led to mixed findings. We follow Daza and Krueger (2021) in developing an agent-based computational experiment to probe SAOM results. This approach gives us full control over the data-generating process by specifying the network decisions agents make based on simulated data. More specifically, we set tie formation and maintenance probabilities as functions of a synthetic variable representing depression. We then systematically manipulated the differential between depressed and non-depressed agents’ tie maintenance and tie creation probabilities (see Appendix D for details). We considered depression differentials that range from -0.8 (i.e., depressed egos have a 0.1 probability of change, and non-depressed egos have a 0.9 probability of change [0.1 – 0.9 = -0.8]) to 0.8 (depressed egos have a change probability of 0.9, and non-depressed egos have a change probability of 0.1 [0.9 – 0.1 = 0.8]). These differentials were manipulated independently for tie creation and tie maintenance in a factorial design. For each simulated network-attribute (i.e., friendship-depression) configuration, we fit a standard SAOM and recorded the estimated depression ego effect (summarized in Appendix D, Figures D.1 and D.2). The code for the agent-based model, and for reproducing the computational experiment, is in Appendix D, Table D.2.

We find that when depression-based tie maintenance and creation go in the same direction, that is, both positive or both negative, the standard SAOM consistently produces a depression ego estimate in that same direction. Moreover, when effects of depression differ in direction for tie maintenance versus creation, the depression ego estimate may be positive or negative depending upon whether the creation or maintenance probability is greater. This is all intuitive and as expected.

Results are more informative when creation and maintenance probabilities are equal. When both probability differentials are zero, the depression ego estimate is close to zero and negative about half the time, as would be expected by chance. However, this changes as probability magnitudes move away from zero, as would occur with stronger effects of depression on network selection. Of greatest interest is how the model detects depression-based churning sociality, which occurs when the maintenance probability difference is negative (depressed actors are less likely to keep ties) and the creation probability difference is positive (depressed actors are more likely to form ties). Setting these values to -0.2 and 0.2, respectively, we find that 46% of estimated ego depression parameters are also negative. When the maintenance and creation differentials are even stronger, set at -0.4 and 0.4, respectively, then the proportion of negative estimates increases to 69%, and when setting those same differentials at -0.6 and 0.6, respectively, the proportion of negative coefficients increases to 74%. These results suggest that as the network selection effects resembling churning sociality become stronger, the standard SAOM will more often produce a negative parameter estimate, but not always. Lastly, consider when the maintenance probability difference is negative and small (-0.2) and the creation difference is zero. This condition is another good example of churning sociality as depressed agents are less likely to maintain ties, while creation probabilities are equivalent regardless of depression. Here we find that the estimated depression ego effect is usually negative, but in 6% of simulation runs the estimate is positive. Our results thus show that when depression only affects maintenance, that is, in scenarios with no effect on tie formation, the standard SAOM specification can still produce estimates that range from negative to positive.

In sum, our computational experiments demonstrate high variability in standard (tie volume) SAOM estimates in the presence of known differences in actors’ tie creation and maintenance by a key actor attribute. Under various conditions, including parameterizations resembling the networks contained in our meta-analysis, the conventional (i.e., tie volume) SAOM produced estimates that were largely in the expected direction, but ranged from positive to negative due to random variability. This lends further support to the possibility that misspecifying the form of the depression-sociality link in prior studies contributed to their inconsistent findings.

DISCUSSION

This study advances our understanding of the relationship between social networks and (mental) health by showing that depressive symptoms do not simply shrink networks but rather destabilize them. Across school-based networks within five countries, we find a predominant pattern of depression-based churning sociality: adolescents with higher levels of depressive symptoms are less likely to maintain existing ties while showing positive or neutral propensities to create new ones. Identifying this process brings clarity to the literature by explaining how the standard practice of collapsing tie maintenance and creation into a single metric can result in estimates that oscillate between negative, null, and positive, as seen across existing studies.

Our main contributions are thus threefold. Theoretically, we advance a framework for how differential tie creation and maintenance processes can result in distinct forms of sociality. Although developed for depression, this framework can be applied to other individual attributes. Methodologically, we describe how to test our framework while controlling for alternate selection mechanisms that can also produce a (negative) association between depression and sociality. By combining a reanalysis of multiple datasets with a computational experiment, we further demonstrate that network churn can lead to unstable model estimates if not properly specified. This has further implications for the application of longitudinal network models that we elaborate below. Empirically, we find that the depression-sociality link in school-based adolescent friendship nominations is best characterized by depression-based churning sociality: high tie turnover, not fewer ties (i.e., no lower overall tie volume), though not universally.

Our central theoretical point is that social integration depends on two distinct processes: creating and maintaining ties. This point has been made previously (Copeland et al. 2023; Krivitsky and Handcock 2014; van Zalk et al. 2010a) but the cumulative effects on an actor’s social integration have only rarely been considered (Small et al. 2015). We extend this work by theorizing how attribute-based propensities for tie creation and maintenance combine to produce distinct patterns of sociality and thus, ultimately, distinct patterns of social integration. Specifically, higher depressive symptoms may be associated with different tie creation and maintenance propensities even when tie volume is constant. Put simply, focusing on tie volume risks obfuscating network disruption, while separating tie maintenance from creation reveals churn as a patterned social process.

Egocentric and sociocentric approaches offer complementary perspectives on these processes. Egocentric panel and qualitative research identifies mechanisms that may produce turnover (e.g., purposive pruning, changing support needs, substitution, and disruptions across social contexts) and demonstrates that stable network size can conceal substantial membership instability. The comparative advantage of sociocentric, whole-network, research lies in modeling structural interdependencies and emergent network patterns, whereas egocentric, individual-level, research offers greater insight into actors’ perceptions, changing needs, and deliberate strategies for managing relationships—processes that are difficult to infer from tie trajectories alone. Egocentric findings can therefore guide sociocentric studies in deciding which individual experiences and strategies to measure and in theorizing whether they operate through tie creation, maintenance, or both; thereby connecting network-wide patterns to the individual processes generating them. Longitudinal sociocentric designs can carry these insights into bounded groups by observing both ends of ties, distinguishing actors’ tendencies to develop and maintain friendships from the relational behavior of those friends and other peers, as well as situating these changes within larger network processes and their complex interdependencies (e.g., triadic closure) and structures (e.g., the pool of potential partners). This makes it possible to investigate how patterned actor-level turnover accumulates into larger structures such as degree inequality, isolation, clustering, segregation, brokerage, and cohesion (Robins et al. 2005). Our study takes a first step by showing that lower depression-based tie maintenance can coexist with neutral or positive tie creation, such that comparable friendship volume can conceal systematically different levels of friendship stability.

These results call attention to the importance of network churn as a phenomenon. Instability in networks, whether through the loss of existing ties or gaining new ties, has an independent effect on well-being net of network size (Shepherd and Reich 2020). Churn may be driven by depressive symptoms, as we found, or a range of other attributes and experiences as other network studies have found. For example, Jacobsen (2020) shows that school suspension disrupts adolescent friendships while increasing affiliation with substance-using peers, producing churn without network isolation. Schaefer et al. (2021) found that first-year university students who engaged in weekly or binge drinking had greater friendship turnover, but also tended to have more friends. By contrast, Copeland et al. (2023) found that poorer adolescent health was associated with fewer friends, but the underlying process was poorer health leading to fewer new friendships, with no effect on friendship maintenance. Others have linked network turnover to delinquency (McGloin et al. 2008), socioeconomic status (Cornwell 2015), shyness (Yang et al. 2021), and social media use (Vriens and van Ingen 2017), among others. While network turnover is not a neglected topic, especially in egocentric analyses (e.g., Perry 2012; Perry and Pescosolido 2012; Small et al. 2015; Fischer and Offer 2020; Cornwell et al. 2021), less sociocentric work has focused on unraveling the network processes behind turnover and their consequences for emergent network structure.

Populations experiencing systematic tie churn hidden under stable tie volume can vary widely, going well beyond depressed youth (Perry 2012). For instance, evidence suggests that compared to non-migrants, repeat migrants report networks of comparable size but with different composition—less family-centric and more geographically dispersed networks (Wu et al. 2024), thus suggesting tie “substitution” (Small et al. 2015). Incarceration can produce a similar pattern: no long-term differences in network size but long-term differences in network stability, with formerly incarcerated older adults exhibiting larger changes in their network composition (Feng and Cornwell 2025). Relatedly, research shows that individuals maintain a characteristic distribution of communication efforts/events across their alters, which stays stable over time despite substantial turnover in who those alters are (Saramäki et al. 2014). Across these kinds of cases, tie-volume metrics would systematically understate churn and the wide range of meaningful social processes that give rise to it.

Our study also advances theories and models of network dynamics (Snijders et al. 2010; Krivitsky and Handcock 2014; Schaefer and Kreager 2020; Leal 2021). Network thinking has helped formalize the various processes that impede integration, such as by distinguishing self-isolation or withdrawal from peer exclusion (Schaefer et al. 2011). We theorized another dimension: differentials in either tie creation or maintenance. Many kinds of selection effects likely have a different effect at the tie creation versus the tie maintenance stage. For example, Newcomb (1961) theorized about the difference between visible and hidden attributes (e.g., values) in driving homophilous selection. Visible attributes are crude filters—important for new network ties—but fade in importance as relationships develop, becoming less consequential for tie persistence (Dahlander and McFarland 2013). Block (2014: 117) goes further by arguing that homophily only matters for tie creation, with tie persistence driven solely by endogenous network mechanisms (e.g., transitivity) that structurally “stabilise” a relationship in a way that being similar does not.

In that context, our study raises a broader question: Under what conditions does an attribute shape tie creation, tie maintenance, or both, especially for attribute dimensions other than visibility? In the case of depression, we argue that what matters most is not whether symptoms are directly visible or even consciously recognized, but how they manifest relationally during social interaction. Symptoms may create demands for reassurance or support that a relationship cannot meet (Starr and Davila 2008; Perry and Pescosolido 2012), prompt depressed individuals to proactively reorganize their ties strategically (e.g., pursuing self-reliance), or indirectly disrupt relationships via downstream consequences of mental illness (e.g., unemployment) (Link et al. 1989; Perry 2012, 2014). More work is needed to understand the conditions under which an attribute (or network mechanism more generally) matters for tie creation, tie maintenance, or both.

Some limitations in our empirical analyses warrant mention. First, our analysis did not distinguish whether adolescents with higher depressive symptoms repeatedly dissolve and re-form the same friendship nominations or cycle into new ones—though prior evidence suggests the latter (Chan and Poulin 2010). Second, we cannot distinguish the timing or ordering underlying tie turnover (e.g., whether a given nomination loss precedes a given addition or vice versa, or whether one change prompts the other). Third, we focus on directed friendship nominations, which are not friendships stricto sensu (Kitts and Leal 2021). Relatedly, it is possible that higher levels of depressive symptoms might be correlated with inaccuracies in friendship nominations, perhaps prompting mismatches between depressed egos’ actual and perceived friendships—though basic analyses suggest that our results remain qualitatively consistent when using reciprocated nominations only.28 Fourth, we focus on school-based networks, as schools remain adolescents’ primary peer socializing context (Crosnoe 2000; Giordano 2003; Kitts and Leal 2021). Yet, churning sociality could be domain-specific, with adolescents cycling through friendships at school while remaining stably embedded in, say, online or religious/youth groups. Fifth, the countries represented across our analyses (including the systematic review) are all in the Global North, limiting generalizability. For instance, cultural norms around emotional expression, including the consequences of disclosing depression or even the type of symptoms commonly associated with depression, can vary between countries and regions (Ryder et al. 2008). Sixth, Add Health School 3’s enclosing sociality pattern raises questions of how school ecology may moderate depression’s effects on sociality. Because an exceptionally large size, ethnoracial diversity, older student body, and potentially more mature and locally segmented friendship market co-occur in this school, we cannot identify which accounts for its divergent pattern of results or whether the pattern generalizes (see endnote 26 for details). Finally, we did not study clinical depression explicitly. The levels of elevated depression risk among the youth in our data were most likely subclinical. Hence, our findings do not speak to how the more severe symptoms of clinical depression affect sociality.

In closing, we argue that distinguishing tie maintenance and creation from tie volume matters because that distinction scales. At the micro level, those who experience high churn (e.g., depressed egos) see their access to emotionally sustaining ties diminished, which may alter access to important stress-buffering resources and support that stable ties can provide. At the meso level, high tie instability can hinder reciprocity, triadic closure, brokerage, and other network processes, ultimately eroding key relational dynamics that can sustain local norms and trust, group cohesion, and intergroup diffusion (Leal 2026). At the macro level, excessive levels of churn can affect the larger community structures that foster embeddedness and overall social integration. Thus, factors that selectively corrode tie maintenance or creation can threaten not only individual well-being, but the organizational and community cohesion built atop stable relationships. The findings from this study thus have broader implications beyond individual-level effects, adolescents, and depression. We encourage future work to further elaborate on these ideas.
