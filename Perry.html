Gendering Genetics: Biological Contingencies in the Protective Effects of Social Integration for Men and Women 1^{1}

Brea L. Perry

Indiana University

Evidence that social and biological processes are intertwined in producing health and human behavior is rapidly accumulating. Using a feminist approach, this research explores how gender moderates the interaction between biological processes and men’s and women’s behavioral and emotional responses to similar social environments. Using data from the Collaborative Study on the Genetics of Alcoholism, the influence of gender, social integration, and genetic risk on nicotine and alcohol dependence is examined. Three-way interaction models reveal gender-specific moderation of interactions between genetic risk score and social integration. Namely, being currently married and reporting positive social psychological integration are predictive of reduced risk of nicotine dependence among men with genetic susceptibility to strong nicotine cravings in the presence of social cues like stress. In contrast, the protective effects of marital status and social integration are substantially attenuated and absent, respectively, among women with high-risk genotypes. This pattern reflects the dualism (i.e., simultaneous costs and benefits) inherent in social integration for women, which may disproportionately affect those with a genetic sensitivity to stress. These findings contest the notion of genotype as static biological hardwiring that is independent from social and cultural systems of gender difference.

In the past decade, there has been significant interest in gene-environment interactions (G × E) and growing emphasis on determining “how genes get ‘outside the skin’ and environments get ‘inside the skin’” (Rutter 2009, p. 45). Yet, despite the promise of a “genomics revolution,” G × E research has fallen short of expectations for new etiological insights and targeted treatments, and findings have proven difficult to replicate (Conrad 1999; Moffitt, Caspi, and Rutter 2006; Seabrook and Avison 2010; Bookman et al. 2011). More important from a sociological standpoint, it is clear that social theory has rarely been extended or applied in G × E research in ways that either draw from or augment the understanding of social forces and processes.

Most conspicuous is the near absence of gender theory in research on G×EG \times E effects in health (see Short, Yang, and Jenkins [2013] for an excellent review), despite conclusions about the most promising candidate genes for major diseases often being specific to men or women. This raises concerns about the ways in which gender is being underplayed in G×EG \times E research. In the past, there has been understandable reluctance on the part of feminist scholars to accept or engage in research that could reify biological differences between men and women (Kennelly, Merz, and Lorber 2001; Risman 2001; Udry 2001). For decades, the distinction between sex and gender has delineated the domains of biology and social science, creating an intellectual space for the study of socially constructed difference and inequality. However, sociologists must now confront the accumulating body of evidence suggesting that experiences correlated with gender interact with biology to produce behavioral and health outcomes (Rinn and Snyder 2005; Cosgrove, Mazure, and Staley 2007).

Along these lines, feminist scholars have recently argued for moving beyond the sex/gender dichotomy (Fausto-Sterling 2005; van Ommen and van Deventer 2012). Fausto-Sterling calls sex “the Achilles’ heel of 1970s feminism” (2005, p. 1493), observing that relegating biological differences between men and women to the natural sciences has undersold the role of culture in producing those differences. Nearly every biological system, from genetics (Rinn and Snyder 2005) to neurology (Fine 2010), is susceptible to influence and modification by features of our gendered social environment (Lorber 1993). In other words, by creating a distinction between sex and gender, second-wave feminists left largely unexamined the possibility that “our bodies physically imbibe culture” (Fausto-Sterling 2005, p. 1494).

While sociologists studying gene-environment interactions have seldom explicitly taken up gender matters (for exceptions, see Guo et al. [2007]; Boardman, Blalock, and Button [2008]; Pescosolido et al. [2008]; Perry et al. [2013]), this group of scholars has been at the forefront of efforts to establish a site for studying meaningful overlap between sex and gender (e.g., Freese 2008; Seabrook and Avison 2010). Shanahan and Hofer (2005) caution against ignoring social categories that reflect systematic inequalities in risks and resources, including gender. They advocate for more sophisticated modeling of gene-environment interactions that accurately reflects the contingencies and complexities of social environments. Specifically, they recommend accounting for moderation of G × E by a second “environmental” variable, arguing that patterned differences in the susceptibility and consequences of social experiences for men and women may lead to a G × E × E effect (here, G × E × gender). This proposition presents enormous potential for the ascendancy of gender theory in transdisciplinary agendas on health and behavior.

While Shanahan and Hofer’s (2005) arguments present a compelling opportunity for meaningful incorporation of gender in biomedical research, we have few examples of how complex G×E×G \times E \times gender relationships might play out empirically (for exceptions, see Boardman et al. [2008]; Perry et al. [2013]). As better tools for identifying complex mechanisms are developed, it is critical that sociologists respond in kind with increased specificity in theory and statistical modeling. Sociologists bring to the study of G×EG \times E a nuanced understanding of the ways in which gender not only stratifies individuals into unique “environments” (e.g., experiences, roles, relationships) but also explains why even similar social environments can differentially shape men’s and women’s well-being (Short et al. 2013; Perry 2014). Moreover, a social epidemiological approach to G×EG \times E expands the concept of the environment beyond the individual, incorporating upstream normative and cultural forces that may moderate genetic influences and critically influence health and behavior (Boardman, Daw, and Freese 2013).

Here, drawing on gender theory, I empirically test Shanahan and Hofer’s (2005) assertion that interactions between biological and social environmental factors are contingent on social structural position. More specifically, using data from the Collaborative Study on the Genetics of Alcoholism (COGA) and three-way interaction models, I explore gender-specific moderation of a GABAergic genetic risk score by marital status and social psychological integration (i.e., G×E×G \times E \times gender effects) in alcohol, nicotine, and comorbid substance dependence. GABA receptor genes confer risk for substance use disorders through reward pathways that increase maladaptive behavioral responses to stress and other environmental cues (Dick, Bierut, et al. 2006, 2010; Philibert et al. 2009; Villafuerte et al. 2012; Janes et al. 2013). At the same time, research on gender suggests that women and men may experience social integration differently, bringing gendered meanings, values, and expectations to social and community ties. Consequently, I hypothesize that the protective functions of social integration experiences will vary as a function of gender and genetic risk.

THEORETICAL BACKGROUND

For the purposes of this research, gender is defined as “an institutionalized system of social practices for constituting people as two significantly different categories” (Ridgeway and Correll 2004, p. 510). In Framed by Gender, Cecelia Ridgeway (2011) argues that gender difference is created and maintained through social relations that shape individuals’ sense of self. In everyday social interactions, individuals must define themselves in relation to others in order to decide how to behave. Ridgeway argues that even decisions and actions that are undertaken “individually” are social interactional if people imagine themselves in relation to others and feel that they will be socially evaluated. She contends that “the use of gender as a framing device spreads gendered meanings…to all spheres of social life that are carried out through social relationships” (p. 7). Because people use sex and gender as a primary frame for organizing social relations, and therefore for shaping their own behavior and sense of self, gender difference is continually incorporated into emerging social structures (Ridgeway 2011).

Here, I argue that the powerful and ubiquitous nature of gender as a frame for interpreting, experiencing, and behaving in the social environment explains why biological realities like genetic coding can have opposing outcomes for men and women. In other words, gender structures men’s and women’s health and well-being through social as well as biological pathways. Biological susceptibilities interact with gendered social relations that shape patterns of exposure to stress and other risk factors, differential access to resources for improving or maintaining health, and the unique meanings that various structural positions and experiences have for men and women (Simon 1995; Bird and Rieker 2008; Read and Gorman 2010).

A wealth of research demonstrates that social ties, broadly defined, are largely protective of health (Cohen 2004; Smith and Christakis 2008; Umberson and Montez 2010). I use the term “social integration” in the Durkheimian sense to refer to boundedness to social groups and therefore to group values, norms, and goals. In line with Moen, Dempster-McLain, and Williams (1989), I draw a distinction between structural and social psychological integration. The former reflects the specific number and characteristics of social ties that a person possesses and is operationalized here by marital status. Being married is associated with a range of physical and mental health benefits relative to being never married or formerly married (Williams 2003; Rendall et al. 2011). I also consider the influence of social psychological integration, or the subjective experience of being embedded in social groups and relationships. Social psychological integration typically fosters a sense of comfort, security, and belongingness that buffers both real and perceived threats to well-being and contributes to positive feelings of self-worth (Lin and Peek 1999). Both structural and social psychological integration encourage prosocial behavior because well-integrated individuals feel a sense of obligation to conduct themselves in ways that benefit their social group.

However, the degree to which social integration is protective of health depends on the meaning and salience of social ties to individuals. Gendered meanings are attached to the performance of particular tasks, including the maintenance of social, family, and community relationships. For women, the meaning and lived experience of family and community include an element of work—both physical work (e.g., child care, elder care, volunteering) and emotion work (e.g., offering appreciation and encouragement, soothing and providing emotional support, expressing empathy). Compared to men, women feel disproportionately responsible for cultivating relationships within and on behalf of their families, producing gendered norms of accountability that make them more cognitively and emotionally invested in those relationships, for better or worse (West and Zimmerman 1987; Erickson 2005). The gendered nature of relationships leads to a duality in which women are more likely to experience and be affected by both the positive (e.g., emotional support) and negative (e.g., caregiver burden) aspects of social integration (Kessler and McLeod 1984; Turner 1994; Wicki 1999).

In contrast, the meanings that social integration hold for men are not framed by a sense of obligation and do not carry the same cognitive and emotional significance that they do for women (Nolen-Koeksema and Jackson 2001). Men are largely the beneficiaries of instrumental and emotional support in relationships with women rather than the givers (Wellman and Wortley 1990; Umberson et al. 1996). Moreover, social and family relationships for men carry few of the value-laden pressures and demands that accompany primary or sole responsibility for home, family, and community. For example, research suggests that men’s participation in family interactions is characterized by leisure and play and women’s by caretaking tasks and other forms of work (Hochschild 1997; Craig 2006; Craig and Mullan 2011). Similarly, women are disproportionately likely to provide unpaid services in churches, schools, neighborhoods, and community organizations, engaging in social capital-building work that confers benefits to the family as a whole (Campbell and Lee 1990; Wellman and Wortley 1990; Naples 1992). Thus, the ideology of social integration—including who is responsible for cultivating and maintaining social ties and who ends up benefiting from them—is a reflection of the reproduction of gender within families and communities (Ridgeway and Correll 2000) and can have divergent health consequences for men and women.

Social integration undoubtedly contributes to women’s feelings of self-worth and positive affect (Monin and Clark 2011). Nonetheless, the cognitive and emotional investment and work associated with women’s primary responsibility for maintaining social ties may lead to stress that partially mitigates the beneficial health effects of social integration for some (Kiecolt-Glaser and Newton 2001). Though findings are inconsistent (see, e.g., Umberson et al. 1996; Williams 2003), numerous studies have found greater or exclusive health benefits of social integration or support for men relative to women (House, Robbins, and Metzner 1982; Orth-Gomer and Johnson 1987; Kaplan et al. 1988; Kirschbaum et al. 1995; Shye et al. 1995; Kiecolt-Glaser and Newton 2001; Scafato et al. 2008; Rendall et al. 2011). For example, using a nationally representative sample, Seeman et al. (2002) found that greater structural integration (i.e., larger social networks and more emotional support) was associated with lower allostatic load (a measure of the physiological damage caused by chronic stress) for men, but not for women.

Along the same lines, research suggests that women’s social and family responsibilities often compete with the obligations of other structural positions, and efforts to simultaneously manage multiple commitments can cause psychological distress (Aneshensel, Pearlin, and Schuler 1993; Moen, Robison, and Dempster-McClain 1995; Durden, Hill, and Angel 2007). Studies reveal gender differences in the influence of multiple roles on mental and physical health outcomes, demonstrating substantial health benefits for men and comparatively small advantages or adverse effects among women (Adelmann 1994; Simon 1995). Similarly, women are more likely than men to experience psychological distress in response to parental role strains because of the greater salience of the parental role for women (Simon 1992). Thus, even when men and women occupy the same structural positions, they bring gendered expectations, identities, and resources to social relations that differentially affect their stress reactions and health outcomes (Simon 1992, 1995). Consequently, it is problematic to assume gender neutrality in the effects of social integration on health.

Gender-Moderated G × E in Disease Pathways

Despite patterns of gender difference in the effects of social integration on health, there is substantial variation in the health-promoting properties of social connectedness within gender categories as well. This suggests that other social or biological factors—including genetics—may moderate this relationship. This is a critical point for G×EG \times E research. Men and women with and without genetic sensitivities to environmental stressors may have divergent responses to the same measures of social environment, particularly if there are gender differences in the meanings and consequences attached to those environments.

Interest in the interplay between genes and environments has often focused on genetic sensitivity to stress (Daw et al. 2013; Mitchell et al. 2013). In these cases, the combination of a harmful environment and genetic sensitivity results in increased risk for adverse outcomes, while the same genotype may have no identifiable influence on disease in the context of protective environmental conditions (Shanahan and Hofer 2005). This model of social and biological interaction is of particular interest to sociologists because the impact of genetic sensitivity on health is often entirely contingent on the quality of the social environment. Because the social environment changes over the life course and across historical and cultural locations, the relationship between genetic coding and environments is complex, dynamic, and dependent on the particular social locations of the individuals being studied (Boardman et al. 2013).

Recently, there has been skepticism about the validity and utility of G×EG \times E research, which may derive from a failure to adequately account for complexities such as the moderating effects of gender and other social categories. One critical review presents evidence of strong publication bias toward positive findings in G×EG \times E research, particularly when samples are underpowered to detect small interaction effects (Duncan and Keller 2011). On the basis of this, the authors posit that most positive candidate gene-environment interactions are type I errors (i.e., false positives). However, others remain more optimistic, noting that failure to replicate G×EG \times E effects does not necessarily indicate that findings are an artifact of chance. Rather, the replication problem may reflect a compound genetic architecture that is further obscured by an even more complex environment (Greene et al. 2009). Most notably, interactions between multiple genes and environments can render even strong general G×EG \times E effects nearly impossible to detect in independent samples.

Along these lines, Shanahan and Hofer (2005) argue that models that test the interaction of genotype and only one proximal indicator of the social environment are overly simplistic. The authors note that “the specific function of the interaction may change across subgroups. These subgroups could be defined . . . by distinctive social experiences” (p. 71). In one of the only empirical examples of this phenomenon, Boardman et al. (2008) find evidence using data on twin pairs that gender differences in the heritability of resilience derive from benefits of environmental mastery that accrue disproportionately to men. The authors speculate that gender influences the genetic epidemiology of resilience through social experiences that differentially structure the psychosocial resources of men and women. Likewise, when there are patterned differences in the way social environments are experienced by men and women, G × E analyses may lead to inaccurate conclusions that ignore gender or reify a sex/gender distinction.

Consequently, in cases in which social “environments” are reflective of gender difference, hypotheses on G×EG \times E must be gender specific (i.e., G×E×G \times E \times gender). Gendered patterns of gene-environment interaction may be observed because men and women are systematically stratified into different environments (e.g., gender segregation into different careers and work statuses). Additionally, men and women may have qualitatively different experiences even when they are embedded in the same environments. Finally, men and women may have different responses to the same experiences. For instance, research suggests that women are more physiologically and subjectively reactive to stressful events and conditions compared to men, on average (Kudielka and Kirschbaum 2005). In such cases, environments are likely to have unique interactions with genotype for men and women, resulting in a G×EG \times E that operates differently across gender (Perry 2014).

The Empirical Case: Gender, Social Integration, and GABA Receptor Genes in Substance Use Disorders

The empirical case examined here is gender-specific moderation of the interaction between single nucleotide polymorphisms in GABA receptor genes and social integration in nicotine and alcohol dependence. This is an ideal case for testing G×E×G \times E \times gender effects for three reasons. First, these substance use outcomes reflect varying degrees of gender difference in rates of abuse and dependence, which have historically been higher among men than women. A fairly large gender disparity is observed for alcohol dependence, which affects men at about twice the rate of women (Keyes et al. 2008). Regular use of nicotine products is also higher among men (33%) than women (21%), though this is less pronounced than gender differences in alcohol dependence (SAMHSA 2013). These patterns are important because sufficient variation on outcomes for both men and women is needed to detect three-way interactions involving gender.

Second, GABA receptor genes are among the most promising candidates linked to substance use disorders. The increased risk of nicotine and alcohol phenotypes associated with genetic variants in the GABA system is moderate but sufficiently large to be detectable in regression models (Edenberg et al. 2004; Agrawal et al. 2006; Dick, Plunkett, et al. 2006, 2009; Philibert et al. 2009; Enoch et al. 2010) and has been confirmed using met-analysis (Li et al. 2014). Also, the high-risk variant is widely distributed and is equally represented in different gender groups.

Third, GABA receptor genes confer increased risk for substance use disorders through disinhibition coupled with heightened response to environmental cues (Koob 2004; Beuten et al. 2005; Grucza and Bierut 2006; Enoch 2008). The GABA system plays a major role in impulsivity and increased craving in response to stress (Enoch et al. 2006; Dai et al. 2009; Villafuerte et al. 2012). GABA receptor genes encode production of five subunits of the GABA-A receptor protein. This protein product is a binding site for GABA, the principal inhibitory neurotransmitter in the human nervous system (Edenberg et al. 2004). People with low levels of GABA acting in the brain are susceptible to sudden and unplanned behaviors or decisions that are carried out without thinking through the consequences. The GABA system is thought to be related to a specific form of impulsivity that is characterized by experiencing quick and unplanned responses to heightened emotional states, including stress (Kreek et al. 2005). Thus, someone with decreased GABA activity in the brain is more likely to initiate behaviors such as smoking and drinking alcohol in response to stress because these have short-term rewards. In addition, a sluggish GABA system increases sensitivity to the presence of smoking and drinking cues, leading to strong cravings (D’Souza and Markou 2013; Janes et al. 2013). Consequently, high-risk variants of GABA receptor genes increase neurological susceptibility to compulsive smoking and drinking as a method for coping with heightened emotional states (Dick et al. 2010; Villafuerte et al. 2012).

In empirical research, social relationships have been found to moderate the impact of GABA receptor genes. Pescosolido et al. (2008) found that the high-risk GABRA2 genotype is associated with increased risk for alcohol dependence in the presence of weak family social support, but not among those with strong family support. Dick et al. (2009) found a similar pattern among differentially sensitive youths subjected to high or low levels of parental monitoring, suggesting a social control function. Likewise, high-risk GABA genotypes are predictive of substance use behaviors in the presence of early and frequent exposure to drugs and alcohol through family and peers (Philibert et al. 2009). Thus, in social environments characterized by high levels of social integration and regulation, individuals with high-risk GABA receptor genotypes exhibit healthier behavior vis-à-vis substance use. Conversely, when people are embedded in more permissive or negative social groups and contexts, the risk for substance misuse and dependence is high.

Combining insights from gender theory with the body of research on the GABA system raises the possibility that interactions between GABA receptor genes and social integration are gender specific (i.e., a gene-environment-gender interaction, or G×E×G \times E \times gender). That is, for men with strong genetic risk factors, higher levels of rewarding social integration may serve as a stress-buffering factor and a form of social control. However, social integration is likely to come with high costs for women, even if it is often rewarding, reducing the protective effects of social embeddedness. In contrast, women with low genetic risk may be more resilient to the responsibilities and chronic strains associated with strong integration and capable of benefiting from its positive social psychological effects. These patterns lead to the following hypotheses:

HYPOTHESIS 1.—Genetic risk for disinhibition and sensitivity to stress associated with the GABA system will be associated with higher odds of meeting criteria for nicotine and alcohol dependence.

HYPOTHESIS 2.—Structural and social psychological integration will be associated with lower odds of meeting criteria for nicotine and alcohol dependence.

HYPOTHESIS 3a.—Within the high genetic risk group, the protective effects of social integration will operate exclusively or more strongly among men compared to women.

HYPOTHESIS 3b.—Within the low genetic risk group, social integration will confer a similar level of protection for men and women.

Using data from the Collaborative Study on the Genetics of Alcoholism, I examine the influence of gender, social integration, and genetic risk on nicotine and alcohol dependence. Three-way interaction models reveal gender-specific moderation of interactions between genetic risk score and social integration, as measured by marital status and a social psychological integration scale. I hypothesize that being currently married and reporting positive social psychological integration will be predictive of reduced risk of nicotine and alcohol dependence among men with higher genetic risk. In contrast, the protective effects of marital status and social integration will be attenuated among women with high-risk genotypes.

METHODS

Sample

Data are from the Collaborative Study on the Genetics of Alcoholism (COGA), a study funded by the National Institute on Alcohol Abuse and Alcoholism to identify and map genes associated with alcohol dependence, other substance use disorders, and related maladaptive patterns of behavior (e.g., depression, antisocial personality disorder). Data were collected at nine venues between 1989 and 1999. A follow-up data collection occurred approximately five years later, between 1997 and 2004 (Edenberg et al. 2004). Since the measures of social integration used here were not collected until the follow-up phase, the analyses are limited accordingly.

COGA utilizes a case-control methodology that relies on a complex, availability-based, family selection strategy (Hesselbrock et al. 2001). To ensure sufficient variation on genetic risk, persons with alcohol dependence were the initial point of contact for recruitment. However, family members were later recruited, many of whom did not meet criteria for substance use disorders. In the final analysis sample, 38% of respondents had alcohol dependence. This family-based strategy for examining genetic linkage and G × E has a number of important advantages over population-based sampling (e.g., they are robust against population substructure and have a lower probability of detecting false positive results; Laird and Lange 2006).

Families that included persons diagnosed with alcohol dependence were systematically recruited from consecutive admissions to alcohol treatment facilities. Families were recruited via a focal respondent who met criteria for alcohol dependence and who had at least two first-degree relatives living within a 150-mile radius of the COGA catchment areas. Focal respondents and their biological relatives over age 6 who agreed to participate completed a structured psychiatric interview, provided extensive data on drug and alcohol use, completed a battery of standardized personality trait measures, and provided blood samples for DNA analysis. Another group of families, designated as community controls, were selected from church congregations, large corporations, dental clinics, driver’s license bureaus, and health maintenance organizations. Focal respondents in control families were not selected on the basis of alcohol dependence. All control families had two parents and at least three biological children over the age of 14, and all family members over the age of 6 were evaluated. A detailed description of COGA study methodology and measures is available elsewhere (Edenberg 2002).

Owing to the reliance on nonrandom sampling through alcohol treatment programs, selection bias raises concerns about generalizability. This issue is partially mitigated by recruitment of family members and control cases without alcohol dependence, increasing the variability of the sample and similarity to nontreated individuals with alcohol dependence. Moreover, nicotine dependence is also used here as an outcome, reducing these concerns. Because the COGA sample is nonrandom, significance tests should be interpreted with caution.

These procedures yielded a sample of 10,295 subjects at follow-up. Variables

Substance dependence.—The Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (4th ed.; DSM-IV), published by the American Psychiatric Association (1994), defines substance dependence as a long-term pattern of maladaptive use with clinically significant behavioral, cognitive, or physiological impairment. These analyses employ two distinct substance dependence outcomes—nicotine and alcohol dependence. These are indexed by the assignment of subjects who meet lifetime DSM-IV criteria for dependence. Dependence is coded 1 if the respondent is classified as dependent according to these criteria and 0 otherwise. Lifetime diagnoses are used here because substance use disorders are considered trait rather than state variables in genetic research and are thus stable over time (Dettling et al. 1995).

Because nicotine and alcohol dependence have been shown to share common genetic antecedents, including single nucleotide polymorphisms in GABA receptor genes (Agrawal et al. 2006; Dick, Bierut, et al. 2006; Dick, Viken, et al. 2007), the role of comorbidity is examined. A binary variable is coded 1 if the respondent meets diagnostic criteria for nicotine and alcohol dependence and 0 otherwise. Another binary variable measures any dependence (nicotine or alcohol) and is used only for the purposes of identifying risk genes for inclusion in the genetic risk score.

Genetic risk score.—Genetic risk is indexed using a hypothesis-driven, literature-based polygenic risk scale (Belsky and Israel 2014): a common method used in research assessing genetic susceptibility to disease in which each individual single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) has a very small effect (e.g., Paynter et al. 2010; Simons et al. 2014). This method for measuring genetic risk has been advocated for use by social scientists interested in gene-environment interactions and correlations (Belsky et al. 2013; Belsky and Israel 2014) and has been used to empirically demonstrate social environmental moderation of genetic risk in recent research (e.g., Meyers et al. 2013; Peyrot et al. 2014).

Sociodemographic characteristics.—Sex/Gender is coded 1 for women and 0 otherwise. Since sex typically overlaps with gendered social experiences, being male or female also taps into issues of gender. Income is measured as the log of household income in tens of thousands of dollars. For the small number of cases containing missing data on household income (n = 82), the value provided in the previous wave of COGA is imputed. The variable Age is measured in years. Alternative coding strategies and use of other sociodemographic control variables do not alter the effects of key variables of interest (i.e., gender, genotype, marital status, and social integration).

Social integration.—Social integration is measured using two variables. First, the presence of a marital partner is used as an indicator of structural integration and therefore of social and economic obligation to at least one other individual. Marital status is coded into three categories: currently married, never married, and formerly married (i.e., separated, widowed, or divorced). Both the never married and formerly married categories are indicative of lower levels of social integration.

Social psychological integration, or the subjective experience of being embedded in social groups and relationships, is measured using a scale. The scale is a subset of a 52-item index that measures day-to-day events that could potentially induce or mitigate stress (DeLongis et al. 1982). To explore the extent to which positive emotions associated with social embeddedness are structured by the gendered nature of social relations, a subscale was created by the author by extracting items capturing subjective responses to social and family relationships (see Moen et al. 1989). The social integration scale includes nine items: children, parents, other relatives, time spent with family, spouse/partner, friends, fellow workers, church and community, and neighborhood ( α=0.66\alpha = 0.66 ). These items measure how uplifting, pleasurable, or fulfilling these were in the last week. Response categories range from none or not applicable, coded 0, to a great deal, coded 3. The scale score is the average of all nonmissing items for which higher values are associated with higher levels of social integration and ranges from 0 to 3.

To determine the robustness of the findings when different coding strategies are used for social integration, two additional scales are created. A restricted social integration scale contains five items: only those that pertain to close social relationships (children, parents, other relatives, spouse/partner, and friends) rather than social domains such as work, church, community, and neighborhood. A broad social interaction scale contains 16 items and includes the original scale in addition to all items that could potentially be conceptualized as measuring social relationships: sex, intimacy, health of family members, financial care for others, supervisor, clients, and pets. These were omitted from the original scale because they might be tapping into indicators that are related to social integration but could result in confounding effects, such as marital quality (e.g., sex, intimacy) or economic security (e.g., financial care for others, clients).

To test whether gender-specific G×EG \times E patterns are simply a function of measurement error or other differences in the way in which men and women respond to questions about aspects of their lives that are burdensome or rewarding, two final scales are included in sensitivity analyses. First, I conduct sensitivity analyses using the full 52-item DeLongis Daily

Hassles and Uplifts scale (DeLongis et al. 1982). Second, I use a placebo scale that contains eight items that are unlikely, at face value, to be gender specific in their effects: the weather, the news, the environment, political issues, conservation, home entertainment, free time, and outside recreation. Placebo regressions are used to test for associations that should not exist if the relationship between two variables is causal (i.e., a “placebo effect”; see Wildeman 2010). In this analysis, I test for a gender-specific G × E effect of daily life experiences that are unlikely to have gender-specific consequences. If a significant relationship is identified, it indicates that the relationship may be spurious and something other than gendered responses to social integration (e.g., another social or genetic factor) is driving the three-way interaction between gender, genotype, and stressful or rewarding daily life experiences.

The variables described above do not reflect the full complexity of either genetic or social mechanisms in substance use disorders. For example, the effects of high-risk GABA receptor genotypes may be contingent on other genes or gene systems (i.e., epistasis, or G×G×EG \times G \times E effects) not examined in this analysis (Enoch et al. 2010; Young-Wolff, Enoch, and Prescott 2011). Likewise, variables tapping into social integration were not designed for this purpose and do not capture role salience, commitment, or strain in adequate depth or breadth. For example, the social integration scale does not distinguish absence of pleasure from absence of social relations, potentially conflating low structural social integration and low social psychological integration. In the future, gender-specific moderation of genetic sensitivity by social integration should be examined using measures designed for this purpose.

Analytic Approach

The associations between gender, genetic risk, social integration, and substance dependence are modeled with random-effects logistic regression models (Rabe-Hesketh and Skrondal 2008; StataCorp 2015) estimated with StataCorp’s (2013) xtlogit command. This model adjusts for the lack of independence among observations as a result of having multiple individuals from the same family. These models reflect individuals (level 1) nested in families (level 2) and contain family-level (i.e., cluster-specific) random intercepts.

To identify the presence of any gene-environment correlations, the relationships between genotype and sociodemographic characteristics are examined using random-intercept regression. These analyses are conducted using a subsample of respondents without nicotine or alcohol dependence to identify any associations between genetic risk and social factors that are not confounded by the effects of having substance use disorders.

To assess whether the effects of genetic risk and G×EG \times E interactions on substance dependence vary by gender, a series of models using a pooled sample are conducted. A set of regression models examines the gendered nature of marriage and emotional responses to social integration. For each outcome, model I includes only the key variables of interest: gender, genetic risk, and structural or social psychological integration, testing hypotheses 1 and 2. Model II adds control variables (race, age, and socioeconomic status); and model III tests hypotheses 3a and 3b and adds a three-way interaction term for genetic risk ×\times gender ×\times social integration (and constitutive terms). I also run a separate model including only two-way interactions to determine whether a G×EG \times E would be detectable if not conditioned on gender. Results are not presented in tables because no two-way interactions approach significance.

For significant three-way interaction models, group-specific effects are included in the text and tables to facilitate interpretation. In addition, figures of predicted probabilities are presented to demonstrate the magnitude of three-way interaction effects. In logit models, Chow-type tests of the equality of coefficients across groups are unreliable since they confound the magnitude of the effect for each group with group differences in residual variation (Allison 1999). Predicted probabilities across groups, however, are unaffected by the confounding of the slope coefficients and error variances (Long 2009). Consequently, the statistical significance of differences in predicted probabilities within genotype groups and across gender is identified using Long’s (2009) delta method and constitute a conservative approach to assessing the presence of significant three-way interactions in logit models. All three-way interactions presented in tables and figures are statistically significant according to this criterion.

In addition, a variety of supplemental analyses are conducted to support conclusions about three-way interactions and to reduce concerns about type I errors. First, all models are replicated using a restricted sample that does not include alcohol-dependent focal respondents. This addresses limitations associated with sampling through alcohol treatment facilities. Second, regressions are replicated using a restricted sample that omits all respondents reporting levels of social psychological integration that fall outside two standard deviations above or below the mean for this scale. This reduces concerns about three-way interactions being driven by strong findings among the most extreme cases. Third, all regression models are replicated using different strategies for coding social integration. Fourth, analyses are conducted with various controls for the family-based structure of the sample because generational or shared environmental influences associated with being raised in the same nuclear family could confound genetic effects. Also, it is possible that the strength of genetic or social effects varies across generations. To address this possibility, all analyses are conducted with and without controls for respondents’ generation (i.e., grandparents, parents, children) and sibling status, as well as interactions between these variables and genetic effects. 3^{3} Also, three-way interaction models are replicated with a subsample that excludes third-generation (i.e., the youngest) respondents, and sibling fixed-effects models are conducted that compare siblings within the same families to one another. Finally, a post hoc power analysis suggests that with a .05 error probability, the sample is adequately powered (0.89) to detect the observed gender- and genetic risk-specific effects of social integration. Despite these extensive sensitivity analyses, the specific interpretive framework provided here is merely suggestive of one plausible mechanism underlying patterns in these data.

Robustness of Findings

Supplemental sensitivity analyses reinforce the conclusions regarding nicotine dependence and reduce concerns about false positive results. Replication of the above regressions using restricted samples provides support for these findings. Analyses using a sample that omits alcohol-dependent focal respondents n=4,592n = 4,592 indicate that the three-way interaction results for social psychological integration are similar in magnitude and remain statistically significant. The interaction of gender, genotype, and marital status becomes marginally significant, but the pattern remains the same. These findings lend support to the conclusions of the study and indicate that patterns are not driven by the inclusion of a clinical sample.

Regression models are also replicated using a sample that omits all respondents reporting levels of social integration that fall outside two standard deviations above or below the mean for this scale n=5,242n = 5,242 . Findings are very similar in magnitude to those using the full sample, remain significant according to the delta method, and are identical with regard to substantive conclusions. These findings reduce concerns about three-way interactions involving social psychological integration being driven by strong effects observed among only the most extreme cases.

To determine whether the effects of emotional responses to social integration are an artifact of strategically selecting particular items from the full DeLongis scale to achieve significant results, alternative coding is tested. Substituting a restricted scale that includes only items pertaining to specific, close relationships produces a pattern of results that is slightly stronger than the original scale used here and remains statistically significant. Using a broad scale that adds all items that could potentially be proxies for social integration also produces very similar results, with the exception that the three-way interaction becomes only marginally significant. Analyses are also replicated using a placebo scale that contains items that are unlikely to produce gendered responses (e.g., the weather, the news) and using the full DeLongis scale. If these produce similar results, it suggests that something other than gendered responses to social integration is driving the patterns identified here (e.g., a measurement artifact). Neither the placebo nor the full scale produces a gender-moderated G×EG \times E , and the patterns of group-specific effects diverge substantially from those obtained using the three versions of the integration scale.

This study provides an empirical example of the critical importance in G×EG \times E research of considering more complex pathways in which gender is a central component of the theoretical framework and analysis. It is important to note that two-way gene-environment interaction effects never achieve statistical significance in these models, nor do interactions between gender and genotype. This suggests that gender moderation can mask even strong G×EG \times E findings in cases in which responses to social environments are likely to differ for men and women.

DISCUSSION

In this research, I argue that gender shapes health outcomes through both social and biological mechanisms, problematizing the distinction between sex and gender or nature and nurture (Fausto-Sterling 2005). Drawing on Ridgeway’s (2011) theory of gender-framed social relations, this article responds to calls for gender research that asks “old questions in new ways so that we can think systematically about the interweaving of bodies and culture” (Fausto-Sterling 2005, p. 1517). The “old question” is, How do gender differences in social relations affect health? The novel development represents a reframing of sex and biology as components of integrated systems that can and should be studied using a gender lens: How are gender differences in social relations moderated by genetic processes to affect health?

Findings from this analysis of the Collaborative Studies on the Genetics of Alcoholism provide support for the theory outlined at the outset: systematic gender differences in social relations differentially shape men’s and women’s health through the interaction between social and biological risk. More specifically, results reveal significant gender-specific interactions between a genetic risk score and both structural and social psychological integration in predicting nicotine dependence. Namely, consistent with hypotheses 3a and 3b, higher levels of social integration are associated with decreased risk of dependence on nicotine products among men with strong genetic risk factors and, to a lesser degree, among women with low genetic risk. However, higher levels of integration are not protective of nicotine dependence for women with strong genetic risk factors.

The gender-specific interaction is convergent both with gender theory and with biomedical research on the GABA system. Individuals with high-risk genotypes on GABA receptor genes are more susceptible to strong craving for nicotine in the presence of smoking cues, including stress, and find it more difficult to resist the urge to use nicotine products under duress (Kreek et al. 2005; D’Souza and Markou 2013; Janes et al. 2013). Men who are highly socially integrated probably experience social support, feelings of self-worth, and a sense of obligation to loved ones that serve stress-buffering and social control functions in the Durkheimian sense. For men with strong GABAergic risk factors, integration into families and communities—where a system of gendered social relations privileges them—is protective, reducing the impact of biological susceptibility. Alternatively, men who are unmarried or who report few positive social and family experiences may feel disconnected, purposeless, unregulated, or stressed by negative relationships, initiating a cycle of stress and anxiety-reducing nicotine use that is likely to be particularly potent among those in the high genetic risk group.

In contrast, there is a duality inherent in social integration for women, for whom social ties carry added significance for one’s gendered sense of self. Consequently, the strong protective effect of social integration for men with strong genetic risk factors is not present among similarly susceptible women. Because women largely carry the burden of care work and community keeping, often in addition to working for pay, high levels of integration and commitment to family and community roles can create conflict and ambivalence toward social obligations (Lachance-Grzela and Bouchard 2010). Women with excessive social demands may smoke in response to being overwhelmed by competing responsibilities, particularly if they are biologically susceptible to stress. This explanation is consistent with social and demographic research suggesting that marriage may confer fewer health benefits for women relative to men (Kiecolt-Glaser and Newton 2001; Scafato et al. 2008; Rendall et al. 2011). In contrast, women lacking high-risk GABA receptor genes may be more resilient to the normal responsibilities and chronic strains associated with social ties and thus capable of benefiting from marriage and social integration. However, this explanation is entirely speculative because the data necessary to test these mechanisms are not available in COGA.

Another potential factor to consider is the meaning of social integration. Research suggests that despite the costs of social integration for women, they tend to minimize the burden of social relationships and emphasize the rewards of integration. According to Hochschild (1979), “feeling norms” shape the range, intensity, and targets of emotion that one should feel in a given situation, and these are highly gendered. Consequently, women may downplay the negative aspects of relationship work, “not only because it is expected to be a spontaneous expression of love, but also because the illusion of effortlessness is part of doing the work well” (Erickson 2005, p. 338). Experiencing the stress associated with social integration for women is itself inconsistent with a gendered sense of self, because performing and enjoying this work is a key aspect of what it means to be a woman. Thus, because emotions themselves are a social product of a gendered system of relations, the social integration scale for women may be more reflective of the salience of social psychological integration or the degree of structural integration than the positive or negative subjective responses to social ties.

Though not without limitations, this research makes unique contributions to the literature on biosociology and gene-environment interactions. This is one of the first empirical tests of Shanahan and Hofer’s (2005) contention that G × E interactions may be additionally moderated by social status that uses direct measures of genotype (see also Perry et al. 2013). That this kind of research has rarely been undertaken before may be due to the limited number of data sets that are adequately statistically powered to support three-way interaction models. Of particular concern are small cell sizes, wherein insufficient numbers of individuals are affected (e.g., have alcohol dependence) across the continuum of genetic risk. These are common problems in nationally representative studies using probability sampling and reflect the primary advantage of case-control methodology for research involving gene-environment interactions. In short, the combination of extensive genotype information, acceptable measures of social integration, and adequate variation and statistical power on independent and dependent variables of interest makes large-scale genetic studies like COGA among the few sources of data with which to test complex hypotheses like those addressed here.

Methodological and Theoretical Implications

One of the most important implications to emerge from this research is that the complex effects of social integration are evident only when gender-specific interactions are examined. The two-way interaction effects between gender and genotype and between social integration and genotype revealed no significant moderating effects of gender or social environment, underestimating the effects of both social and biological risk factors. Moreover, the two-way interactions masked the most important finding, namely, that the gendered nature of one social environment can produce different G×EG \times E patterns of nicotine dependence for men and women with genetic vulnerabilities. Thus, gender differences in the effects of social experiences (i.e., E×E \times gender interactions) effectively negated significant G×EG \times E patterns.

These results also underscore the need to consider gender-specific social pathways in G×EG \times E research, providing empirical support for Shanahan and Hofer’s (2005) appeal for complex models that go beyond the interaction of genotype and only one indicator of the social environment. As these authors claimed, the role of the environment in moderating the phenotypic expression of genotype may be complicated by patterned social heterogeneity that influences gene-environment interactions. While not all (and perhaps not even most) gene-environment interactions will be moderated by gender, at minimum it is important to conduct G×EG \times E tests separately by gender to ensure similar patterns across groups.

In addition, findings presented here constitute one empirical example of the primacy of gender in the complex system of factors affecting health. In direct contrast to the biological determinism confronted by second-wave feminists, I demonstrate that the outcomes of genetic “hardwiring” are at least partially contingent on a system of social relations that differentially affects men and women. Gender is a primary frame for social interactions, real and imagined (Ridgeway 2011). Gendered social relations shape behaviors, values, and identities to the point of moderating and even creating biological differences (Fausto-Sterling 2005). Hence, moderation of biological realities by social environments is gender specific, not because of sexual dimorphism but because of the omnipresence of gender in our every interaction. In other words, the biological risk and protective factors are in part determined by gendered culture, reproducing differences in family and community obligations and experiences that make similar social environments more or less favorable for men and women.

Likewise, these results also provide insight into potential mechanisms underlying the gendered health benefits of marriage. These complex patterns may relate to the way in which men and women with particular traits experience the environment, such that biology not only is layered with our gendered culture but also interacts with it. In other words, understanding which social conditions are beneficial and which are not likely to be highly contingent both on genetic endowments and on subjective social psychological responses to those environments (e.g., salience and meaning). Existing research indicates that the latter is likely to vary systematically by gender, but genetic risk—a black box that is often conceptualized as (unexplained) resiliency or susceptibility in sociology—may vary by gender (or sex) as well. In sum, the relationship between gender and genetics is highly complex, dynamic, and likely dependent on the particular social locations of the individuals being studied.

This complexity poses challenges for intervention. For decades, we have known that policies or clinical practices that are effective for one gender may not be effective for the other (Bird and Rieker 2008). However, G × E × gender adds another dimension to an already complex landscape because responses to policies or treatments may vary as a function of both gender and genotype in a nonadditive fashion. To date, relatively little is known about gender-moderated gene-environment interaction. Thus, it is extremely important to identify in future research how resources or conditions that are protective for one gender or genotype group can be leveraged to the benefit of other groups. An alternative is to pursue interventions that target conditions that are likely to be universally harmful (e.g., child abuse) or beneficial (e.g., food security) with redistributive social policies aimed at fundamental social causes of disease (Phelan, Link, and Tehranifar 2011). However, it is critical to keep in mind that the value of any such policies will be underestimated since their efficacy is likely to vary as a function of genetic risk and gender, in addition to other social categories.

For their part, geneticists are increasingly making room for the social environment in their etiological models. However, the continuing emphasis on mechanisms inside the body is reflected in methodological decisions and disregard for relevant social science literatures. This perpetuates an inappropriate prioritization by funding agencies of genetic and biomedical pathways that confer a small degree of risk and are often contingent on social experiences and environments (Duster 2006; Pescosolido et al. 2008). While gender is the focus here, implications extend to race, socioeconomic status, and other fundamental social causes of disease. In response, sociologists have argued that we are uniquely positioned to develop integrative theories (e.g., Shanahan and Hofer 2005; Freese 2008; Seabrook and Avison 2010), yet biosociology remains a very small subfield, despite the growing availability of genotype data. This has created a striking absence of social theory in the genetics literature, where G × E effects are often reported with little speculation about social mechanisms underlying them (Moffitt, Caspi, and Rutter 2005; Rutter, Moffitt, and Caspi 2006; Freese and Shostak 2009). These findings on gender and GABAergic genetic risk reaffirm these arguments and constitute a cautionary tale, providing a clear empirical example of why genetic research informed by gender theory is of paramount importance to our understanding of health and well-being.

Methods

Selecting genetic variants for the risk score proceeded in three steps, following Belsky and Israel (2014). First, consistent with a hypothesis-driven approach, the risk score is constructed to maximize biological coherence of the genetic variants (e.g., Nikolova et al. 2011; Stice et al. 2012) and is limited to SNPs in GABA receptor genes. A total of 21 GABAergic SNPs are available in the COGA genotype data. Second, a conservative “top-hits” strategy is used to further cull genetic candidates, wherein SNPs are included only if there is empirical evidence in the published literature of an association with substance use disorders or behaviors (Belsky and Israel 2014). This step reduces the number of available SNPs to 12. Third, SNPs are eliminated if they provide redundant information about genetic risk (i.e., they are in linkage disequilibrium [LD]), often because they are located near one another on the genome. High LD is indicated by an R2R^{2} (squared correlation of genotypes) of >.50. In cases of high LD, the SNP with the larger published effect is retained. This third step eliminated four additional candidate SNPs, leaving eight SNPs for inclusion in the genetic risk score.

Genetic risk scores are less vulnerable to false positive results than the candidate gene approach, making them particularly suitable for research on gene-environment interactions (Belsky and Israel 2014).

Other potential methods of constructing genetic risk scores were used, including an additive model (i.e., a count of the number of risk alleles) and a weighted polygenic score, wherein the contribution of each SNP to the scale is weighted by the size of its published effect on nicotine or alcohol dependence. Both the additive and weighted scores performed more poorly than the dichotomized, unweighted genetic risk score, as determined by comparing Bayesian information criteria across models predicting nicotine or alcohol dependence. Consequently, the dichotomized, unweighted scale was retained in all subsequent analyses.
