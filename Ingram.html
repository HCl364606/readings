The Cultural Contingency of Structure: Evidence from Entry to the Slave Trade In and Around the Abolition Movement 1^{1}

Paul Ingram

Columbia University

Brian S. Silverman

University of Toronto

The economic effects of social structure are dependent on culture and must be understood in their cultural context. The authors demonstrate this with an analysis of the Liverpool slave trade. They show that as abolitionism became more salient in British culture, connections in a coinvestment network to both slavers and nonslavers mattered much more for predicting entry into the slave trade. As abolitionism rose, nonslavers in that public network gained relatively more influence than slavers, but the reverse was true in the private network of an elite social club. Furthermore, the status of a potential slaver mattered much more during the abolition era, as the emergent clarity of norms against slaving constrained high-status traders less than middle-status traders. The authors present advice to network and status theorists as to how they can reflect cultural contingency even in studies conducted in stable cultural contexts, thereby creating a more integrated and robust economic sociology.

To the extent that sociologists understand economic behavior differently than others, they do so because they focus particularly on culture or structural positions most often represented by networks and status hierarchies. In this article, we consider the interdependence between culture and structure. At a definitional level, every sociologist would agree that these forces are interdependent in that, for example, culture enables the interactions that become network relations or defines the values that determine who has status. We aim here to make a more urgent argument, that the network and status effects that are the everyday tools of explanation for economic sociologists are culturally contingent. Whether or not they work as expected depends on elements of culture—particularly what is most valued—that can vary and do sometimes change. We will show that understanding the cultural contingency of structure is imperative for making sense of economic activity in times of change. We think that even when studying stable cultural contexts, economic sociologists need awareness of cultural contingency to make optimal use of their structural tools.

Of course, there has been prior attention to the interdependence of culture and structure, most prominently with regard to networks. However, the energy has mostly gone into examining the influences of networks on culture, for example, using network methods to analyze the structure of narratives and symbols, identifying the structural determinants of fields and publics, and specifying social influences on tastes (Pachuki and Breiger 2010). Far less research has employed Emirbayer and Goodwin’s (1994, p. 1414) idea that culture has an “analytically autonomous” influence on structure, or their resultant advice that “an adequate approach to historical explanation must encompass both social structural and cultural perspectives on social action” (emphasis ours). It is this argument of the necessity of considering culture to understand network effects that we apply here. This position is also encouraged by Thornton (2004), who contended that networks are the influence on economic behavior that depends most on cultural context.

Why, given these arguments, doesn’t culture already occupy a more prominent role in network theory? Swidler (1986, p. 281) highlights the implications of cultural inertia: “In settled cultural periods, then, culture and social structure are simultaneously too fused and too disconnected for easy analysis.” She encourages “focus on those historical junctures where new cultural complexes make possible new or reorganized strategies of action” (p. 282). That is the strategy we follow here, focusing on a moment that has been called a “cultural revolution” to show that the very same network ties function differently depending on the cultural context. While the unsettled cultural context is key for our empirical analysis, our theoretical argument applies in more stable contexts. Awareness of the cultural contingency of networks can help analysts reconcile superficially incompatible empirical results, for example, that social ties between competing shipbuilders reduce their failure risk in the late 19th century but not in the early 20th; or that networkers who span structural holes get ahead in American companies but not Chinese ones; or that one economic practice diffuses through a network of board interlocks while another does not (Davis and Greve 1997; Ingram and Lifschitz 2006; Xiao and Tsui 2007). Awareness of this contingency is also necessary to identify the root causes of structural effects and the role of agency and ultimately to produce useful guidance for actors (Emirbayer and Goodwin 1994).

Arguments relating positions in status hierarchies to economic behavior are similarly dependent on culture. This is implicitly recognized by Homans (1974) when he conditions predictions about the deviance of the English gentry on specific times and specific practices. It is equally apparent in the attention the current middle-status conformity literature gives to developing a typology of norms. Phillips, Turco, and Zuckerman (2013) introduce categories of ethical, betrayal, and membership norms as necessary to understanding just when we should expect high-status actors to engage in economic deviance. This is an alternative way of saying that there is a cultural mediator, in the form of interpreting and mapping onto value systems, between the structure of status and behavior. As with networks, we show that the same status position and economic behavior are related in markedly different ways depending on the cultural context.

That our theoretical focus on the cultural contingencies of network and status effects requires attention to a moment of cultural change presents us with a secondary theoretical opportunity. The engine of the cultural change we study is a social movement, so at the same time we document the cultural contingencies of structures we are also able to illuminate aspects of social movement impact. Specifically, by examining changes in individual behavior, affected by a change in culture, sparked by a social movement, we can trace a micro path of social movement impact to the individual decision maker through cultural change. Our approach is in line with Polletta’s (2008, p. 93) recommendation that social movement theorists “focus more on people’s beliefs about appropriate means,” what she calls the institutional schema, or what others call an institutional logic (Thornton, Ocasio, and Lounsbury 2012). This dimension of our article is all the more useful because the effects of social movements on culture are understudied (Earl 2004; Polletta 2008).

Our empirical analysis examines the structural and cultural forces behind entry into the Liverpool slave trade, with particular attention to how those forces changed around the abolition movement. In other words, we ask, what makes a trader become a slave trader? Eighteenth-century Britons viewed “slaver” as a dichotomous category (e.g., Broadbent 1908, pp. 120–21). To move from nonslaver to slaver was a greater leap in cultural category than “minor slaver to major slaver” or “slaver to ex-slaver.” So, the focus on entry to the slave trade appeals to us because it represents such a dramatic normative transition and therefore a good opportunity to expose the cultural contingency of structure.

The empirical focus of this article presents the opportunity for a third contribution, one more phenomenological than the preceding two. The slave trade in Georgian Britain was an instance of “dirty business,” by which we mean economic activities that are not effectively prohibited by legal authority but are nevertheless counter to social values. We see dirty business as a phenomenon with substantial contemporary resonance. Relevant research includes analyses of sacred markets and stigmatized industries. Work on those topics has tended to examine how participants manage value challenges and mitigate their costs (e.g., Zelizer 1979; Carberry and King 2012). But before they engage in cultural and categorical entrepreneurship and strategizing, they must enter the contested industry. That foundational entry decision is nontrivial, perhaps even baffling, given the potential social costs. Cultures and economies may turn on whether capable and influential people take up activities such as slave trading, selling life insurance, performing abortions, or marketing complex financial products to people who may not understand them. By applying structural insights regarding which people, at what cultural moments, are more inclined to dirty business, and potentially shielded from the social penalties of engaging in it, we seek to explain the entry stage of this important economic phenomenon.

LIVERPOOL AND THE TRANSATLANTIC SLAVE TRADE

Overseas traders in Liverpool in Georgian Britain had a choice: they could invest in the slave (triangle) trade or the more common direct trade. The direct trade involved taking immigrants and manufactured goods to the Americas and returning with agricultural staples. The slave trade involved taking textiles, manufactured goods, bar iron, or salt to the West Coast of Africa, trading them with African traders or European factors for slaves, sailing to the Americas, selling the slaves, and, if time permitted, acquiring a cargo of agricultural staples for the return trip. Precise estimates of the returns of Liverpool overseas trade in the 18th century do not exist, but there is a consensus that the slave trade represented more risk and more reward than the direct trade.

Of all of the transatlantic slaving voyages with known ports of departure, more originated in Liverpool than in any other place (17.5% of the global total). In the years when Liverpool was most active in the slave trade, from the mid-18th century to abolition in 1807, it dominated all other ports in the world. Between 1791 and 1806, its market share, measured in terms of percentage of the world’s slaving voyages, never fell below 30% and was as high as 63% in 1798 (Trans-Atlantic Slave Trade Database; described in the appendix). Liverpool was well deserving of its sobriquets “slave trading capital of the world” and “the metropolis of slavery.”

London was initially the leading British slaving port owing to a monopoly granted to the Royal Africa Company that lasted in some form until 1712. For a number of decades after that, Bristol and London vied for leadership in the global industry until Liverpool took over first place in 1744 and held that position until abolition. Liverpool’s dominance of the trade in this period is attributed by historians to three factors. The first was that it was a first mover in building a number of wet docks, which facilitated overseas trade and shipbuilding. 2^{2} Power (1997, p. 311) attributes this initiative to a town council dominated by the interests of overseas traders, noting that Liverpool experienced a “commercial coup d’etat” around the turn of the 18th century.

The second Liverpool advantage was its location. Liverpool ships could sail north around Ireland, taking them away from privateers during the frequent wars of the 18th century, while ships from the southern ports of Bristol and particularly London were more vulnerable. Liverpool slave traders also benefited from proximity to the Isle of Man, which was a tax haven until 1765, allowing Liverpool traders to avoid duties on foreign goods used to barter for slaves in West Africa (Morgan 2007). Further, Liverpool benefited from the burgeoning textile and manufacturing industries in nearby Lancashire and Manchester and from improvements in the transportation linkages between those places and the port. Textiles were the most common commodity in the barter for slaves, typically making up about half of traded value (Morgan 2007).

The third Liverpool advantage was human capital. Commerce had a large influence over the local culture, which was particularly materialist and mercantilist and often openly hostile to the aesthetic and humanitarian aspirations of the enlightenment (Checkland 1952). This commercial focus, combined with the opportunities and risks of overseas trade, produced improvisers and innovators, and in many ways, Liverpool simply did the slave trade better. Liverpool slavers implemented effective organizational governance mechanisms and trade remittance mechanisms for their far-flung enterprise before counterparts in Bristol and London (Morgan 2007; Silverman and Ingram 2016). They developed close, even social, relationships with the African traders who supplied them with slaves, allowing them to rely on trust to smooth exchange (Behrendt, Latham, and Northrup 2010). They exploited market power in both acquiring and selling slaves, and they were first movers into new slave markets (Morgan 2007). Liverpool was also ahead of Bristol and London in the supply of capable ships’ officers who were able to operate in places such as the bight of Biafra that did not have European resident agents and therefore required trading directly with African merchants (Behrendt 2007). McDade (2011) attributes many of these advantages to the larger, more connected network of traders in Liverpool (compared to Bristol), which she sees as an advantage for knowledge transfer and access to resources of all types.

THE CULTURAL CONTEXT OF THE BRITISH SLAVE TRADE

One might reasonably ask whether slavery was in fact “dirty business” in Georgian Britain given the economic importance of the slave trade and slavery to the metropole and her colonies, the fact that the trade was carried out legally by British traders for more than two centuries, and that there was no active abolition movement in Britain until late in the 18th century. The answer is yes. In Brown (2006, p. 37), it is argued (in a chapter titled “Antislavery without Abolitionism” no less) that the slave trade represented a divergence between economic practice and normative values, and although it was seldom applied before the abolition movement, there was a potential social cost: “Slave traders in Britain encountered public disapproval early in the eighteenth century, decades before the emergence of those cultural movements [the abolition movement] often credited for engendering antislavery sentiment.” He documents antislavery arguments in print from the 1730s and 1740s. Although these arguments were not frequent in the periodicals of that time, they were presented as being uncontroversial and did not draw rebuttals (p. 38). Antislavery sentiment did not emerge simultaneously with the abolition movement but was instead widespread throughout the period we study (Davis 1999).

Drescher (1986, p. 18) further documents the low normative standing of the slave trade before the abolition movement: “Tension between economic utility and social distaste was present early in the development of the British slave colonies. Such attitudes appeared far too soon to attribute the phenomenon to the enlightenment. The condemnation of the slave trade as un-Christian and inhuman was distilled even into mid-eighteenth century children’s literature.” Yet, he describes that, before the abolition movement, explicit complaint against the trade and traders was rare. The view is of a fragmented cultural system (DiMaggio 1997)—that is, of a muffled tension between a British culture that celebrated liberty operating adjacent to a colonial economic system that relied on slavery. These separate moral worlds could coexist because slavery occurred far away from Britain. While its perpetrators were not lauded at home, neither was it necessary to constantly confront their offense to British values. Slavery violated those values, but distance from the slave colonies made it possible to avoid the conversation, and that was mostly what happened until the late 18th century.

This “fragmented era” of seldom spoken antislavery sentiment was brought to a noisy end by the abolition movement, which began in earnest in 1787, and after two waves of intense activity, succeeded in legislation 20 years later. The abolition movement was from the beginning a cultural project, setting out to affect, according to Wilberforce and Wilberforce (1838, p. 153), “the general moral feeling of the nation.” Consistent with the preceding account, d’Anjou and Van Male (1998, p. 222) claim that “every argument in favor of abolition and emancipation had been present for a long time.” Antislavery action was organized in a hub-and-spoke fashion, with a dominant London Committee of the Society for the Abolition of the Slave Trade providing information, encouragement, and organizing resources for local organizations throughout the country (Oldfield 1998). An indicator that the movement occurred in a fertile cultural context was its extremely rapid embrace throughout the country. Within a couple of years of the onset of the movement, hundreds of petitions in its favor had been sent to Parliament. These came from every place of significance except Liverpool. They notably included a petition bearing 10,000 signatures from Manchester, a place with close economic ties to Liverpool’s slave trade.

Much of the rhetoric during the abolition movement focused on whether slaving was a way to further British interests or a serious moral transgression, in other words, whether the relevant norms were of pragmatism or morality. 3^{3} Drescher (1990, p. 575) documents abolitionists’ emphasis on moral norms, for example, that their most characteristic arguments in parliamentary debates were policy irrelevance (the idea that economic interests are irrelevant to the fundamentally moral issue), British guilt, British pride, religion, and justice. For antiabolitionists, the top arguments in Parliament were pragmatic: British trade, international demand, British capital, practicability, and international practice. Proslaving advocates characterized the trade as a “necessary evil” (Swaminathan 2009, p. 166), conceding its immorality but weighing that as secondary to interests such as trade and global competition. Sometimes, the sides tried to argue from the opponents’ normative standards, for example, antiabolitionists defending the trade on religious grounds by citing many noncondemning references to slavery from the Old Testament (Harris 1788). But the consistent rhetorical patterns indicate that these forays into enemy terrain never gained traction and that the sides returned again and again to their arguments about what normative standard should be applied to the trade.

As indicated by the widespread petitions, the abolitionists’ moral arguments succeeded with the balance of the British population. The movement effected what Drescher (1986, p. 86) called a “cultural revolution,” succeeding “in fundamentally changing the way people thought about trade in slaves and about slavery itself” (d’Anjou and Van Male 1998, p. 221). While the public was largely swayed, antiabolitionists did keep pragmatic concerns alive, particularly in the social and political upper echelons, and were at one moment able to dampen the moral outrage. The heat of the first wave of the abolition movement receded in 1792 when geopolitical events allowed opponents to associate abolitionists with radicalism and threats to the state and society (Drescher 1986). Its reemergence around 1804 depended on political opportunities that supported the frame that abolition contributed to pragmatic goals of trade, order, and geopolitical power (Anstey 1975a), but as we will show, was also accompanied by widespread public condemnation on moral grounds.

We turn now to arguments as to how these cultural shifts affected economic behavior even before the trade was abolished on British ships in 1807. Slave traders are a particularly likely group to reveal these effects, as they were literally the brokers between the metropole and the slave economy, that is, the only residents of Britain actively involved in slavery. Slave traders were the bridge between those previously separate worlds and therefore the focus of the energy produced when the abolition movement forced them together (Brown 2006).

Social Influence and Entry to the Slave Trade

A large and robust literature on social influence shows that attitudes and behaviors are more likely to be shared among connected actors. Some studies have identified social influence in the context of behaviors that could be argued to be antinormative (e.g., Bearman and Stovel [2000] on becoming a Nazi; Christakis and Fowler’s [2007] analysis of the diffusion of obesity; Younts [2008] on cheating). Norms rely on social sanctions, so if one’s social group adopts antinormative behavior, one could find local social support for engaging in the same behavior. In Liverpool, a trader might feel emboldened (or even pressured) to enter slaving if his network contacts were slavers. This suggests a default expectation that social influence could act with regard to entry to the slave trade as it does in so many other contexts, such that individuals are more likely to adopt practices if their network partners engage in them and less likely if their partners do not.

We go beyond this default expectation to consider changes in network effects as a function of the changing cultural context. Specifically, we ask how social influence concerning the slave trade differs during the abolition movement, when the trade was at the top of the cultural agenda, compared to the fragmented era when the trade was in tacit tension with cultural norms and values but was seldom discussed. 4^{4} Our core theoretical argument is that the implications of a network tie change as a function of the cultural context in which the tie is embedded. As Skocpol (1985, p. 91) observes, “[The] very definitions of groups, their interests, and their relations to each other will be determined by cultural idioms.”

As a starting point, we posit that networks will matter much more for social influence on matters of cultural salience. The slave trade was seldom discussed before the abolition movement, but it was a point of constant contention during the movement. As Howman (2007, p. 282) puts it, “It seems that the matter was being widely discussed [in Liverpool] and strong positions on both sides of the argument were being adopted.” Network ties either to nonslavers who are likely to argue against it or to slavers who are likely to argue for it should matter more during the abolition movement. The idea of contention playing out through interpersonal conversations appears elsewhere, for example, in Gould (1993, p. 748). We add cultural context to that structural effect by arguing that the conversations, and the influence they produce, happen more often and with more intensity at particular cultural moments.

Mische and White (1998, p. 695) argue that “discourse is the stuff of social networks.” They conceive of “netdoms” as networks embedded within cultural domains, and they describe the processes behind switching of domains. A switch between netdoms changes the cultural content (e.g., the stories that can be told, the symbols that can be used) and therefore the implications of the network structure. They are explicit that the context influences the domain, focusing mostly on social spaces that they call “publics.” However, they recognize temporal contexts as affecting domain shift, specifically when unsettled, high-stakes “situations” arise. In their language, we consider the implications of a situation produced by cultural dynamism.

We see two specific mechanisms that will cause a network tie to be weightier for social influence regarding the entry to slave trade during the abolition movement. First, the cultural salience of the abolitionist cause and of the slave trade raises the stakes for the actors we study and therefore their propensity to engage the issue with their networks. Gamson (1995) describes experimental research that shows that media coverage of an issue raises the perceived importance of that issue for actors who are directly touched by it. Liverpool traders were all touched by the slave trade, and the operationalization we will use of cultural change depends on media content. Of course, media attention is not the whole of what we mean by culture. The simple fact that the standing of the trade was actively contended during the abolition movement—with the threats that contention represented for the trade’s legitimacy, legality, and morality—raised the stakes for the traders we study.

The second mechanism we see is that the capacity for discourse around the slave trade increases during the abolition movement. Both the pro- and anti-slave trade camps invested in developing and disseminating arguments for their positions, and these would have been useful to Liverpool traders in a way they would not be when the issue was beneath the cultural surface. If culture is a tool kit (Swidler 1986), it was expanded, both to support the slave trade and to attack it, during the abolition movement. The contentious nature of the moment could also affect actors’ capacities to influence by causing them to feel their own positions more strongly (Swidler 1995).

The argument we are making requires at least one scope condition. We can imagine that an issue may be sufficiently contentious as to cause the destruction of some types of relationships when it emerges and network partners find themselves on opposing sides. In that case, networks that predated issue activation would lose, not gain, weight in social influence after the issue is activated. For example, the issue of slavery became a sufficiently controversial one that we could certainly imagine some relationships failing over it. The network ties we will use to test our hypotheses are coinvestment ties in small groups that represented a substantial amount of contact and trust. Those ties seem strong enough to endure (at least sometimes) disagreement over the slave trade, although we do expect they were strained by the abolition movement, a point we take up in the next section. More generally, the key condition is that the ties be costly to sever, either because they are strong or for some other reason. 5^{5}

HYPOTHESIS 1.—Social influence through network ties to enter, or forgo entering, the slave trade will be stronger during the abolition movement.

Since we are examining entry to the slave trade, all of the “targets of influence” for hypothesis 1 are nonslavers at the moment of influence. So there are two types of influence ties at work: (1) nonslaver influencing nonslaver and (2) slaver influencing nonslaver. While hypothesis 1 argues that nonslavers will be more influenced by both their slaving network ties and nonslaving network ties when abolition and the slave trade are hot topics, we expect that nonslavers grew more in influence than did slavers in this cultural context. Following Emirbayer and Goodwin (1994, p. 1441), who explained that “symbolic polarities crystallize within cultural structures, dividing social and metaphysical reality into such antithetical categories as ‘pure’ and ‘polluted,’ ‘just’ and ‘unjust,’ and ‘sacred’ and ‘profane,’” we argue that the abolition movement strained the relationships between nonslavers and slavers by undermining the moral equivalency of the actors on each side of the relationship. The abolition movement transformed relationships between slavers and nonslavers from structural holes, from which the parties have access to different but potentially reconcilable bases of knowledge, to a form of “cultural holes” (Pachuki and Breiger 2010), which span differences of values. Cultural holes are harder to traverse, suggesting that slavers will lose influence relative to nonslavers, given that the latter group’s ties to the nonslaving targets of influence are not compromised by a cultural hole.

We see processes of stigmatization and social distancing behind the emergence of a cultural hole between slavers and nonslavers. Abolitionists explicitly evoked the concept of stigma and aimed to attach it to the trade: the word appears nine times in the account of the movement by its foremost leader, Thomas Clarkson (1839). The abolitionist’s strategy for producing this stigma was to make salient the abject elements of the trade. As Anstey (1975a) explains, it was not enough to document mere “brutality” in the trade—for that condition of life would be familiar and perhaps deemed necessary to late 18th-century Britons. Instead abolitionists had to show that that trade regularly created “atrocities,” violations of cultural values so acute as to confront the legitimacy of the trade. Accounts promoted by abolitionists therefore highlighted deviant forms of cruelty such as the torture on ship of a nine-month-old child (Anstey 1975a, pp. 32–33). It was further argued that the slave trade brought practitioners into proximity with sexual deviance practiced on the ships and plantations (Drescher 1986) and with the stigmatized Britons (“the very dregs of the community . . . escaped from jails . . . undiscovered offenders” [Williams 1897, p. 688]) who composed the crews.

The stigmatizing strategy of the abolition movement made the trade “collectively defined as abject and immoral” (d’Anjou and Van Male 1998, p. 221). We see this recategorizing as having an effect on the interactions within networks that are part of social influence. How does a slaver begin a conversation with a nonslaver network partner about the upside of a practice that has become explicitly associated with atrocious cruelty, sexual deviance, and the dregs of British society? Although—as we explain in the lead-in to hypothesis 1—we expect slavers to be motivated to make their case, and armed with arguments of their own, we think they will be disadvantaged in discourse relative to nonslavers. The cultural playing field tilted against the defenders of the slave trade. This argument is braced by previous evidence that less legitimate business practices are less likely to diffuse through networks (Davis and Greve 1997).

The abolition movement affected not only the perception of slaving but also that of the slavers themselves. Stigma is contagious, and people seek distance from stigmatized individuals, even more so if the source of the stigma is social rather than physical (Albrecht, Walker, and Levy 1982; Pontikes, Negro, and Rao 2010). This suggests another mechanism by which influence through a slaver-to-nonslaver tie changes during the abolition movement: that the stigma attached to the slaver causes the nonslaver to seek social distance and therefore undermines influence through the tie. Interestingly, this mechanism should operate for social influence of all types, not only with regard to slavery. For example, slavers’ influence on nonslaving network partners as to whom to vote for or what to consume should decrease with the abolition movement. But for our purposes, the stigma of the slaver operates in the same way as the increasing illegitimacy of the practice of slaving, supporting the following hypothesis:

HYPOTHESIS 2.—The influence of slavers on their nonslaving network partners will decrease relative to the influence of nonslavers on those same nonslaving network partners during the abolition movement.

The mechanisms behind hypothesis 2 are more likely to occur in particular types of networks, those that are more public, and therefore ones that are more exposed to the broad culture that makes a particular practice legitimate or illegitimate, in that interactions are more observable and therefore more likely to be affected by concerns over the contagion of stigma. More private networks should provide more latitude for influence regarding antinormative practices. Yue, Luo, and Ingram (2013) found that collusion was more likely to emerge among New York bankers connected through elite private clubs than through public directorships. We consider here that networks embedded within a private business club in Liverpool could serve as the basis of social influence to enter the slave trade even when that effect was dampened in more public networks. The context of the private club, which not only is private but is composed of elites, is particularly relevant to dirty business given that elites are more and more likely to be composed of business leaders (Khan 2012).

Both the private and elite characters of an exclusive club indicate that its culture will be distinct in some ways from public culture. If the values, beliefs, and discourse in a private club were just like those in the public sphere, why meet in private at all? Moreover, the elite actively cultivate cultural distinctions from the broader public (Lamont 1994). Some forms of deviance would also be expected in a club because elite members are generally less subject to social sanctions (Homans 1974), a phenomenon we consider at length below. Liverpool clubs of the 18th century certainly endorsed eccentricity. The short-lived Ugly Face Club celebrated and exaggerated its members’ ugliness. 6^{6} The longer-lived Sephton Club, which is the source of our club membership data, saw the business and political elite of Liverpool meet to role-play the governance of the small neighboring town of Sephton. The contemporary analogue might be a model United Nations, staffed by leaders of the real U.N., with drinking. Doran (1881, p. 129) describes London clubs of this time as home to “fashionable eccentricity.” There was even an Eccentric’s Club, founded in 1781.

Of course, we would not expect the culture of an elite private club to oppose that of the broader society in every way, so we must consider just what values and practices would be proscribed or endorsed in an 18th-century Liverpool club. Clubs are locations where the elite are socialized, specifically toward values that sustain their status (Domhoff 1974). Although there were plenty of elite-status abolitionists, generally “the British elite’s reaction to abolitionism . . . [was] that the higher the social level the deeper was the opposition” (Drescher 1986, p. 140). Abolitionism was broadly associated with other antihierarchical impulses of the late 18th century and therefore likely to be opposed in an elite private club, more so in Liverpool given the deep and long association of its elite with the slave trade.

Clubs also serve the purpose of building and maintaining cohesion among elites (Domhoff 1974). In this sense, it is relevant that the club we analyze was founded 35 years before the abolition movement. At the onset of the abolition movement in 1787, slavers outnumbered nonslavers by more than three to one among overseas traders in the Sephton Club. At the same time, nonslavers outnumbered slavers by about two to one in Liverpool’s trading community as a whole. It may be hard to stigmatize a large minority of a community, but history shows that it was possible given the social pressure against slaving from the rest of British society. On the other hand, within the Sephton Club, it is almost inconceivable to imagine a minority of nonslavers stigmatizing the large majority of slavers. This seems especially true given that their relationships were embedded in an elite club where differentiation from the public culture was valued and where sympathy to abolition was likely lower. For a member to bring abolitionist arguments into a private club in Liverpool would be to threaten the cohesion of the elite. All of these arguments suggest the following hypothesis:

HYPOTHESIS 3.—The differential effect described in hypothesis 2, that nonslaving ties will be more influential relative to slaving ties in the abolition movement, will be attenuated for private club ties.

Status and Entry to the Slave Trade

Various arguments in social psychology and sociology point to status as a moderator of social control. Phillips and Zuckerman (2001) and Phillips et al. (2013) integrate those arguments and evidence to offer a theory of middle-status conformity. The idea is that norms grip most tightly for those in the middle of a status hierarchy. Those at the top and bottom of the status hierarchy have more freedom from the norm and are more likely to engage in antinormative behavior. The key mechanism is security of position in a status system that is quite sticky for those who are clearly insiders, or outsiders. Since those at the top are safe in their status, they feel autonomy from norms. Since those at the bottom do not perceive much chance of climbing in the status hierarchy, they too feel autonomy. Those in the middle, however, feel the potential to climb and the risk of falling, and they therefore attend most carefully to normative expectations. Status effects on normative compliance are culturally contingent because the norms and their salience derive from cultural forces. Whereas above we considered how network determinants of entry to the slave trade changed with the abolition movement and the cultural shift it introduced, we now ask the same question for the effect of status.

In one important sense, the literature on middle-status conformity fits the phenomenon of dirty business well, in that the sanctions for norm violation in both cases are social. There are, however, other ways in which typical middle-status conformity settings seem different from the question of how traders were influenced by antislaving culture and from dirty business more generally. One is that applications of middle-status conformity arguments tend to examine social units such as groups, organizations, or transaction partnerships, whereas dirty business deals with the violation of cultural norms embedded in a society. Homans (1974, pp. 337–38) cautions against assuming that societies work like small groups but goes on to suggest that “[in] England in the sixteenth century, there are hints that both upper and lower classes were less restrained in fields of gambling, drink, and fornication.” Of course, societies are less cohesive than the organizations embedded within them and contain less general consensus over norms (Simmel

1950). This suggests the possibility that members of a society may find subcultures of individuals who support a norm that is unusual, or even offensive, to others within society. If, for example, (a) antislaving norms did not take hold in Liverpool and (b) Liverpool merchants could carry out their social and economic lives within that community or others like it, then the influence of status on norm conformity would not be relevant for predicting entry to the slave trade there.

We see a key role of successful social movements with regard to dirty business as reducing the sustainability of deviant subcultures. The idea that Liverpool slavers could have found local normative succor while the abolition movement was active simply cannot be credited. Even if they had no network ties to nonslavers, they would have encountered abolitionist sentiment in everyday civic and business life. Although organized resistance to the abolition movement did emanate from Liverpool, antislaving sentiment reached the town through the arts and literature and through interactions with people from outside Liverpool. Abolitionist campaigners such as Thomas Clarkson took the message directly to Liverpool, and the abolitionist cause was pushed there by local leaders, most notably William Roscoe, a zealous abolitionist who won election to Parliament from Liverpool in 1806. Perhaps the most direct evidence of the normative climate of the town was its presentation to outsiders in town guides, which before the abolition movement presented the slave trade as a nonissue by simply describing the volume of activity, and by the end was decisively anti-slave trade. The Picture of Liverpool published in 1805 begins its discussion of the trade by calling the label “metropolis of slavery” an unfounded and illiberal characterization of the town, and it asserts a false claim that the slave trade there was conducted mostly by nonresidents. 7^{7}

Further, whatever the normative status of the slave trade within Liverpool, investors from the town could not avoid exposure to external influence from elsewhere in Britain. First, Liverpool was growing rapidly by domestic migration in the relevant years (a 40% population increase between 1790 and 1801 alone). Second, approximately 40% of investments in overseas trade from Liverpool were attributable to investors from elsewhere. Third, while Liverpool had a strong local identity, it was nevertheless influenced by cultural trends from London, and the elite were the most likely to follow the metropolis (Longmore 2007). 8^{8} Most important, however, is that as a trading community, Liverpool could not sustain isolation from the rest of Britain. Liverpool merchants had to look to London for finance and as the largest market for most of their imports. Easily accessible by canal or road was Manchester, the place of origin of many of the manufactured goods that were part of overseas trade from Liverpool, and a place where as much as 50% of the adult male population signed an abolition petition in 1792. The abolition movement ensured that investors in overseas trade from Liverpool were exposed to antislaving norms.

There is no tension between the argument we are making here and the idea in support of hypothesis 3, that the Sephton Club provided a safe space for proslaving discourse. Even members of the Sephton Club would be exposed to antislaving sentiment through their other civic engagements. It is fully possible that the same person could be exposed simultaneously to pro-slaving influence through the Sephton Club and antislaving norms from the broader society. As the abolition movement effected an increase in normative pressure against slaving, those norms would be expected to bite hardest for those in the middle of the status hierarchy, leaving those at the top and bottom relatively more free to enter the trade. Our hypothesis focuses on the norm violation of high-status individuals, because low-status members of British society did not have the means to invest in the slave trade: 9^{9}

HYPOTHESIS 4.—Relative to middle-status individuals, higher-status individuals will be more likely to enter the slave trade during the abolition movement.

DATA, ANALYSIS, AND RESULTS

We relied principally on data from five sources: (1) the Trans-Atlantic Slave Trade Database (to identify slave voyages and their owners); (2) the Liverpool Plantation Register (to identify nonslave voyages and their owners); (3) Gore’s directories of Liverpool and Liverpool voting records (to identify the occupation and status of owners); (4) archival records of the Mock Corporation of Sephton (to identify members and their dates of joining); and (5) the Gale Collection of British Newspapers, 1600–1950 (to document antislaving rhetoric in public discourse). Details on these sources and our use of them appear in the appendix. Our data on investments in shipping begin in 1730. We start our analysis in 1740, so that our variables, some of which are based on a trader’s history of investments (e.g., network variables and direct trade experience), are not biased by left-censoring of the data. Our results are comparable when we begin our analysis in 1730. Our observation window closes in 1807, because that is the last year that the trade was legal in the United Kingdom. In all, we utilize data on 14,244 direct trade voyages and 3,012 slaving voyages between 1730 and 1807.

Measuring Cultural Pressure against the Slave Trade

To measure the cultural context of the slave trade as affected by the abolition movement, we relied on a content analysis of British newspapers from the period we study. We looked in particular for the incidence of various forms of antislaving rhetoric. The logic of this approach is that it allows us to get closest to the broad public sentiment that both best represents the general cultural attitude to the slave trade and drives the normative mechanisms we have described. Public communications such as newspaper articles represent more than simply traces of underlying cultural values. They are also part of the process of cultural change, contributing, for example, to the categorization of the slave trade as abject and illegitimate (Ocasio, Loewenstein, and Nigam 2015).

Using the electronically searchable Gale collection of newspapers, we searched in every year of our analysis for the coincidence in an article of “abolition” and “slave trade.” Because of the format of newspapers of the day, which often amounted to continuous text without headlines, our electronic searches sometimes yielded “hits” that did not represent anti-slave trade sentiment (e.g., “slave trade” being used in a list of returning slave ships, “abolition” used in application to a tax, or some other object unrelated to slaving). We therefore employed a research assistant to manually examine every hit in our search to confirm whether it actually represented an expression of abolitionist sentiment. The Gale collection has an irregular representation of newspapers, and some articles appear more than once. We counted the same article only once, no matter how many times it was represented in the database, and we standardized each year’s count of abolitionist articles by the number of articles that appeared in the whole database for that year.

The resulting measure of cultural hostility to the slave trade is presented in figure 1. The figure also highlights the years that historian Seymour Drescher (2010) identified as “hot” for the abolition movement using indicators of abolitionist efficacy such as petitions and parliamentary debates. As the figure indicates, there is a strong correspondence between Drescher’s periodization and our text-based measurement of the intensity of abolitionist sentiment. The pattern is of two intense waves of abolitionist sentiment, separated by about a decade during which antislaving sentiment was much less likely to be expressed. This pattern may seem at first surprising, as the inertia of culture seems to suggest that transitions will be slow and monotonic, like the turns of a supertanker. But it is consistent with historical descriptions of the abolition movement (Anstey 1975a; Drescher 1986; Brown 2006) and with the views that unsettled cultural periods can be affected by entrepreneurs, that cultural concepts exist in a fragmented system, and that the salience of any element can be affected by a range of contextual triggers (Swidler 1986; DiMaggio 1997).

Network Measures

We examine social influence to enter the slave trade through connections in a coinvestment network. Shipping voyages in the period we study were typically owned by a partnership of investors. These partnerships were small enough to credibly represent substantial levels of contact among their members. Coinvesting groups for nonslaving voyages in our data had on average 3.17 partners, the modal partnership had 2 members, and 99% had 10 or fewer partners. Coinvesting groups for slaving voyages were a little larger, probably because slaving voyages were more expensive to launch: 4.08 partners mean, 2 partners mode, and 98% consisting of 10 or fewer partners. The coinvestment network was calculated as

where XtX_{t} represents a time-varying matrix of shipping investors by voyages. Multiplying this matrix by its transpose produces TtT_{t} , an investor-by-investor matrix in which the cells represent the number of times two investors have coinvested in a voyage. The network variables in the article were calculated using voyages that had occurred in the previous five years. We dichotomized TtT_{t} for the network variables used in the analysis below. Two investors were therefore considered to be tied to each other if they had coinvested at least once in the past five years. 11^{11} For the variables that test hypotheses 1 and 2 regarding the social influence to enter the slave trade or forgo entry, we create two variables: the totals of the number of co-investment network ties a focal investor has to slavers and to nonslavers. We log these tie counts to reflect the reality that the first tie to a slaver or nonslaver represents more potential social influence than the fifth tie, which itself represents more social influence than the tenth tie.

To test hypothesis 3, regarding social influence toward the slave trade within a private club, we include, for members of the Sephton Club in each year, a count of the number of other members who were active slavers and a count of active members who were nonslavers. These variables were highly correlated with each other and with the indicator variable for Sephton member, so we orthogonalized them before entering them in our models.

Status Measures

As Phillips and Zuckerman (2001, p. 386) explain, status in theories of elite deviance is intended to represent “honor and esteem” (as opposed to, e.g., professional competence). To test hypothesis 4, we capture the honor or esteem accorded to overseas traders using legal status, identifying high-status traders as those who held the titles of worship esquires and gentlemen. An authoritative contemporaneous account of English law defines esquires to include the first sons of knights and their eldest sons in perpetual succession; the younger sons of peers and their eldest sons in perpetual succession; those who receive the designation from the king and their eldest sons; and those who bear any office of trust under the Crown (e.g., Liverpool mayors gained the designation of esquire). Gentlemen were those who “studieth the laws of the realm, who studieth in the universities, who professeth liberal sciences, and (to be short) who can live idly, and without manual labour, and will bear the port, charge, and countenance of a Gentleman” (Blackstone 1765, bk. 1, p. 394). Esquires and gentlemen were not the top social rank in Britain. The order of ranks was the nobility, titles of dignity (knights and baronets), titles of worship (esquires and gentlemen), yeomen or freemen (commoners with enough capital to qualify to sit on juries and to vote in elections for public office), and all others. However, nobles and titles of dignity did not appear among the investors in overseas trade in Liverpool. 12^{12}

By statute, individuals were required to identify by rank in legal proceedings and other actions, so these categories were not superfluous. Further, among the traders we study, only 3.5% were gentlemen or esquires. This paucity of high-status titles suggests that they were not claimed idly in the sources we rely on. Given their rarity, we collapse esquires and gentlemen into one category for analysis, which we will refer to as simply “gentlemen,” following Edward Coke, who confounded the two titles and observed “every Esquire is a Gentleman” (Blackstone 1765, p. 393).

Among the remaining 96.5% of traders, we differentiate between merchants (51.5%) and others (45%). Merchants and others were all freemen. Others performed some designated profession or occupation (e.g., brewers, glaziers, captains). Merchants lived off their capital through importing and exporting, and we therefore categorized them as closer to the so-called idle gentlemen. Haggerty (2012, p. 26) notes that the title “merchant” was prestigious, designating the “elite of the trading community,” giving them clear precedence over others, although nobody would argue that merchants were above gentlemen in status.

Control Variables

We controlled for two features of a trader’s investment history: the percentage of his ships that had been lost, which may indicate wealth and desperation, and the number of investments he had made in the direct trade. That variable, direct trade experience, is an indicator of wealth, as investors with a high number of past investments are expected to be richer. This variable may also represent a trader’s know-how in the direct trade, which was a competing investment path to the slave trade. We include an indicator variable for whether the trader was a resident of Liverpool. As controls for macro events we have an indicator for the U.S. war, which greatly reduced transatlantic shipping because of the risk of privateers, and a time trend.

Control variables for conditions in the slave trade include global competition, which is the number of slave voyages in a year that came from all ports (British and non-British) except Liverpool. We included estimates of slave and sugar constant-pound prices in the Caribbean from Eltis, Lewis, and Richardson (2005). We also included measures of temperature shocks at African slave trading ports, taken from Fenske and Kala (2012). Those authors show that the supply of slaves at a given port was lower in years when the temperature was higher, an effect they attribute to changes in disease patterns and agricultural productivity. Our own analysis of voyage outcomes supports Fenske and Kala’s in that voyages experienced lower yields of slaves when they traded with African ports experiencing shocks of warmer temperature. The variable we call negative supply shock is an average of the deviation from annual mean temperature at African slaving ports, weighted by the number of Liverpool voyages to the port in the year. We lagged all the variables on industry conditions by two years, reflecting the fact that slave voyages would have to be planned before news

Analysis

We seek to determine if and when a trader enters the slave trade. We include in the analysis only traders who have never before invested in a slave voyage. The phenomenon we study could be called the transition to slaving, and we model it with a piecewise exponential model, an event-history (hazard) model that allows the risk of becoming a slaver to vary over pre-selected ranges of the trader’s career history. The advantage of the piecewise exponential model over alternatives is the flexible parameterization of time dependence, appropriate in this case because we have no prediction as to how career tenure will affect the transition rate. The model estimates different constants for the hazard of becoming a slaver depending on whether a trader is in the first year of his career, in years 1–3, 3–5, 5–10, 10–20, or more than 20 years. We split the career history of each trader into spells that begin with each new voyage investment and update our covariates for each spell, allowing trader and industry variables to vary over time. Our analysis includes 5,918 traders, 1,760 of whom eventually become slavers. The regressions include 25,694 spells, corresponding to 25,694 separate voyage investments made by the traders we observe. An alternative analytic approach would be to use a hazard model with investing experience rather than career history as the time dimension. Yet another alternative would be to use a logit analysis, with random effects for traders to account for the hierarchical nature of the data. The online appendix presents reestimates of our main results using these alternatives. They yield results that are fully comparable to those we report here.

Our position in this article is that the influences on economic behavior depend fundamentally on culture. We therefore apply a structural change model, where we interact all of the independent variables with our measures of abolitionist culture. When calculating the interaction variables, we centered all of the noncultural variables. This transformation affects only the coefficient for culture in our model estimates, producing a coefficient that can be interpreted as the effect of culture when all other variables are at their mean level. To produce the coefficients that appear in table 2, we used Stata’s nonlinear combination function to combine for each independent variable two effects from the underlying model: the variable’s direct effect and its interaction with abolitionist culture. Results

Hypothesis 1, which argued that social influence through the trader network would be greater during the abolition movement, is strongly supported. Ties to slavers do more to pull a trader into the slave trade, and ties to nonslavers do more to keep a trader out of the slave trade, during the high abolition context. The tests of structural change are significant in both cases. Figure 2 illustrates the magnitude of the effects, showing the effect on the transition rate of one network tie to a slaver or nonslaver compared to none. The story of the figure is the contrast between the low and high contexts of abolition discourse. The effect sizes in the low context are substantial in terms of impact, with a tie to a slaver increasing the rate of transition to slaving by 17% and one to a nonslaver decreasing the rate by 15%. Yet these effects seem small in the figure because they are dwarfed by those from the high contexts, when a tie to a slaver increased the rate of transition by 132% and a tie to a nonslaver decreased the rate of transition by 78%.

Hypothesis 2, which predicted that social influence from slavers increased less than that of nonslavers with the rise of abolitionism, is tested by comparing the difference in the coefficients for social influence for network slaver ties and network nonslaver ties in the low and high moments of abolitionist culture. This test is the same as testing that the difference of the absolute value of the coefficient on the interaction between abolitionist culture and trade network nonslaving ties was greater than the coefficient on the interaction between abolitionist culture and trade network slaving ties (those coefficients do not appear directly in table 2, although they are used in the interpretations of the model that do appear). Consistent with hypothesis 2, the influence of nonslavers through the publicly observable network increases significantly more than that of slavers as abolitionist culture increases (z = 2.18, P < .05).

Hypothesis 3 predicted that in contrast to the publicly observable co-investment network, in the private network embedded within the Sephton Club, nonslavers would not gain relative influence to discourage entry to slaving with the rise of abolitionism. This is supported. Indeed, a trader’s nonslaving network ties through the Sephton Club decrease the likelihood of entering the slave trade in the fragmented era but do not in the abolitionist era, and this structural change is statistically significant. Furthermore, a trader’s slaving network ties through the Sephton Club do more to encourage entry to the slave trade in the abolitionist era, and this comparison is significant. During the abolitionist era this elite, private club becomes more hospitable to the slave trade.

Robustness of Results to Alternative Explanations

A multivariate analysis such as the one we present does not provide causal certainty, but there are a number of reasons to favor our interpretation of the results. First, because we hypothesize about changes in structural effects rather than main effects, the usual alternative explanations for network and status effects are less relevant in our study. The most direct challenge to our interpretation of the results would be that the structural changes we identify are not a function of culture, but of some other macro change. One defense against this in our models is the inclusion of the time trend. It also supports our identification strategy that the emergence of abolition culture occurred in two waves. We do not know just what alternative macro force might have been coincident with abolitionism, and if one exists, it has been missed by historians who see the abolition movement as the compelling issue of the era we study, at least for overseas traders in Liverpool. A more likely alternative would be that the abolition movement could have caused change in some variable(s) besides culture, and it could be these other changes that drive our results. Here again we are fortunate that our models have a number of relevant controls, for activity in the slave trade beyond Liverpool, for prices of sugar and slaves, and for the supply of slaves. If the abolition movement affected any of these variables, their effects would show up directly in our models. Additionally, we have access to aggregate cost estimates for launching slave voyages dating from 1760 from Anstey (1975b). Adding these to our models means that we lose the first 20 years of our observation window, but the resulting estimates do not differ in any substantive way from the ones we report here.

We can also defend the specific network and status arguments we have presented. One possible concern regarding the network effects is that traders might have formed network affiliations for different reasons during the abolition movement, so the differential effects we show may actually represent different types of network ties rather than the same ties operating in different ways. We examined this possibility by estimating our results using only network ties that were formed when abolitionist culture was low and excluding any that were forged in the heat of the abolition movement. 14^{14} The results are comparable to those in table 2, supporting our contention that the very same network ties operate differently depending on the cultural context and indicating against an argument that our results obtain because of differential selection into networks at different times.

Other alternatives could challenge our interpretation of the network effects as representing the mechanism of social influence. For example, the fact that traders tied to slavers are more likely to enter the slave trade while those tied to nonslavers are more likely to invest in the direct trade could be a function of access to information and investing opportunity rather than social influence. Whether the basic network effect is attributable to information or social influence does not really matter for our core contention that it is contingent on culture. Nevertheless, we think that the quantitative and qualitative evidence supports our interpretation of the effect as representing social influence.

Qualitatively, there is little reason to believe that it was difficult for traders to find investing opportunities. In 65% of traders’ initial slaving investments, the group they invested with included nobody who was previously part of their network. Clearly, it was possible to find investment opportunities without relying directly on one’s network. If we allow for indirect mechanisms to discover investments, myriad paths were available to the Liverpool traders. Quantitatively, we do not see how the information argument can explain the cultural contingency we identify, that network effects became stronger during the abolition movement for both ties to slavers and ties to nonslavers. It could be argued that in the stigmatizing abolitionist culture, slaving investments went “underground,” making network access to slavers more important at that time. But why, then, were the majority of initial slaving investments made with partners who were outside the focal trader’s prior network? And why was there a comparable effect for direct voyages and network ties to nonslavers, given that there was no stigma at all attached to the direct trade? And further, why, if networks represented information about investment opportunities, would ties to nonslavers in the context of the Sephton Club encourage entry to the slave trade? These effects, which are the focus of our hypotheses, are consistent with our idea that these networks serve as interpersonal paths for contention over the merits of the trade, and not with the idea that they represent only information about investment opportunities.

Consider also a common alternative for status effects on economic activity, that they represent capability or economic resources or propensity for risk rather than “honor and esteem.” Again, we have controls that are relevant to capability and economic resources, particularly percentage of ships lost and direct trade experience. Furthermore, given the definition of status we use, it is hard to credit the capability argument. The definition of gentlemen is in many ways the antithesis of business competence. More importantly, while capability, economic resources, and risk preferences might be part of the reason gentlemen (and merchants) are more likely than others to enter the slave trade when abolitionist culture was low, they cannot explain why gentlemen (and merchants) were even more likely to enter when abolitionist culture was high. Abolitionism did not increase the competence or economic resources of high-status traders, and it did not increase the cost or risk of investing in a slave voyage. The correlation between Anstey’s (1975b) cost estimates for launching a slave voyage and our operationalization of abolitionist culture is negative (-.14). Together, the various analyses of the performance of gentlemen slavers suggest that it would have been very hard for even a careful observer of the industry to develop a belief that gentlemen had a performance advantage in slaving based on anything except superstition. Our outcome measures for direct trade voyages are not as detailed as they are for the slave trade, but analysis indicates that gentlemen were not more or less likely than other traders to have their direct-trade voyages sink, be captured, or be condemned (analysis available from the authors). The bottom line is that the distinct pattern of gentlemen’s entry into slaving appears most consistent with a status-based explanation involving the honor and esteem of the gentleman position.

DISCUSSION

In this article, we look at familiar structural effects of networks and status in two different cultural moments. We find that the structural effects differ markedly. Network connections to both slavers and nonslavers mattered much more for predicting entry to the slave trade when abolitionist culture had taken hold compared to the fragmented cultural era, and nonslavers gained relatively more social influence than did slavers. Similarly, status had a small effect on entry in a fragmented cultural context but a large effect when antislaving norms were salient. We begin by discussing what economic sociologists might do if they wanted to make the cultural contingency of structure more central.

We believe that structure is always contingent on culture and that economic sociologists should always remember this. At the same time, we do not intend this article as an attack on middle-range theory of the type that has been so successful for the field. We admire the portfolio of insights that have come from examining the impact of networks and status within stable and consistent cultural contexts. However, as this portfolio grows, the challenge of integrating diverse results increases, as does the desire to apply structural insights in new contexts. We offer methodological and theoretical advice that all economic sociologists could apply in the interest of producing a more integrated corpus that is more robust to cultural contingencies.

The theoretical advice is to consider values explicitly and to consider how they operate to enable structural processes. Network theorists should ask the following questions: What do these actors value? Which values are salient and in tension? And how do those values affect their motivation and capacity to interact? We see this as a return to first principles. For example, early network theorists such as Lazarsfeld and Merton (1954) saw similar values as the source of homophily. The substitution of demographic categories for values was initially an empirical convenience but has been so successful that contemporary network theorists could be forgiven for forgetting just why it is that superficially similar nodes are more likely to connect. They should remember and (at the least) make explicit their assumptions as to what compels and enables the nodes they study to connect and sustain relationships.

Similarly for status research, values have been recognized at the microfoundations of structural effects. Of course, status emerges by interpreting individual behaviors over time through the lens of a group’s values (Homans 1950), so values are germane to understanding who has status at any moment, and how they might lose it. Closer to the core of recent developments in the literature, we see the initiative to categorize different types of norms and recognize their implications for deviance as being closely in line with our advice to take values seriously (Phillips et al. 2013). We find categories such as identity, ethical norms, and betrayal norms to be useful for understanding how behaviors map onto culture within a context. We would emphasize, however, that these categories do not represent universal connections between behavior and expectations and that they take their ultimate significance from the cultural values within which they are embedded. Analysts could usefully ask, What would be expected of this actor by others, and what would she expect of herself? The point is that what belongs in normative categories such as “ethical” is not universal, but depends on cultural context.

Our theoretical advice to consider the role of values is closely tied to our methodological advice, which is to continue to employ contextualist methods, with the obvious candidates being history and ethnography. Con-

American Journal of Sociology

textualism can be contrasted with reductionism, which is the tendency to reduce causes to independent variables (Gaddis 2002). We recognize that the multivariate analysis we present is not fully contextualist. Although we interact everything in our models with culture, a contextual purist might argue that it is necessary to interact everything with everything else. We do not go that far because models must represent a reasonable trade-off between parsimony and reality, and with both theory and history as guides, we felt capable to make some of those trade-offs in this context. Still, we do not feel we could have produced a reasonable multivariate model of the entry to slaving had we not spent some years absorbing the relevant history of Liverpool and the trade. Of course, we recognize that contextualism is relatively common in economic sociology, with classic works that are fully contextualist (e.g., Zelizer 1979; Vaughan 1996) and others that strike a balance between context and multivariate analysis as we try to do here (e.g., Padgett and Ansell 1993). Still, there are pressures against contextualism, not the least being the current fashion in economics to value the identification of unitary causes above all else. We think those pressures should be resisted.

We now turn to more focused implications of our analyses for various streams of literature, starting with network theory. The transformation, at least in its influence, of the Liverpool trading network wrought by the abolition movement reminds analysts that relationships depend on identities, interests, moral standing, and the quality of interaction, which in turn is affected by shared values and symbols. Take for example our finding that influence through the network structure, for and against entry to the slave trade, was so much greater in the abolitionist context. We have been struck by how little there is in the vast literature on network and social influence around what might be called issue salience and activation. Mostly, those literatures begin following ideas and practices after they have become socially significant; but what happens before that, to determine which of the thousands of possibilities gain attention? There is some treatment of this question in the diffusion literature with the recognition that diffusion may start when an innovation creates an opportunity, or when salience is created by reaching a threshold of adoption (Strang and Soule 1998). But those arguments are insufficient to explain the heightened social influence for and against slaving, a practice that was both common and frequently criticized for generations before the abolition movement. On the basis of our case, we suggest that culture presents a spotlight on ideas and practices that is a precursor to network influence. In culturally stable moments, the concept of cognitive schema as channels of attention could be applied to understand what flows through networks (DiMaggio 1997; Ocasio 2011). In unsettled cultural moments, analysts should look for values in a conflict and expect the social-structural action to be where they overlap (Swidler 1986).

When the two parties to a relationship represent incompatible value systems, the concept of a cultural hole (Pachuki and Breiger 2010) is a useful way to think of another effect of culture on a dyadic relationship. We have shown that influence through the relationship decreases in this situation. Future research should examine other implications of cultural holes. Staying in our empirical context, we wonder about the domain specificity of the effect we identified. After abolition, might slavers and nonslavers influence each other in ways besides entry into the slave trade: For example, might they influence each other’s votes for Parliament or taste in art? And what of the ongoing dynamics of the network relationship? Trapido (2013) showed that in the same period we study, Whigs and Tories partnered under certain conditions in overseas trading voyages in Bristol. It remains to be determined if the much wider cultural hole between slavers and nonslavers after the abolition movement could be traversed to allow the continuity of co-investment relationships.

Another theoretical opportunity our study presented was to examine the effect of social movements on culture, which has been characterized as understudied (Earl 2004; Polletta 2008). The abolition movement has previously been identified as an example of this effect (d’Anjou 1996). Our contribution comes from our analytic attention to individual choices made in cultural context. Polletta (2008, p. 93) identifies the “methodological precision of psychological treatments of cognitive schemas” as a model for analyzing changes in “people’s beliefs about appropriate means.” Attention to individual models of action not only provides a rare opportunity to document cultural change but also provides insight into change as it occurs in higher-order structures. Changes in organizations and states, the typical explanatory targets of social movement analysis, occur on the foundation of changes in the people who populate them. This suggests another path for social movement theorists seeking to understand the macro effects of movements, through changes they create at the microlevel. Social movement research has examined individuals, but typically those who are mobilized rather than those they mobilize against (Giugni 2004). The literature on “micromobilization” is a good place to start for insight into how movements change minds (e.g., Snow et al. 1986). Our approach suggests that it could be applied to understand the culturally informed models held by the individuals who populate the institutions that social movements target. 15^{15} Understanding those models could help predict which social movement tactics will be effective and when macro change will occur. As Drescher (1986, p. 94) said about the abolition movement, “The creation of a climate of opinion was therefore the most important residue of frequently renewed petitions.” That climate of opinion not only changed the behavior of Liverpool traders but also ultimately led to a change in British law to outlaw the slave trade.

Our analysis also suggests that the study of social movements might examine culture’s effects on individual choice as an end in itself, and not only as a trigger for more macro change. For example, we wonder if the abolition movement could have eventually strangled the Liverpool slave trade through normative pressure, in the absence of a change in law. Regardless, there were certainly other behavioral changes that resulted from the cultural shift affected by the abolition movement. Soon after British abolition, Liverpool became an enthusiastic center of support for subsequent abolition and emancipation campaigns, an effect that is hard to explain except as a redemptive response to earlier normative targeting of the city by abolitionists. Attention to the cultural impacts of social movements invites attention to the models of action of individuals. This opens up a range of potential outcomes of social movements, micro and macro, intended and unintended.

Of course, our case has limits as a basis for future theorizing. Obvious among them is that we have treated the abolition movement as a purely exogenous influence in our analysis. We see that as justified given our focus on the investing behavior of Liverpool traders, but we do not suggest that the abolition movement emerged independent of the economic interests that it affected. We encourage theories of the reciprocal relationships between social movements and their targets, even when those targets are individuals. Similarly, the abolition movement is probably an unusual case in terms of its impact on cultural change. The culture it affected was ripe for change. D’Anjou (1996) considers the implications of this for general theories of social movements and cultural change. Furthermore, the cultural hole we study, that between slavers and nonslavers in the heat of the abolition movement, may itself be unusual. It represents an extreme case of value incompatibility, and it may therefore be unrepresentative of the ways in which cultural differences affect relationships and networks. Additionally, we have had to make inferences about what happens within network relationships to connect our macro arguments about cultural change to the micro impacts on traders’ decisions. We have benefited from the wealth of evidence about the abolition movement and its impact on British culture, but there is little direct evidence of just how Liverpool traders influenced each other regarding the trade, probably because such influence must have taken place mostly in private conversations. Historical studies such as ours have limits for documenting discourse within relationships and are therefore complemented by other research methods and by theory.

Beyond theories of culture, networks, and social movements, we also see in our study an opportunity to examine an important phenomenon, which we called dirty business. When will economic actors do things that are legal but against cultural values? The answer includes the ingredients of individual status and social influence, but their quantities are contingent on the clarity of the cultural values (Davis and Greve 1997). When slaving was antinormative in an ambiguous sense (“a necessary evil,” distasteful but not confronted openly), network and status structures mattered somewhat. When the cultural ordering was settled decisively against slaving by the abolition movement, they both mattered much more. Increased attention to the dirty business dramatically increased the relevance of an individual’s location in the social structure to predict his participation in the business. One practical implication of this fact is that anyone who sets out to campaign against a dirty business (or, for that matter, to defend it) should understand the relevant social structures first and perhaps work to change them at the same time as the normative status of the business.

Our results also point to private clubs as relevant to the practice of dirty business. In this, our study joins that of Yue et al. (2013), who found that collusion was more likely to emerge among New York bankers connected through private clubs than through public directorships. In Liverpool, the Sephton Club offered a space where the elite could associate in private, and it proved to be a fertile incubation site for the slave trade. One strategy for reducing the incidence of dirty business would be to publicize the associations of the elite. The significance of the private club also relates back to our main thesis of cultural contingency, illustrating the important point that cultures vary not only across time but also across social space at any one time (Mische and White 1998).

Our final step is to interpret our analysis for what we know about the slave trade itself. We see our results as confirming what is perhaps the core tenet of the last half century of history on the trade: that it was counter to key elements of the British culture, but that these differences were mostly tacit until the abolition movement. We analyzed participation in the slave trade as an act of economic deviance and gained substantial purchase on it by applying theories as to who breaks social rules. On the other hand, there is no anticipation in the extant historical literature of our finding that gentlemen played a key role in the prosecution of the trade or that social-structural positions affected who took up the trade. In the infrequent instances when the question of who the slave traders were has been considered at all, the conclusion has been (upon little or no evidence) that they were no different from their contemporaries, and the implication that the decision to take up slave trading was comparable to the decision to take up any other business (High 1959). That idea we have shown to be false. Entering the slave trade was different even from participation in the direct trade to the slave colonies. It was nothing like becoming a grocer.

The particular role of gentlemen does suggest an interpretation for British history beyond the one for social theory. Gentlemen (and esquires) in the

late 18th century did not merely represent an institutionalized social status. They could also be seen as the representatives of a hierarchical system that would soon change (Clark 1985). Our analysis might not have been possible a generation later, as the titles, particularly esquire, proliferated to the point of losing their meaning (Leneman 2000). Given this, the question occurs as to whether gentlemen entered the slave trade not only because they were less subject to social penalties but because they were more attracted by its inherent hierarchy (Drescher 1986). However, although there was a negative correlation between status and abolitionist sentiment, there were high-status Britons who opposed the slave trade. Hudson (2001) shows the Tory and Church of England ties of a number of abolitionist leaders. He also identifies planters who returned to Britain with slave-made wealth as a boorish threat to the country gentry. Compelling histories of the abolition movement have been based on the idea that exogenous forces cemented surprising alliances (Brown 2006), and status deserves more attention in this light. Additionally, our focus on the commercial partnerships of the time provides a new methodology for examining the impact of the myriad political, social, and cultural forces on actual Britons as they pursued their fundamental interests (see also Trapido 2013).

CONCLUSION

We have studied entry into the slave trade to illuminate the interdependence between culture and structure. Ours is not the important but familiar argument that “culture matters,” but instead that the workhorses among structural effects must be understood as products of cultural context. We demonstrate this in an analysis in which the influences of network ties, and of status, differ in predictable ways in different cultural conditions. Even when they are doing middle-range theory, economic sociologists should make cultural contingency salient in the interest of producing an integrated literature. They can do this by attending more to values in theorizing, for example, by developing the concept of cultural holes in network theory and by asking “what kind of norms based on what values?” when examining the relationship between status and deviance. Continued reliance on contextualist methods, including history, is also called for.

Sources and Uses of Data

The Trans-Atlantic Slave Trade Database (TAST; http://slavevoyages.org/tast/index.faces) compiles data on all known slave voyages throughout the entire transatlantic slave trade period, between 1514 and 1867. We use the data for Liverpool-departing voyages to identify slave voyages undertaken by Liverpool ships and the shipowners involved in these voyages. The Liverpool Plantation Register database (compiled by David Richardson, Kathy Beedham, and M. M.Schofield) is a series of computer files compiled by historians of Liverpool’s maritime trade. The files cover a range of data, including information on all vessels registered at the port of Liverpool between 1734 and 1784 (as recorded in the official Register of Vessels at Liverpool) and on all voyages known to have been undertaken by these vessels (as recorded in voyage registration documents, insurance documents, and newspaper advertisements and articles). These data cover both slave trade and non-slave trade voyages. We use these data to identify the nonslave voyages undertaken by Liverpool ships and the shipowners involved in these voyages. We also use these data to double-check the TAST data on slave voyages. Although the Richardson et al. compilation ends in 1784, the original Liverpool Plantation Registers continue well past 1807, the end of our observation window. Historian Stephen Behrendt is in the process of compiling all subsequent records and graciously shared with us the vessel registrations and voyage information for Liverpool vessels from 1785 through 1807.

Beginning in 1766, John Gore began publication of Gore’s Liverpool Directory, Containing an Alphabetical List of the Merchants, Tradesmen, and Principal Inhabitants of the Town of Liverpool with Their Respective Addresses. This directory was published roughly every three years through the mid-1800s. We used this directory to identify occupations of those shipowners whose occupations were not provided in the Liverpool Plantation Register database and to double-check the occupation data that were provided in that database. Similarly, we consulted voting records for Liverpool over the period we study for yet another source of data on the occupations of shipowners. By integrating the information in these sources, we were able to identify occupations for more than 98%98\% of all owners appearing in our data set.

The Records of the Mock Corporation of Sephton were accessed directly by us in the National Archives housed at the Liverpool Record Office. These were complemented by reference to Horley (1893), who wrote a history of Sephton that relied extensively on the Records of the Mock Corporation. He transcribes some records that we have not found the originals of. The records present summaries of meetings. They also present a comprehensive list of members, which provides information on when they joined in most cases. We relied on this list to construct the associational network of the Sephton Club. The members list also presents the legal status of the members, which we used in some cases to fill in status data that were missing from other sources.

The Gale Collection of British Newspapers, 1600–1950, includes the most comprehensive digital collection of British newspapers covering the 18th and 19th centuries. The collection includes digitized copies of virtually all national, regional, and local newspapers published in Britain and stored at the British Library.
