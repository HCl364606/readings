ORIGINAL PAPER

Efficiency and the Fix Revisited: Informal Relations and Mock Routinization in a Nonprofit Nursing Home

Steven Henry Lopez

Published online: 10 April 2007

C Springer Science+Business Media, Inc. 2007

Abstract Participant observation in a nonprofit nursing home reveals that informal patterns of work routinization depart markedly from official procedures designed to protect the health and safety of workers and residents. Six aspects of the informal organization of work are found to correspond closely to patterns observed by Roy (1954): the mismatch between time and tasks, the development of new (informal) skills, the institutionalization of rule-breaking, negative effects on quality, the collaboration of shop-level supervision, and workers’ experience of managerial irrationality. However, whereas classic manufacturing studies emphasized upper management’s periodic attempts to force compliance to official rules and routines, here upper management engages only in symbolic interventions, collaborating with workers and nursing home residents in the “mock routinization” of work. The article concludes by showing how, in the context of contradictory external workplace regulation, all three parties to the labor process of the contemporary nursing home experience mock routinization as compatible with their own interests.

Keywords Routinization . Bureaucracy . Service work . Nursing homes

All organizations experience some slippage between official rules and actual practices. Sometimes, however, formal and informal ways of doing things diverge into parallel universes: official rules, procedures, and routines become fundamentally disconnected from real-world behavior. Such was the situation at Heartland Community, a well-staffed, non-profit nursing home in a large Midwestern city, where I was trained, licensed, and employed for five months in 2004 as a part-time, day-shift nurse’s aide. Throughout my fieldwork, I confronted a series of vexing questions. Why did non-profit managers with a demonstrable commitment to an ethic of care consistently avoid enforcing basic safety rules? Why were genuinely-caring workers willing to jettison official procedures, designed to protect their own safety and the safety of nursing home residents, in favor of informal procedures that were clearly less safe for both groups? Even more puzzling, why didn’t residents of the nursing home – at least those who were most cognitively intact – object to informal procedures that increased their risks of falling or getting sick?

Neo-institutionalist discussions of how formal organizational structures can become “decoupled” (Meyer & Rowan, 1977) from actual organizational processes are relevant to these questions. So is the notion of “three-way interest alliances” in the routinization of service work (Leidner, 1993). Unfortunately, neither of these two approaches is really satisfactory. The neo-institutionalist perspective helps us understand the external environments in which organizational decoupling can become rational for managers, but tells us little about why workers or clients might go along. Leidner’s notion of three-way interest alliances in service work, on the other hand, provides a framework for understanding why service workers follow or disregard managerial rules – but improperly equates rule-breaking with “resistance,” ignoring both the informal side of routinization and the external context of organizations. As a result this framework misses a central neo-institutionalist insight: sometimes managers expect workers to ignore official rules, despite public pronouncements to the contrary.1

The critical sociology of work does contain a body of literature with the right conceptual tools to answer the questions posed above. Industrial sociology in the 1950s did focus – though in a very different context – on informal shop-floor relations, on the interplay between official and unofficial rules, and even (sometimes) on the relationship between these dynamics and analyses of external context. Industrial sociologists were very interested in explaining why factory workers and managers might both have interests in seeing to it that official rules go unfollowed. But does it make sense to view contemporary service work through the lens of 1950s industrial sociology? Aren’t factory work and service work too different to be comparable? I argue that they are not. Indeed, as I demonstrate in the first part of this paper, it turns out that certain processes of routinization in contemporary nursing home work are remarkably similar to the observations of Donald Roy’s (1954) classic analysis of work in a mid-twentieth-century machine-shop. Moreover, examining things that carry over from the industrial era helps to clarify what is new. In the second part of this paper, I adapt Alvin Gouldner’s classic (1954) notion of “mock bureaucracy” to understand how the contradictory imperatives of state regulation – and the interests of nursing home managers, care workers, and residents – all converge on what I call the “mock routinization” of work.

Organizational audiences, labor process contradictions, and routinization

Neo-institutionalists have shown that managers have important audiences (besides customers) in the external organizational environment; that organizational environments can exert contradictory pressures; and that, as a result, official managerial policies and rules created to satisfy these external audiences may conflict with other managerial goals (Meyer & Rowan, 1977; DiMaggio & Powell, 1983, 1991; Dobbin et al., 1993; Edelman & Suchman, 1997; Edelman, Uggen, & Erlanger, 1999; Clarke, 1999; Scott, 2001). Thus, managers may have official interests in seeing that policies and rules are followed, and unofficial (even, at times, unmentionable) interests in seeing that they are not. Neo-institutional organizational theorists invoke the notion of “organizational decoupling” (Meyer & Rowan, 1977) to describe the resulting gap between official policies and actual organizational practices.

Despite the importance of these ideas, however, neo-institutionalists’ focus on managers and their external audiences leads them essentially to ignore what actually goes on inside organizations, under the overarching assumption that the “internal legitimacy” of decoupled formal structures depends on the “confidence and good faith of their internal participants and their external constituents,” who cooperate in practices of “avoidance, discretion, and overlooking” in order to assure that “individual participants maintain face” (Meyer & Rowan, 1977, p. 358). Because neo-institutionalists have generally avoided studying work or the labor process, this assumption remains speculative. Neo-institutional theory actually has very little to say, beyond this, about how workers and managers interact, or about what workers actually do if they don’t follow official rules – and it has not taken up questions about customers at all. As a result, the perspective is better at explaining why particular policies are adopted by managers than whether or not workers or customers follow or subvert those policies (Barley, 1996).

In contrast, Robin Leidner’s (1993, 1996) influential study of routinization in service work examines the labor process directly in order to understand why service workers sometimes obey managerial rules and sometimes resist them. Her analysis of “three-way interest alliances” in service work convincingly demonstrates that service workers’ decisions to follow or resist managerial rules are always made in the context of complex, shifting, three-way power relations involving workers, customers, and managers. Sometimes managerial rules reflect the shared interests of managers and customers in controlling workers; at other times, managerial rules may reflect managers’ and workers’ common interests in controlling the behavior of customers. Workers, for their part, are particularly likely to resist managerial rules when they find that their interests and those of their customers align together against those of management. This framework clarifies much that was murky about obedience, resistance, and power in service work. Bringing customers into view allows us to understand, for example, why workers may resist or ignore managerial routines that annoy customers – even if managers very much want them carried out.

On the other hand, Leidner makes two mistaken assumptions about routinization. First, she assumes that it is driven entirely by considerations internal to the labor process, thus ignoring external pressures and audiences (see Leidner, 1993, p. 27). Second, she assumes that routinization is a process involving only official routines, thereby ignoring the possibility, highlighted by the neo-institutionalists, that tacitly-sanctioned unofficial routines may play a crucial role in getting things done while official ones legitimate the organization externally. These assumptions truncate Leidner’s ability to understand the contradictory yet symbiotic relationships between official and unofficial routines – and this is clearly visible in her own empirical analysis of Combined Insurance (Leidner, 1993). She notes that some insurance agents act unethically, using abusive or overly aggressive sales tactics, writing policies that the customer cannot afford, misrepresenting coverage in order to get a sale, or encouraging customers to cancel an existing policy in favor of a new (more expensive) one. All of these practices are against official rules. However, “in the past a local sales manager had encouraged the agents on his team to cheat customers” (p. 163), but these practices “would no longer be tolerated,” Leidner is told (p. 164). This of course implies that the practices had been tolerated, for some unspecified period of time, by upper management. Moreover, managerial encouragement for cheating means that a series of unofficial rules had been applied that ran counter to the official ones. Why had this been tolerated from above? Leidner offers no answers because she examines only the official side of routinization and does not look outside the labor process to understand the external pressures that impinge upon it.2

The unofficial side of routinization once figured prominently in industrial sociology (c.f. Roy, 1954, Gouldner, 1954; Blau, 1955; Kusterer, 1978; Burawoy, 1979, Juravich, 1985). Donald Roy’s (1954) classic study of machine shop workers provided the first empirical description of how contradictory dynamics involving official and unofficial work routines play out on the shop floor. Roy’s production workers routinely broke rules and subverted managerial authority, compromising quality in order to attain quota earnings. While officially against the rules, these compromises were tacitly encouraged by time-study men (the “henchmen” of top management, who set piece rates so low that the operators were forced to be creative in order to achieve quota earnings) and shop floor supervisors, who looked the other way or actively collaborated in rule-breaking. Workers’ “subversive organization,” strenuously opposed by upper management wherever it was found, helped realize the tacit managerial goal of sacrificing quality to output. Despite this, upper management periodically imposed new rules aimed at breaking the informal organization of the machinists in order to “expurgate all modifications and improvisations and force a strict adherence to the rules” (Roy, 1954, p. 258). These interventions were invariably abandoned because, as Roy shows, workers’ informal methods – i.e., “the fix” – were in fact crucial to production.

Thirty years later, Burawoy (1979) returned to the same machine shop (which had now entered the monopoly sector as an engine division of “Allied,” a large national firm) and observed the very same pattern, involving some of the very same “new rules” – leading him to ask, “if rules hamper production, why does management persist in imposing them?” (p. 174). Unlike Roy, who made no attempt to explain managerial behavior, Burawoy showed how this cyclical pattern of periodic managerial interventions emerges out of the opposing interests of different layers of management with respect to market regulation. Shop management is charged with meeting production targets; its external environment is not the market but rather the internal planned economy of the industrial corporation. In order to meet production goals, shop management “allows the labor process to drift in accordance with the dictates of making out, which increases costs and reduces quality” (176). Eventually upper management must intervene in the interests of continued profitability. The only available mechanism for intervention from a distance is the production of new rules, which are in turn resisted mightily by workers on the shop floor, who deploy all their resources to dramatize managerial dependence on their ability to rationalize production via their informal routines. Thus, Burawoy managed to do for the factory what neither contemporary neo-institutional theorists nor sociologists of service work have done: connect an analysis of the interaction between official and unofficial work routines in the labor process to an analysis of external context.

What is the significance of all this for the contemporary nursing home? In the first part of this paper, I compare Roy’s original analysis of Geer with Heartland nursing home in order to demonstrate that a series of distinctive, contradictory processes of work routinization, involving both formal and informal routines, are common to both workplaces. In both settings, I show, workers invented new skills, systematically breaking official rules and institutionalizing deviant routines in order to reconcile the amount of work with the time allotted. In both settings, they did so with the collusion of shop-level supervision, and in both settings the contradictions involved reinforced workers’ perceptions of managerial irrationality. Neither neo-institutional theory nor the contemporary sociology of service work focuses on these processes, which are at least general enough to cross the sectoral and historical gulfs separating Roy’s machine shop from Heartland nursing home. (I do not claim, of course, that these dynamics are found in every workplace and every kind of work; rather, the comparison reveals that the presence or absence of clients is not the distinguishing feature separating workplaces exhibiting these dynamics from workplaces that do not.)3

Highlighting what is the same allows us to see more clearly what is different. First, the most important aspect of the external context for the contemporary nursing home is not market regulation (as in Burawoy’s analysis) but state regulation. At Heartland, care standards are not imposed by upper management but from outside, by the state – which also sets limits on resources (by setting reimbursement rates for Medicare and Medicaid, the programs that pay for most nursing home care). Because these resources are insufficient to allow any nursing home to actually live up to mandated care standards, top managers do not try periodically to wrest control over the shop floor from workers but instead rely on shop floor organization and informal work methods to sustain the illusion that externally-imposed rules are being followed. Second, unlike machine shop workers, nursing home workers do not deviate from official rules in pursuit of either financial gain or symbolic victories against management. Instead, they accept the informal work procedures that are necessary for the normal functioning of the nursing home because a process akin to the “normalization of deviance” (Vaughn, 1996) allows them to feel that their own skill and experience mitigate the potential risks of informal procedures, rendering these procedures relatively safe. The virtuoso ability to perform difficult maneuvers safely in turn becomes an important intrinsic job reward and a source of shop-floor status. Third, unlike machines and raw materials, nursing home clients (especially the least cognitively impaired) can be active participants in the care process. To the extent that they accept their caregivers as skilled, residents accept workers’ informal methods (and may, in some cases, even insist upon them) because they have their own interests in ensuring that the care schedule itself is maintained in the face of obstacles.

If this analysis of routinization at Heartland nursing home is correct, it is very reminiscent of Alvin Gouldner’s (1954) classic notion of mock bureaucracy: externally imposed rules that both management and workers agreed to ignore. In Gouldner’s typology of bureaucracy, when labor and management both viewed bureaucratic rules as serving their interests, representative bureaucracy was the result. Punishment-centered bureaucracy obtained when one party tried to enforce rules while the other resisted. Gouldner’s key example of mock bureaucracy, drawn from the gypsum factory he studied, was the “no-smoking” rule, required by the factory’s insurance company but ignored by workers and managers alike because both groups wanted to smoke on the job. The virtue of Gouldner’s concept – distinguishing it from both neo-institutional theory and contemporary notions of “three-way interest alliances” in service work – is that it connected an analysis of the shop-floor interests of labor-process participants to the external context of the organization.

Adapting the idea of mock bureaucracy to the contemporary nursing home requires, first and foremost, examining clients’ interests alongside those of workers and managers. But it also involves deepening our understanding of the nature and consequences of external regulation for shop floor organization. A vast historical increase in external regulation in the nursing home sector, and the associated contradictory pressures, have catapulted mock bureaucracy from a minor organizational feature into the dominant one. Mock bureaucracy in this context does not concern one or two rules, externally imposed, that can be conveniently ignored; rather it takes the form of an entire universe of detailed work routines that cannot, in practice, be followed. Far from a process of official routinization a la Leidner, we encounter instead something we might call mock routinization. Unlike conventional work routines, which aim to regulate small increments of time, mock routines are very detailed in every respect except that they do not specify how long any of the operations ought to take. The fact that they inhabit and describe a time-free fantasy world is their essential and crucial feature, insulating management from official knowledge of informal work practices that are necessary to keep the workplace running. But mock routinization – unlike the idea of organizational decoupling – is not simply a managerial strategy for dealing with the external environment. Rather, as my analysis makes clear, it depends on specific constellations of interests in the labor process involving managers, workers, and clients, each of whom has their own reasons for participating in the mock routinization of work.

Carrying out the research

Heartland Community was a non-profit, non-union, religiously-affiliated nursing home with good staffing – one nurse’s aide for every nine residents on both the day and afternoon shifts. There were 82 beds divided across three “hubs” (public areas with a small office that replaced the traditional nurse’s station). From early April through the end of December, 2004, I conducted nearly 1,000 hours of participant observation there. My activities were divided into three phases. During the first phase (in April and May of 2004), I volunteered four days a week with the Activities department, assisting activities staff with the social and recreational program of the nursing home. During the month of June, I took and passed the state-approved nurse’s aide training course offered at Heartland, which included both classroom and practical instruction. In August I received my certification as a state-tested nursing assistant (STNA). From August through the end of the year, the period from which the data for this paper is mainly drawn, I worked two shifts a week (36 shifts in total) as a nurse’s aide on the day shift, for which I was paid the usual starting wage of $9.25 per hour.

I made no secret of my identity as a sociologist and initially negotiated entree to the site ´ by pitching my project to the nursing home’s executive director, the administrator, and the director of nursing. I also agreed to meet with management at the conclusion of the study and provide feedback on the nursing home’s organizational practices. I made it clear up front that I would restrict my comments to organizational issues only and would not report on the performance of my co-workers, a condition to which management readily agreed, and which I also communicated individually to my co-workers when I told them about myself and my project. I told each of my co-workers about my dual role the first time we met; the project and my identity were common knowledge in the nursing home and I often spoke with co-workers about it during the fieldwork.

Working regular shifts as a nurse’s aide – rather than relying on official records, interviews, or observations conducted on some other basis – was essential to the project. Very little of the interplay between organizational structures and everyday routines and interactions in Springer

direct-care areas of the nursing home is recorded in official organizational records. Also, since people’s descriptions of their own behavior are strongly influenced by social expectations and routinely diverge from their actual behavior, I considered this strategy to be superior to an interview-based approach. The labor process in question involved intimate physical care carried out in private, and I needed to know not only how the work was officially supposed to be done but also, in detail, how it was actually done.

I carried out my duties as an aide to the best of my ability, while looking to my more experienced co-workers for crucial help and advice without which I could never have survived a single shift “on the floor.” I participated in routine rule violations, and instead of hiding behind a fac¸ade of “neutrality,” I honestly shared my views of organizational and managerial practice when asked by co-workers to divulge them. Despite the potential risks, my coworkers overwhelmingly decided to trust me enough to teach me what they did and how they did it; specifically, they taught me how to circumvent official rules in order to get the work done. I do not interpret this as evidence that I was ever completely accepted as an insider, since my co-workers were aware of my privileged position and temporary status; but had they wished, they could have simply allowed me to fail as an aide by leaving my to my own devices. They did not do this, and I have tried to honor their trust by carefully protecting their confidentiality, and by attempting to call attention, via this and other work (Lopez, 2006a, 2006b), to the terrible choices our society forces on nursing home workers.

I recorded my observations in detailed field notes at the end of each shift. These were analytic field notes (Burgess, 1982) consisting not only of detailed accounts or “thick description” (Geertz, 1973) of everything that I did and observed during the shift but also of ongoing analysis of the meaning of, and questions raised by, these observations. I was influenced by the extended case method approach (Burawoy, 1998), in which initial hypotheses are revised as field observations refute or modify them. Each successive entrance into the field, therefore, became an opportunity to test ideas formulated during analysis of the preceding day’s field notes.

Comparing machine shop to nursing home

Studies of service work rarely undertake direct comparisons with manufacturing, generally assuming that they are too different to be comparable. And in many ways, Heartland Community and Geer, the machine shop studied by Roy sixty years ago (and then as “Allied” 30 years later by Burawoy), could not have been more different. Roy’s co-workers were white and male; mine were nearly all female and mostly black. Of these, nearly one-quarter were recent immigrants from West Africa and the Caribbean. The activities of these nursing aides, caring for the dependent elderly, also were a world apart from the activities of machine shop workers who drilled, punched, and shaped pieces of metal for a living.

Likewise different were the attitudes with which these two groups of workers approached their tasks. The enthusiastic efforts of Roy’s machine-shop operators to submit jobs they viewed as “stinkers” to the “whitest heat of intelligence and the most cavalier disregard for company property” (Roy, 1954, p. 257) in order to wrest victory in the form of quota earnings, can only be contrasted to the remarkably caring way in which my co-workers – in exchange for an exceedingly modest hourly wage – washed, bathed, dressed, fed, and comforted their residents. Aides playfully joked with residents, who often saved newspapers for them to read on their breaks. Aides worried when residents were seriously ill, and some came in on days off to attend funerals when “their” residents died. Months after the death of an elderly white lady, a favorite with aides because of her quick wit and live-life-to-the-fullest outlook, even the male aides choked up when they spoke of her. Heartland was thus a far cry from the industrial battle zone Roy describes. Nursing home residents for their part were often active participants in the care process, directing the work, complaining about it, praising it; at other times they actively resisted – cursing, hitting, screaming. The emotional tensions and stresses, as well as joys, of these relationships were obviously quite different from the satisfactions and frustrations machine operators experienced with their tools and materials.

But it was not only the nature of the work with human beings or workers’ approach to the work that separated Heartland from Geer. Roy writes in detail of Geer’s authoritarian management, of management “henchmen” against whom workers waged an unceasing battle. Management at Heartland, in contrast, aligned itself with the contemporary “culture change” movement in nursing home care (Weiner & Ronch, 2003), and expressed commitment to the premise that nurse’s aides cannot care compassionately for nursing home residents unless they are also accorded respect and dignity at work (see Thomas, 1996).

In consequence, Heartland managers from top to bottom generally saw their role as to nurture caregivers and to facilitate care work, and they even managed to live up to these principles much of the time. When they needed something done, for example, Heartland managers always asked nicely; I never ever saw anyone ordered about. A workplace culture of civility and respect prevailed that finds no counterpart in Roy’s descriptions nor in other descriptions of mid-twentieth-century factory life. Managers were effusive with thanks as well, and regularly offered praise for work well done. Even when workers made mistakes, I observed managers to be sensitive and kind in their interventions, handling issues respectfully but calling attention to changes that needed to be made. Aides appreciated this treatment and with only one or two exceptions viewed the respectful, friendly atmosphere at Heartland as one of the attractions of working there.

Given all of these divergences, it is reasonable to wonder whether Roy’s machine shop and my nursing home were not so different as to be incomparable. What is to be gained from a comparison of such apples and oranges? I can only tell the reader with wry understatement that I did not go looking for this comparison. I do not exaggerate when I say that it was forced on me by the absolute shock of recognition I experienced when I returned to Roy’s description of informal relations at Geer. My first reaction – like Michael Burawoy’s when he discovered he’d stumbled into Roy’s old shop – was horror: Roy had already said everything I wanted to say. More slowly I realized that although many of the dynamics identified by Roy in the machine shop had their counterparts in the nursing home, these dynamics did not play out in the same way. Thus, although the analysis of the next section emphasizes continuities, I do not question the distinctiveness of service work, nor do I discount the importance of historical changes that have occurred between Roy’s fieldwork and my own. The sociology of service work, in its efforts to theorize work with people as distinctly different from work with things, has (understandably perhaps) neglected to focus sufficiently on the things that are more continuous, the elements that carry over in some way. The point of refocusing on these elements, as I have noted above, is not to argue that there aren’t real differences between service work and manufacturing, or that those differences aren’t important. Rather, identifying what is the same allows us to see more clearly what has changed.

Efficiency and “the fix” revisited

The interplay between official and unofficial routines contains within it a series of dynamics that can be found on both sides of the sectoral divide. First, in service work as in manufacturing, workers are often given quantities of work that they find impossible to carry out in the Springer

time allotted if they follow official procedures and routines. Second, figuring out how to meet managerial production quotas forces workers to develop new skills. Third, rule-breaking is itself unofficially routinized, not in order to resist managerial goals but in order to carry them out. Fourth, as a result quality is compromised. Fifth, supervisors facilitate and collude with routine rule-breaking. And sixth, these dynamics reinforce workers’ perceptions of managerial irrationality. These six points are drawn from Roy’s (1954) analysis of the piecework machine shop, but as we shall see they also describe many of the fundamental experiences of work at Heartland Community nursing home. I recognize explicitly that these points do not provide the basis for a complete analysis of care work in the nursing home. Rather, these elements call attention to neglected elements that are crucial for understanding routinization in the contemporary nursing home setting.

The mismatch between time and tasks

Roy’s machine-shop co-workers contended: “You can’t make out if you do things the way management wants them done” (Roy, 1954, p. 257). At Geer, “the canniest operators often gave their best in timing duels only to get ‘hopeless prices’ for their pains” (p. 257). In these cases workers could not achieve quota earnings if they followed official procedures for doing the work. Although nursing home workers at Heartland did not do piecework and thus were not concerned with “making out,” a parallel could be observed in the mismatch between time, tasks, and official procedures on the day shift, particularly in its crucial first 90 minutes.

During this time each aide was officially required to perform rounds, physically checking nine residents to see if they were wet. She was to pass linen to these residents, and to rouse as many as six of them – six residents who then needed to be changed or toileted, washed, dressed, and transferred to wheelchairs. They needed their teeth brushed, their hair combed and/or braided; men needed shaving; the bed needed to be made; and dirty linen, soiled clothing, and trash had to be taken to utility room. Finally, the resident could be taken to the dining room. These tasks, taken together, were known as “A.M. care.” Official routines spelled out in great detail how each of these jobs were to be completed. Although space does not permit a complete description, a single example will convey the level of detail at which these tasks were specified. There was a specific, official procedure for precisely folding and refolding the soapy washcloth used to wash female residents’ perineal area; aides were to wipe exactly nine times – three times in each of three locations – using a different area of the washcloth each time. The same procedure was then supposed to be repeated with a different cloth used for rinsing. Most of the tasks aides did were as closely regulated.

The very first and most fundamental fact of life for aides at Heartland Community was the absolute impossibility of completing all of the tasks associated with A.M. care for more than three residents in the allotted 90 minutes, if one followed official procedures. Yet seven of the nine day-shift assignments required workers to deliver A.M. care to more than three residents before breakfast. According to my calculations, five of the nine assignments permitted aides following official procedures to spend 15 minutes or less on each resident’s AM care (see Lopez, 2006b for a detailed discussion). Three assignments had six residents requiring AM care before breakfast; two assignments had five; and two assignments had four. On these assignments, following official procedures was an impossibility not only for me but also for highly experienced aides.

Time pressures and skills

Roy reports that machine operators were forced to develop new skills in order to turn “stinkers” into “gravy.” Geer operators applied creativity and intelligence to the problem of shaving time from jobs in order to attain quota earnings despite tight timings. The “hopeless price” did not always remain hopeless but “surprising metamorphoses sometimes took place” (p. 257) as operators invented new ways of working. At Heartland, a parallel process could again be observed. In my training course I was taught, and tested on, twenty-three specific skills, ranging from how to take vital signs to how to change a bed with an elderly person in it. Most of these skills were wholly inadequate to the real tasks of care “on the floor.” Once I arrived there I was taught by other workers, of necessity, an entirely new set of skills for accomplishing the same tasks in less time.

For example, in place of laborious procedures involving a hydraulic machine called a “standing lift,” which allowed a single worker to change the adult diaper of a resident who could bear some weight but could not stand up safely for long, my co-workers taught me to perform the “stand-up change,” in which one aide hoisted a resident out of a chair and held her upright while a partner whipped down the resident’s pants and changed her diaper in an eyeblink. The entire job could be accomplished in less than a minute, as compared to the ten minutes that might be required to go find the standing lift, position the sling behind the resident and attach its canvass loops to knobs on the lifting arms, raise the resident slowly to standing position, change her, lower her back into the chair, remove the sling, and put the lift away.

Although I am not focusing here on the communicative aspects of such operations, I do not want the reader to be left with the impression that I was in any way oblivious to them; these were social interactions as well as physical ones. Any attempt to describe these relations fully would obviously rely heavily not only on Leidner’s theory of three-way relations, on Hochschild’s concept of emotional labor, and even on my own notion of “organized emotional care” (Lopez, 2006a). Residents were often active participants in these events in ways that machine tools and materials are not – a point I return to below. The limited point I make here is that Roy’s (1954) discussion of skill – specifically, his observation that workers are forced to invent new skills in order to carry out informal routines that contravene official organizational goals – adds something important to the work of Leidner (1993, 1996) and Hochschild (1983), who both view routinization as a form of deskilling. Even subsequent discussions of interactive skills in the literature on service work that emphasize the depth of service workers’ interactive skill (e.g. Paules, 1991, 1996) do not capture this informal-skill-producing dynamic.

The routinization of rule-breaking

Rule-breaking at Heartland, as at Geer, was not done haphazardly but systematically. The list of rules that were routinely broken in the quest to complete AM care in under 15 minutes at Heartland is too long to enumerate here (see Lopez, 2006b), but a few examples illustrate the point. The “stand-up change” described above not only ignored rules governing use of the mechanical standing lift but also relied on an illegal technique for raising the resident from the chair. (Aides simply leaned in, locked their arms underneath the resident’s armpits and behind the resident’s upper back, and stood up, bringing the resident with them.) This technique was the de facto standard for all “transfers”; no one ever used the required “gait belts” for assisting patients from bed to chair or the reverse. Workers often ignored rules requiring two aides to perform transfers with mechanical lifts; sometimes workers did not use the lifts at all but simply picked residents up and carried them from bed to chair.

The list of deviations goes on: it was unheard of to actually do rounds at the beginning of the shift. No one ever followed official procedures for washing residents, which involved two washbasins – one soapy, one clear. I never heard of nor did I ever observe an aide who bothered with washbasins at all; whole minutes could be eliminated by dispensing with them. Perineal care in practice never followed the detailed rules for folding and refolding washcloths so that each of nine specific wipes would use a clean surface; instead it often meant a perfunctory, single swipe between the resident’s legs with a soapy cloth, before the resident was turned and washed from behind. Rules against leaving residents on the toilet alone were also routinely violated; one could hardly afford to waste five or ten minutes waiting and watching while a resident sat on the toilet. The only way to get done in time was to get the resident up on the toilet and go on to the next resident. In cases where residents could not be trusted to remain seated, aides ignored nursing orders requiring that they be assisted to the toilet; instead they changed them in bed, often substituting adult diapers filched from other residents’ supplies for the supplied pull-ups worn by residents who used the toilet.

Compromising quality

At Heartland Community just as at Geer, workers’ efforts to “get the work out” necessarily involved compromises in quality. At Geer this meant producing parts that did not conform to specifications. At Heartland, when aides disobeyed rules for lifting and transferring patients, they increased the risk of falls and injuries to residents (as well as to themselves). When they washed their hands less frequently or less thoroughly than they should have because they were in a hurry, they increased the risk that they might transmit infectious diseases – to which the elderly and sick are especially vulnerable – from patient to patient. When they did not perform rounds they increased the chance that a resident’s suffering would go unnoticed until later in the shift. And of course, the very effort to complete care tasks in extremely compressed periods of time impaired the quality of nursing home care by forcing workers to give short shrift to the emotional aspects of care (Stone, 2000; Cancian, 2000). Many nursing home residents, who are much more likely than the non-institutionalized elderly to suffer from loneliness, helplessness, and depression (Thomas, 1996), evaluate the quality of their care primarily in terms of the quality of their relationships with their primary caregivers (Bowers, Fibich, & Jacobson, 2001). But these relationships were themselves undermined by the way that workers were forced to rush through care tasks as efficiently as possible.

It might be tempting to view the situation as simply a case of managerial and worker interests aligning at the expense of powerless residents. But workers were breaking managerial rules, not following them – and moreover, in doing so, nursing home aides not only placed their patients at risk but also assumed serious legal and physical risks themselves. Paradoxically – a point to which I return below – it would seem to be in workers’ and patients’ interests for workers to insist on following the rules that management officially enforces but tacitly allows them to break.

Managerial collusion in the routinization of rule-breaking

Both Roy (1954) and Burawoy (1979) discuss how floor supervision and local plant management tacitly approve of, collude with, or ignore certain kinds of routine rule-breaking at Geer/Allied. This dynamic was also readily observable at Heartland. To be sure, some rules were strictly enforced, especially those whose violation posed immediate health and safety risks that were readily visible to visitors. For example, around midmorning on one shift,

Charlene, a unit supervisor (an R.N., one rung above the L.P.N charge nurse), approached me as I was walking through North Hub, and asked me if she could speak with me for a moment. “I just came in,” she said, “and I noticed something that really is not O.K.” Charlene was tacitly acknowledging that there were many rule violations that management did not enforce, but here was something different. She led me to a room where one of my residents was sleeping. “Now,” she said, “What’s wrong with this picture?”

I looked at the resident and said with dismay, “Oh no, I left her side rail down.” Whenever a resident was left unattended in bed, the side rail was supposed to be raised to prevent a fall. “Right,” said the supervisor. “And what else?”

I looked but didn’t see anything else wrong. “I’m not sure,” I said hesitantly.

“You left the bed raised up to the highest position instead of lowering it when you were done,” she said gently. Again I expressed remorse. She continued, “I know you’re trying hard and you’re doing fine. Pretty soon these details will be natural for you. Even experienced aides make mistakes like this too, so don’t feel too bad.”

Charlene handled the infraction nicely, but the message was clear: here were rules that management would enforce. Allowances were being made because I was still fairly new and had made an honest mistake, but I was being warned against repeat offenses. And there were excellent reasons for enforcing this rule: when the bed was raised to its highest position, it was nearly four feet off the floor. A resident who rolled out of such a bed would likely be seriously injured.

On the other hand, other rules with equally serious implications were not enforced. For example, on another occasion, the very same unit supervisor who called attention to my mistake in the previous example condoned the practice of leaving residents unattended on the toilet. One afternoon I had helped Mrs. Walker to the toilet, and told her to use the call light to let me know when she was done. Mrs. Walker could not stand up safely by herself, even with her walker; she needed someone there to assist her to a standing position and clean her. While she was on the toilet I answered another light – Mrs. Brown was suffering from diarrhea and had had a bowel movement. It took just under ten minutes to clean her up. As soon as I was finished with Mrs. Brown, I headed out to check on Mrs. Walker. When I got to the hub I saw that her light was off. Charlene saw me checking and said, “Hey, I knew you were busy, so I got Mrs. Walker off the toilet for you.” I thanked her gratefully. “No problem,” she said. “It’s my job to answer call lights too.” She made no comment about the fact that it was against the rules to leave residents unattended in the bathroom, thus signaling an understanding that this was an acceptable deviation.

The experience of managerial irrationality

Finally, we find in Roy’s analysis of the above dynamics at Geer the recognition that workers have good reason for believing that it is they who are rational and management who is irrational. Roy is careful to say that he does not in fact believe that management is irrational (though he makes no real attempt to explain managerial behavior). Still, the reason it so often appears that way to workers has to do with conflicting managerial expectations. At Geer, the time study “henchmen” set rates that necessitated corner-cutting; at the same time the inspector tried to enforce quality. It is dynamics like these that so often lead workers to despise management for its irrationality and ignorance.

Similar dynamics were visible at Heartland. Workers were conscious of the impossibility of following official care rules and routines, and thus always had good reason to view official versions of reality cynically. Management continually claimed (often, in my view, sincerely) Springer

that it cared about quality – but the necessity of ignoring important care rules in order to fulfill job assignments, the failure to enforce care rules with clear quality implications, the crosspurposes at which managerial interventions often worked, made this a difficult proposition for workers to swallow. There seemed to be only two possibilities: either management knew how things were really done, in which case the rules were a joke and management’s claims to care about quality were bogus – or else it did not know, in which case (in workers’ view) management was incompetent. Workers sometimes took one view, sometimes the other, but either way management could only appear as the object of derision and ridicule.

The interests of customers can become central to considerations like these in service work, but workers do not view management as irrational simply because managerial interests oppose the interests of customers or themselves. Workers can understand management’s pursuit of its own interests, but what they often find more difficult to understand is the way managerial practices so often undermine management’s own interests and even management’s own pronouncements about what it considers important. This is what creates the feeling among workers that management is irrational, clueless, idiotic, and screwed up. This feeling is common to many different kinds of work.

Mock routinization and “the fix”

So far we have considered how aspects of routinization at Geer and Heartland nursing home were similar. But there were key differences in how routinization played out in these two settings. In order to see these differences, let us return once more to Roy (1954). Upper management at Geer, Roy writes, periodically promulgated new rules aimed at regaining control over the labor process:

The first new rule during this study went into “effect” less than two months after the writer was hired. It was designed to tighten controls in the tool-crib sector, where attendants had not only been passing out set-ups ahead of time but allowing operators or their setup men to enter the toolroom to make the advance pickups themselves. An aim of the new rule was to curb the operators’ practice of keeping “main setups” at the machines instead of turning them in at the completion of operations (Roy, 1954, p. 261).

Thus, consistent with Gouldner’s (1954) view of punishment-centered bureaucracy, management identified a problem, issued new rules aimed at fixing it, and then had to try to enforce those rules. Roy goes on to describe how the rules introduced new inefficiencies and new problems: under the new system, work was often delayed for hours while tools that operators once had readily available at their stations were located. Management in the end threw in the towel and allowed workers to revert to their customary practices. He concludes, “The ‘fix’ was on again, and operators and their service-group allies conducted business as usual for the remaining weeks of the author’s employment” (Roy, 1954, p. 265). Thirty years later, as noted above, Burawoy (1979) observes the very same pattern; unlike Roy, Burawoy links this cyclical pattern of enforcement and relaxation to the conflicting demands of market regulation on shop supervision and top management.

At Heartland, the pattern was very different. Top management did intervene periodically on the shop floor, but these interventions took a different form. Instead of attempting to assert control by demanding adherence to new rules aimed at “breaking” the existing system of informal organization, managerial interventions at Heartland took the form of polite reminders, in-service trainings, and posted notices, all of which could easily be (and were)

essentially ignored by front-line caregivers. These interventions were, in other words, entirely symbolic. Here, unlike at Geer/Allied, upper management were in on “the fix” as well.

An example of this was top management’s response to the problem of urinary tract infections (UTIs) in the resident population. During the period of my fieldwork, Heartland experienced a spike in UTI rates that showed up in its official quality indicators. Because poor perineal care is a leading cause of UTIs in nursing homes, top management responded to the UTI rate increase by scheduling mandatory individual “brush-up” trainings with Maureen, a registered nurse and the director of staff development. When I went to the classroom to take the brush-up training, Maureen asked me to demonstrate, on an anatomically-correct dummy, how I did peri care on a female resident. I decided that I had better do it the way I had been trained (as opposed to the way I had been taught to do it on the floor), so I got out the two basins and made eighteen separate swipes with the two washcloths, refolding the cloths in the approved manner each time. Maureen said, “That’s very good! You’re doing peri care correctly. We need to make sure that we do it this way every time.” She went on, “A lot of aides have just gotten out of the habit of doing it the proper way. They just need a reminder, you know, to get back on track.”

Clearly, poor peri care was a problem at Heartland, but not because workers “needed a reminder” to do it right. The real reason was that following the official procedures was out of the question given the amount of time workers were given to complete their tasks. At first, I interpreted Maureen’s comment as evidence that management was simply disconnected from the realities of the floor; she simply did not understand the way things really were. But gradually I realized that my own desire to take Maureen at her word had caused me to suppress an important fact: Maureen, now an important manager, had begun her nursing home career as an aide. She had worked as an aide for many years, before attending school part time to earn her L.P.N. and then R.N. degrees. It finally dawned on me that she understood full well the realities of the floor, and thus, undoubtedly knew very well that holding a “brush-up” training to “remind” workers how to do it properly would in no way solve the problem.

Ifthe symbolic nature ofmanagerial interventions was one difference between Geer/Allied and Heartland, a second had to do with the unusual nature of official routines. Whereas normal routines are concerned with regulating the pace of work, and thus with regulating small increments of time (either directly or via financial incentives, as in the piece rates of Geer/Allied), official work routines at Heartland carefully described every step of specific care procedures but, oddly, omitted any mention of how long each step ought to take.

I got my first glimpse of this during the training course, when Maureen taught the class on proper handwashing technique for preventing the spread of infectious disease from one resident to another. This was the first of 23 required skills for nurse aides, and one we were supposed to enact before and after caring for each resident. Maureen first applied an invisible dye to our hands that would show up under an ultraviolet light, and instructed us to wash off all the dye. I scrubbed for what seemed like a long time, then submitted my hands to the UV scope. There was lots of dye under and around my nails, between my fingers, and I had missed the entire area between thumb and forefingers on both hands as well as my wrists. It took several more minutes of scrubbing to get it all off. “Anywhere the dye remained,” Maureen said, “germs could have remained as well. Thorough hand-washing is the most important way to prevent the spread of infectious disease.”

I timed Maureen as she demonstrated how to do it right: eight specific steps that took nearly a minute and ended with hands dried and ready for work. (It was important to get one’s hands absolutely dry because it is extremely difficult to don latex gloves if there is any moisture anywhere on the hands.) The steps were listed in our training materials, but the fact that it should take nearly a minute to complete them was not. But with nine residents to check Springer

during rounds, I realized, following these procedures would mean spending about sixteen minutes (nearly twenty percent of the total time available for A.M care before breakfast) on handwashing before the real work of the shift had even begun. If we washed our hands the way we were taught, I guessed, we might even spend an hour out of each eight-hour shift just washing our hands. Alarmed, I mentioned these thoughts to Maureen. “I never really thought about it like that before,” she said. “But you’re right, it’s a challenge. You just have to do the best you can.”

Every official routine we learned had the same blindness to matters of time. Procedures for transporting residents, interactive routines for communicating effectively with residents – all were detailed but none were timed. These official silences about time were essential to the mock routinization of work. If mock bureaucracy (Gouldner, 1954) consisted of specific rules that workers and management agreed to ignore, mock routinization depended on the existence of official, detailed work routines that were vague about how much time their steps required, and which in practice none of the parties to the labor process sought to follow or enforce. But in order to understand how and why mock routinization emerged as the dominant organizational pattern at Heartland, we need to understand the specific external pressures leading management to pursue it, as well as how and why both workers and nursing home residents could come to feel that mock routinization was consistent with their interests. In the next three sections, I examine the interests of each group of labor process participants in turn.

Upper management and mock routinization

Part of management’s motivation for mock bureaucracy at Heartland might have stemmed from the fact that, in the nursing home, there was no piece-work system to manipulate, so informal routinization did not increase costs, but, on the contrary, helped to reduce them. On the other hand, informal routinization obviously compromised the quality of care, and upper management had many reasons to care about that. Thus, to understand why Heartland management did not attempt energetically to intervene on the side of official routines, we have to understand the contradictory, external pressures to which they responded.

Contemporary nursing homes face a great deal more external regulation than manufacturing firms did sixty or even thirty years ago. Part of this has to do with the general increase in workplace regulation during the last half of the twentieth century, but more specifically, the nursing home labor process has been subject to increasingly stringent federal regulation during the last forty years. An understudied aspect of work involving people is that it can be risky for customers and clients (see Heimer & Stevens, 1997 for a rare examination of organizational risk created by customers).4 In nursing homes, frail and vulnerable people are objects of intimate care; the labor process itself exposes them to serious risk of physical harm. The particular vulnerability of nursing home residents created political constituencies whose actions have radically transformed the regulatory environment in the decades since the nursing home industry assumed its modern form in the 1960s.

During the 1970s and 1980s, federal regulation of the nursing home industry was essentially limited to the requirements of the Medicare and Medicaid programs through which most nursing homes were paid, and basic fire and safety rules created by the Hill-Burton Act of 1954. In the 1960s federal guidelines for state licensure programs were published, but there were no uniform federal standards of care, or even any federal training requirements for the nurse’s aides who performed most of the care work. State licensure programs did not reliably enforce Medicare and Medicaid requirements for certification, and licensure inspections continued to be geared toward surveying the physical plant rather than assessing the quality of care (Institute of Medicine, 1986). During the 1970s and 1980s, however, a series of influential publications (Mendelson, 1974; Vladeck, 1980; Institutes of Medicine, 1986) documented the existence of appalling conditions in American nursing homes and helped to create political pressure for fundamental nursing home reform.

This pressure culminated in the Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act (OBRA) of 1987, which ushered in a new era of nursing home regulation as its requirements were phased in during the first half of the 1990s. The law created training requirements for nurses’ aides; staffing requirements for registered nurses; and spelled out the specific rights of nursing home residents. OBRA required nursing homes to conduct regular assessments of each resident’s needs using a standardized Resident Assessment Instrument (RAI), and to create and follow individualized care plans for each resident, aimed at maximizing residents physical, mental, and psychological capabilities and well-being. Although the RAI is primarily a clinical tool, each nursing home is also required to report a standardized subset of the data it collects on each resident to the state to facilitate external analysis of nursing home quality. And although inspection and enforcement functions remained with the states, OBRA created new federal standards for nursing care; food service; housekeeping and maintenance; and medical services; and set out a range of sanctions states could employ against nursing homes that did not comply.

Nursing home managers very much want to avoid findings of “deficiencies” in state inspections. These inspections are ordinarily annual but can also be triggered by complaints from residents or family members. Deficiencies are reported publicly at www.medicare.gov and thus have the potential to adversely affect future admissions (and, therefore, future revenues). Serious deficiencies could, in principle, lead the state to suspend the nursing home’s license, suspend admissions, or take other corrective action. Thus, top managers know that keeping their jobs depends in part on having trouble-free state inspections.

Another set of liabilities care standards pose for nursing home managers is the risk of being sued for not living up to them. Even though nursing home residents’ ability to advocate for themselves is often limited, there is no question that nursing home managers want to avoid lawsuits from residents or (more likely) family members in the event of an accident or other major care problem. Lawsuits at the very least cost money to defend; at worst they contain the potential for bad publicity as well as adverse financial consequences. Nursing home administrators consider litigation to be a major source of strain on industry resources. Nationally, there were more than 750 care-related lawsuits against nursing homes in 2001 (Johnson et al., 2003), which means that about five percent of all nursing homes were sued in that year alone. 88 percent of all claims are settled out of court, and the average amount of these settlements is over $400,000 (Stevenson & Studdert, 2003).

The difficulty for top nursing home managers – even in non-profit nursing homes like Heartland where every effort was made to maximize staffing – is that the existing funding mechanisms in Medicare and Medicaid do not allow them to hire enough staff to actually live up to the care standards spelled out in OBRA. According to a recent study by the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services, the minimum level of nurse aide staffing necessary to guarantee good care would be between 2.9 and 3.2 nurse aide hours per resident day (U.S. Department of Human Services, 2002). At Heartland – where staffing was substantially Springer

better than the industry average – there were 2.4 nurse aide hours per 24-hour resident day. Meeting the proposed staffing standard would have required Heartland to increase nurse aide staffing by more than 20 percent – and even this may still be too low (see Lopez, 2006b).

Medicaid, the program that pays for long-term care for the indigent, pays for nearly 70 percent of all nursing home patient-days – but, because Medicaid reimbursements are substantially less generous than Medicare reimbursements, Medicaid payments account for less than half of nursing home revenues (Rhoades and Sommers, 2001). The Department of Health and Human Services report concludes that even if all nursing home care was reimbursed at the higher Medicare rate, it would not be enough to cover the nursing costs associated with a hypothetical minimum nurse aide staffing level of 2.9 nurse aide hours per resident day (DHS, 2002). Given these realities, no level of staffing supported by the current federal reimbursement system would allow nurse aides to actually live up to official care standards. Therefore, nursing home managers are caught in a difficult bind. On one hand, they need to be able to demonstrate to a range of external audiences that they are doing everything possible to deliver high-quality care that meets or exceeds official standards; on the other hand, externally-imposed resource limitations mean that there is no question of actually living up to these commitments.

An analysis of these tensions allows us to understand why management pursued mock routinization; but it also allows us to understand the differential enforcement of individual rules. The rule requiring beds to be lowered and side rails to be raised, discussed above, did not interfere with informal routines of care, and thus could be enforced within the constraints of the external environment. The rule requiring aides to stay with residents while they used the toilet, however, could not be enforced without causing chaos. During A.M. care, following this rule would have been out of the question without significant – and unaffordable – increases in staffing. But even during the afternoon hours when things weren’t quite so busy, strictly enforcing this rule would have increased the amount of time residents waited for call lights to be answered. This increase would have shown up on the computerized record of each call signal and the time it took to answer it – and these records were subject to review by state inspectors. For all these reasons, management allowed workers to use their own judgment to determine which residents could be trusted to remain seated and call for assistance when they were finished with the toilet. Workers, for reasons I explore next, assumed the concomitant risks.

Nurses’ aides and mock routinization

The tensions outlined above do not, as Meyer and Rowan (1977) hypothesize, lead nursing home managers to adopt an attitude of good faith toward workers. Managers do not simply assume that workers are following official procedures and overlook or discount instances in which they do not. Rather, they make bad-faith public statements about the importance of following official procedures – statements that workers recognize as such because they understand that they would actually be punished for taking them at face value. The following incident illustrates the point.

One morning about one month into my shift work as an aide, the Director of Nursing (DON) covered for an absent charge nurse on my unit. At the mid-morning “huddle,” the DON asked each of us how things were going. When my turn came, I said that I was feeling more comfortable about being able to do the job, but that I still worried about being too slow. She replied, “Steve, it isn’t about how fast you can go, it’s about giving good care. It’s not a race. We’re not concerned with speed here.”

Later, one of my co-workers who’d witnessed this exchange told me, “That’s bullshit.” She snorted and went on, “When you’re new, of course everyone understands that [you’re slow]. But over time, if you’re not getting your residents to the dining room on time, aides will complain to the nurses and you’ll start getting written up. If it keeps happening and you don’t improve, you can be fired for that.”

Her assessment of the importance of speed in official evaluations of performance was confirmed when a unit manager (also a nurse) delivering a verbal summary of my 90-day probationary review said, “Steve, I’ve asked the charge nurses and other aides about how you’re doing. They tell me that your residents are clean and neatly dressed. Your interactions with residents are appropriate and positive, and you’re getting to the dining room on time in the mornings” (my emphasis).

Thus we see that workers are not simply “participants” in “ceremonies” or “rituals” but rather that management threatens real sanctions against those who might insist on following official routines and pronouncements (a similar point about nursing homes is made by Foner, 1994). Like machinists at Geer who respond to time-study’s real incentives by bending and breaking official rules, nursing home workers respond to the real pressures imposed on them in practice and recognize that managers who claim that time isn’t as important as doing the job right do not mean what they say. In this way, the external pressures on managers are transmitted downward and applied to workers as well.

But while the threat of sanction against those who might stubbornly insist on following official routines is an important part of the explanation for workers’ participation in mock routinization, by itself this explanation fails to capture the willingness with which workers assume the risks involved in unofficial routines. Workers know that the official routines really are designed to decrease the risk that they or their residents will be injured during lifts and transfers, and to minimize residents’ exposure to infectious disease. The puzzle is to explain why, instead of resisting mock routinization, or at least resenting it, workers identify mastering the informal routines of nursing home care as the definition of skill itself. Why do they view themselves as good, competent caregivers insofar as they are able to subvert official routines that are actually in their own interests (as well as in the interest of residents)?

Part of the answer is that nurses’ aides do consider the interests of residents in their decisions about how to deliver care. Since aides cannot do anything about the mismatch between time and tasks, deviation from official routines is the only way to make sure that everyone gets at least some minimal, unofficial standard of care. In the context of individual action, refusing to deviate from official routines on the grounds that those routines are safer for residents and themselves would actually increase the misery of residents who would have to wait longer for care. Thus, aides feel that their unofficial efforts are, in fact, rather heroic and represent, if anything, a sacrifice undertaken for the benefit of residents themselves.

Thus, at Heartland the question was not whether one could “make out” by shaving time off jobs but rather whether one could “get done.” At Geer/Allied, machinists who saw that quota earnings were unattainable slacked off, engaging in “goldbricking” (Roy, 1952). At Heartland, not getting done on time entailed serious human consequences: vulnerable and dependent elderly people went uncared for. Aides exerted themselves to avoid such outcomes in part because the suffering of residents in such situations was genuinely heart-rending. As a result, there was no goldbricking at Heartland: everyone always tried hard to “get done” on time, even when it was not possible. When there were “call-offs” and we were “working short,” aides who weren’t “caught up” worked at a frantic pace.

This understanding moves us beyond simple managerial compulsion, but still presents a picture of workers compelled by exigency to adopt informal routines they experienced as contrary to their own interests. In order to understand more fully how workers viewed the Springer

informal routines they carried out, we must understand how the transition from apprentice to skilled caregiver affects the experience of risk. My own experience recalled Diane Vaughn’s notion of the normalization of deviance (1996). Procedures I found shockingly risky at the beginning, as a relative outsider – such as singlehandedly picking up a frail resident and carrying her to her chair – began to seem normal as I mastered the technique. In each case, the first time I tried such a procedure (after having had it demonstrated to me by other aides who were trying to teach me), I was concerned about the risks yet motivated by the desire to maintain the care schedule. But as I mastered these unofficial routines, I began to feel that, in fact, they weren’t as risky as they had looked from the outside. As my confidence in my own skills increased, I began to feel – rightly or wrongly – that I could manage the risks involved, that they were small and therefore acceptable, and that I would never cross the line between the marginally and the truly risky. Thus, by the end of my fieldwork as an aide, I was quite comfortable with routines that I initially found shocking. I knew, of course, that in the aggregate, the informal routines were not as safe as the official ones. But this came to seem unimportant as I learned to believe that I knew how to tell the difference between what was really safe and what was not.

Moreover, part of the prestige-enhancing effects of mastering informal routines lay precisely in acquiring the ability to do routinely and safely things that were theoretically risky. Thus, as I began to feel more and more confident in my ability to follow unofficial routines safely, I began to feel more and more successful, and my co-workers began to regard me as a good, reliable aide. Similar to the observations of Blau (1955) about the solidaristic functions of asking for and receiving advice in bureaucratic organizations, giving and receiving assistance involving the mutual performance of unofficial, time-saving procedures created solidaristic ties between the aides while simultaneously validating the unofficial routines. The first time I really felt accepted by my co-workers occurred when another aide approached me and asked me to help her with the “standup change” of one of her residents, in exchange for which she proposed helping with one of mine. In sum, not only do workers view informal routines as serving managerial interests and the interests of residents (at least given the structural circumstances in which care is delivered), the informal normalization of risk allows also them to feel that these practices are not incompatible with their own interests as workers.

Residents and mock routinization

Many nursing home residents cannot exercise the degree of agency that the notion of threeway interest alliances presumes. Therefore it is tempting to assume that residents were victims: mock routinization was not in their interest but they simply lacked the ability to object effectively.

This is an accurate description of certain kinds of situations, especially those involving residents suffering from advanced Alzheimer’s Disease or other dementias. Such residents were not able to control very much about what happened to them or how it happened. Even if their family members visited regularly and took an active interest in their care, these visitors generally were not able to observe the process of care but only its result. In other words, they were able to see that their loved one was clean, dry, and that his or her basic physical needs were being met, but it was not easy for them to find out how they got that way.

In other kinds of situations involving more cognitively intact residents, however, things were more complicated. For these more able residents, keeping to the care schedule was usually very important. Most residents wanted to be awakened at just the right time, assisted with dressing at just the right time, taken to breakfast on time, and assisted with toileting (if, like the vast majority of residents, they required such assistance) at the appointed time, for example, promptly after breakfast. In general, they did not experience disruptions to their normal routines as pleasant experiences. Routines are reassuring, and the ability to have things happen when one expects offers some sense of control over one’s life. Therefore these residents asserted their own interests in keeping to the schedule (even if in a larger sense their interests as human beings were not necessarily perfectly served by the regimentation of an institutional life).5 I soon learned the ire I would encounter if I attempted to give AM care to Mrs. Cassi before breakfast instead of after. Although Mrs. Henry did most of her own AM care, she favored being awakened at precisely 6:45 (as I discovered by awakening her at 7:15). And, I learned, woe unto the man (or woman) late for Mrs. Mercer at 7:00, or at 9:15 after breakfast, when she needed assistance going to the toilet.

As a result of their own desire to keep things on schedule, I found that cognitively-intact residents generally were willing participants in the informal routines of the nursing home, and that, in fact, they only objected under special circumstances – such as receiving care from a new aide, someone they were not yet sure of.

My encounters with Mrs. Peters illustrate this point. During my “orientation” period to shift work, when I was still accompanying experienced aides to learn their assignments, Charles demonstrated for me the steps involved in Mrs. Peters’ morning care routine. Mrs. Peters took a bedpan before breakfast, ate breakfast in bed, and then received morning care after breakfast. Charles showed me how to wash and dress her lower half while she lay in bed. Then he positioned her electric wheelchair so that it faced the bed. He lowered the bed all the way, raised the head of the bed so that Mrs. Peters was sitting up, and helped her swing her legs over the side so that her shoes were touching the floor. Taking hold of the waistband of her corduroy pants, in one smooth motion – Mrs. Peters was a short but heavy woman – Charles pivoted her through a standing position and into the seat of her wheelchair. He turned to me and said, “See, this is how I do her.” This was a more-or-less standard method of transferring residents from bed to chair if they could bear their own weight (except for the fact that technically it was supposed to be done with a gait belt) – but Mrs. Peters’ legs were really too weak to bear enough weight. Charles, however, was strong enough to bear this weight during the transfer, and it was clear from Mrs. Peters’ reaction that this was a routine event.

A few weeks later, now working on my own, I was given Mrs. Peter’s assignment. I managed to remember most of the details about the timing of her care, but just as I was about to transfer her to her chair, Mrs. Peter said, “You need the lift.”

Thinking she was only offering advice, and knowing that Charles never used the lift to make this particular transfer, I said, “No, this is OK.”

“No,” she insisted. “I need the standing lift. I’m gonna fall.”

Still not understanding, I said, “Don’t worry, Mrs. Peters, I’m not going to let you fall.” “No!” she said emphatically. “Go and get the lift.”

At this point I finally realized that Mrs. Peters simply did not feel comfortable allowing me to transfer her the same way Charles did. Although I am male and reasonably strong – though not as big as Charles, who was taller, fitter, and obviously infinitely more experienced as an aide – she was not sure of my abilities, not sure she could trust me to keep her safe. Therefore, she insisted that I follow the official procedure in this case and use the standing lift, even though it took longer. And Mrs. Peters was particularly concerned about the time, because her routine was to be dressed, up in her chair, and have all of her things – water, Kleenex, book, telephone – organized at her side before her favorite religious television program began. On that day, the program started before we were done. She was a little bit annoyed about this, because it meant that she had to interrupt the normal order of things to tell me to get the remote control, turn on the television, change the channel, and position her chair so she could see the screen; and then of course she had to try to peer around me as I helped her with her dentures and fixed her hair.

In sum, residents were not an undifferentiated group. While some residents essentially played no role in making decisions about how their care was delivered in practice, others took an active role and had their own reasons for collaborating in the mock routinization of the nursing home, helping aides set aside official procedures designed to protect them both.

Conclusion

The analysis presented in this paper draws on insights from two literatures: first, the neoinstitutionalist idea that pressures in the external organizational environment may induce managers to “decouple” formal procedures from actual organizational practices, and second, the notion of three-way relations of control in service work. However, this paper goes beyond both perspectives by showing that the mock routinization of work cannot be fully understood either by an analysis of managers and their external audiences or by an analysis of internal labor-process relations alone. As I have tried to demonstrate here, in order to understand how mock routinization emerges as a coherent and stable form of routinization, it is necessary to explain both the external pressures leading management to pursue it and to examine why workers and clients do not feel compelled actively to resist.

Mock routinization is a notion with wide potential application in the study ofcontemporary organizations and work, in both service and manufacturing settings. The rise of external regulation in the areas of workplace safety, environmental protection, and human resources practices has demonstrably led to the growth of new formal structures and, likely, to a good deal of organizational decoupling. But attempts to decouple formal rules from actual practices can lead to different outcomes depending on whether the disjuncture is accepted or resisted by the other parties to the labor process. Neo-institutional theory has assumed that the gap is unproblematic for all participants – but as this analysis shows, it can have negative consequences for workers and their clients even if it is accepted by them. In such situations, the absence of conflict in the labor process must be explained. Conversely, if workers and/or clients do resist (leading to some form of punishment-centered bureaucracy in which they try to coerce management into providing the resources necessary for following the rules) the processes leading people to decide that informal procedures are incompatible with their interests would also need to be understood.

Mock routinization is also particularly significant because its dynamics are quite different from those of punishment-centered bureaucracy. Punishment-centered bureaucracy carries with it more conflict, as one or more parties to the labor process periodically attempts to force the others to follow the rules, or to resist others’ attempts at enforcement. But the lack of conflict in mock bureaucracy is not necessarily a positive thing. In the nursing home sector, as I have tried to show here, the absence of overt conflict may only signal that all parties to the labor process have found ways of adapting to the systematic under-funding of nursing home care. Moreover, in punishment-centered bureaucracy, parties to the labor process may seek to reduce conflict via negotiation, compromise, and accommodation. At

Geer/Allied, for example, labor-management conflict was gradually reduced between 1945 and 1975, in part, by reducing the number of “stinkers,” making it easier to “make out,” and thus relaxing the time squeeze. The relative absence of conflict in mock routinization creates no such corresponding pressures for change. Absent external intervention, mock routinization, unlike punishment-centered bureaucracy, seems to create relatively stable (bu hardly optimal) “solutions” to the time squeeze problem.

These considerations tell us something about the possibility of improving the quality of nursing home care by legislating higher care standards and/or enforcing them more strictly. Highly punitive sanctions might goad some for-profit nursing homes into raising staffing from minimum legal levels to something closer to the staffing levels at Heartland, but as this study shows, even well-staffed, non-profit nursing homes like Heartland cannot meet existing care standards. Therefore, beyond a certain point, enforcement of care standards will not lead to better staffing but will only promote mock routinization. The analysis presented here suggests that raising mandatory minimum staffing levels and adjusting Medicaid and Medicare reimbursement rates upwards would be a necessary (though probably not a sufficient) condition for the emergence of representative bureaucracy in the nursing home field.
