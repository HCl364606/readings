9. Scientific nationalism in a globalizing world Creso Sá and Emma Sabzalieva

INTRODUCTION

Science is an increasingly globalized activity. Not only is science performed in a growing number of settings, but the make-up of the international scientific community has become more diverse in recent decades as globalizing processes have increased flows of people and knowledge across national borders. New networks of academic ties and scientific collaboration are evidenced in measures such as joint publications, cross-national research projects and in the funding and governance of science. And yet despite repeated claims about the impending erosion of national borders, there are similarly insistent counterarguments that the state still matters to and for science. We argue that these debates are a reflection of the tensions that surface when stable but competing sets of beliefs and principles about an organizational field coexist.

In both interpretations, science and politics are inextricably linked, especially in the realm of academic science, the object of analysis in this chapter. Academic science encompasses activities that take place in institutions with an academic research mission, including universities, research institutes, and other related organizations specific to certain countries. In the case of academic science, these contrasting beliefs and principles can be framed as scientific globalism on the one hand, and scientific nationalism on the other. In this chapter, we frame these ideas conceptually as institutional logics. We apply these ideas through a study of a global sample of national science policies, examining established, emerging and evolving economies in all parts of the world. We use this analysis to explore how scientific nationalism has been understood and interpreted in an array of global contexts. Through this analysis we demonstrate not only how scientific nationalism is manifested in national plans and strategies, but also how there has been a remarkable convergence across the countries in their articulation of national goals and visions. We identify the tensions that arise when scientific globalism intersects with scientific nationalism. We find that while scientific nationalism remains the pervasive logic, it is continuously negotiated and shaped by the interplay of diverse and sometimes contradictory ideas.

INSTITUTIONAL LOGICS

The metatheoretical lens of institutional logics brings together the theoretical notions that (1) cognitive and social processes shape the behaviour of individuals and organizations; and (2) multiple and often contradictory logics coexist in society, creating tensions and also providing actors with opportunities to exercise agency (Friedland and Alford, 1991; Thornton, 2004; Thornton and Ocasio, 2008; Thornton et al., 2012). This theoretical perspective, in line with the broad new institutionalist thinking in the social sciences, rejects rational-choice models and atomistic views of individual and organizational behaviour. Instead, an institutional logics perspective highlights the cultural and cognitive aspects of human action, as well as the socially constructed and reproducible nature of institutions and norms through which individuals and organizations express their identities and preferences.

Institutional logics may develop at multiple levels. These might incorporate individual organizations or entire industries, specific markets and communities, or society as a whole. At each of these levels, institutional logics can be seen as ‘organizing principles’ (Friedland and Alford, 1991, p.248) or ‘belief systems’ (Scott et al., 2000, p.170) that provide order for social worlds. They are a source of identities, roles and categories that individuals and organizations employ to navigate industries, professional fields, and other social arenas. One pertinent definition explains institutional logics as ‘socially constructed, historical patterns of cultural symbols and material practices, including assumptions, values, and beliefs, by which individuals and organizations provide meaning to their daily activity, organize time and space, and reproduce their lives and experiences’ (Thornton and Ocasio, 2008, p.4).

The institutional logics perspective emphasizes cultural symbols and material practices, considering them to be connected and mutually reinforcing (Thornton et al., 2012, p. 10). Having been employed previously to examine different organizational fields, including higher education, institutional logics provide a useful tool to examine the field of academic science (Bastedo, 2009; Thornton and Ocasio, 2008). We thus employ this perspective to understand which beliefs, assumptions and norms related to academic science reach the political agenda, how national science policies purport to address those issues, and why some ideas become legitimate within and across countries.

SCIENTIFIC GLOBALISM AND SCIENTIFIC NATIONALISM

In conceptualizing these institutional logics, we recognize the cultural and historical contingencies of the field of academic science. Academic science today is closely associated with Anglo-European academic traditions and institutions, which nonetheless stem from and encompass a range of contextually grounded cultural practices from many different societies (Boden, 2004; Fan, 2007; Key Chekar and Kitzinger, 2007). Academic science as an organized, government-supported and -legitimized endeavour has diffused across the globe over the past century, with greater intensity from World War II. State investment in science during World War II is epitomized by the first large-scale science project, the Manhattan Project, which galvanized the involvement of the American federal government as a research sponsor (Shore and Zollo, 2015). Adherence to the idea of sustained government support for academic science was subsequently based on a ‘science-push’ model, wherein significant investment in research was expected to (eventually) benefit national security and economic growth (Hellström and Jacob, 2005). In parallel, the cultural authority of science has greatly expanded, along with its influence on educational systems, medical practice, and economic and industrial affairs, among other aspects of society (Drori et al., 2003).

Universalist ideals were constitutive in the rise of modern science, as reiterated by scientists themselves. In the nineteenth century, Louis Pasteur professed, ‘science knows no country, because knowledge belongs to humanity, and is the torch which illuminates the world’ (Dubos, 1950). More recently, biologist E.O. Wilson claimed that science, like art, ‘is a universal possession of humanity, and scientific knowledge has become a vital part of our species’ repertory’ (Wilson, 1998, p.294). The idea of scientific ideas and practices crossing borders, just as knowledge cannot be constrained by geography, has existed for centuries. Terms such as ‘scientific cosmopolitanism’, ‘transnational science’ (Lipkowitz, 2014) and ‘scientific internationalism’ (Manzione, 2000) have all been employed to describe the universal value of scientific knowledge, the need for unrestricted scientific exchange, and science as a ‘language that we can share across cultural and political borders’ (UNESCO, 2015, p.10). Such commonly evoked sentiments entered the sociological lexicon through Robert Merton’s (1942) work, which identified universalism as one of the four norms of science.

Merton’s norms offered a set of four ‘cultural values and mores governing the activities termed scientific’ (Merton, 1942, p. 268). Firstly, in the norm of universalism, identifying factors such as nationality, religion or personal qualities in a scientist are irrelevant: the pursuit of science is impersonal in this sense. Universalism pertains both to an intellectual outlook – that scientists ought to evaluate knowledge claims on their intrinsic merit, regardless of who makes them – and to the organization of the scientific enterprise, as Merton argued that expertise should be the defining criteria in the appointment of scientists. The second norm of communism – also referred to as communalism – denotes scientific knowledge as communal property. Any gain from scientific discovery or output should be related to ‘recognition and esteem’ (Merton, 1942, p. 273), not privatization or commercialization. Thirdly, scientists are disinterested because they are swayed only by the evidence and pursue their own academic interests without being distracted by the demands of funders with their ulterior motives. Finally, the norm of organized scepticism suggests that scientists engage in an ongoing questioning of scientific ‘facts’ and ‘truths’ within their disciplines.

Merton’s norms were first developed during World War II. Following the dramatic impact of the Manhattan Project’s invention of the atomic bomb, there ensued a ‘crisis of conscience’ (Hellström and Jacob, 2005, p. 453) in science around the world, as the social consequences of scientific discovery and collaboration became readily apparent. As the Cold War unfolded and former European colonies became independent nations, the connections between science and society became more intertwined. During this time, science paradoxically played a crucial role both in supporting national government platforms but also in the continuing pursuit of the ideal of the international scientific community speaking up for reason and truth (Fan, 2007). We define this ethos as constituting a logic of scientific globalism. Here, science is characterized as a global endeavour with norms deriving from the scientific community, cutting across political, ethnic and cultural borders. Academic science is not a means to an end but a quest for discovery, oriented towards universalist ideas such as the betterment of human society.

The basis for the scientific mission is intellectual, regulated not by national governments but by disciplinary networks, as famously articulated by Polanyi (1962) in his idea of the ‘Republic of Science’. This logic is most explicitly enacted in transnational large-scale, or Big Science, projects, the goals of which are significant and legitimated by expert communities around the world. These projects go beyond individual nation-states, obtaining support from consortia of national governments, regional organizations as well as transnational funding agencies. In the scientific globalism logic, the Republic of Science operates through research fields across national borders. Here we see, for example, advocates of ‘open science’ making use of Internet technologies to share data with peers and indeed with the wider world, driven by an altruistic desire for knowledge advancement (Lasthiotakis et al., 2015). Such normative orientation is part and parcel of scientific culture, and often expressed by scientists in national policy debates.

By contrast, in the scientific nationalism logic, support for science accrues from and is intended to derive benefits to nation-states. Scientific nationalism has been interpreted in a range of ways over different periods of time, just as the idea of nationalism has variously been seen as a doctrine, an identity and a cultural movement (Breuilly, 2013). From the nineteenth century, the notion of nationalism has had an overtly political dimension because of connections to the development of nation-states, particularly in relation to those ‘modern’ nation-states in today’s ‘West’. Since the contemporary model of the nation-state has become institutionalized, science has been critical to state bureaucracies. Indeed, the same Louis Pasteur who claimed that science knows no country also asserted that ‘science is the highest personification of the nation because that nation will remain the first which carries the furthest the works of thought and intelligence’ (Dubos, 1950).

The political dimension of nationalism became more relevant in the colonial era, when science became an ‘essential element in the creation of national identity’ (Moon, 2012, p. 65). For instance, the failure of the anti-foreigner Boxer Rebellion (1899–1900) in China and the subsequent unfavourable treaty with the United States and Japan created a desire among scientists to ‘save’ the country from foreign domination through the development of science and technology (Wang, 2002). Science and technology were viewed as symbols of a new kind of modernity, a break from traditional Chinese learning. Similarly, Canada made a concerted investment in national science and technology to overcome a perceived dependency on the United States from the mid-twentieth century (Sá et al., 2013). The threat of economic dominance from Canada’s powerful neighbour underpinned nationalistic efforts directed at the application of science to the development of domestic industry.

Over the last three decades, nationalistic sentiment has become intertwined with the competitive arena of global knowledge-driven capitalism, reflecting a powerful trend in science policy towards the idea of innovation (Dill and Van Vught, 2010). Whereas science policies and government ministries to regulate science proliferated in the post-World War II period (Jang, 2003), the rise of policies encompassed by innovation has become widespread in recent decades, having first emerged in response to economic growth imperatives in the 1970s (Lundvall and Borrás, 2006). National policies on innovation now supersede ‘traditional’ science or science and technology policies because policy-makers make connections between human capital and the global knowledge economy.

Academic research institutions are understood as critical infrastructures supporting innovation. The mimetic behaviour of policy-makers regarding specific elements of this logic has been well recognized, for example in the now familiar aspiration to replicate Silicon Valley around the world, and in the usage of concepts such as innovation systems (Mowery and Sampat, 2006). Models of innovation have become common currency in science policy circles, providing schemas and scripts for policy entrepreneurs (Mintrom, 1997) and decision-makers (Sharif, 2006). Thus, where scientific nationalism in the nineteenth and early twentieth century was framed around nation-building, it is now more commonly associated with developing or consolidating national economic advantage.

Owing to these close connections to the nation-state, science is regulated primarily from above through laws, policies and regulatory frameworks. Support for academic science is found across a range of government ministries, and to a lesser degree in the private sector, as there has been an ongoing push in many countries towards greater academic–industrial cooperation. Extending support for science beyond the state has also led to a diversification of funding sources for academic science, from industry to philanthropic trusts and foundations.

Underpinning the logics of both scientific globalism and scientific nationalism is the state. More specifically, the state provides the key conceptual context to be supported or extrapolated in policy thinking towards science. States that consider themselves ‘modern’ do not question the authority and legitimacy of science (Drori et al., 2003, p.221). Rather, they continuously constitute the authority of science through claims of relevance, importance and impact for national affairs. The state has the ability through public policy tools to steer science towards certain aims and modes of work (Boden, 2004), and it does so in reference to institutional logics that are widely shared. NATIONAL SCIENCE POLICIES

A growing number of countries around the world have national science plans or strategies, which are now often presented as joint science, technology and innovation policies. The existence of such national strategies is no longer confined to the so-called ‘Triad’ of North America, Europe and Japan, as tended to be the case in the past (Archibugi and

Filippetti, 2015; OECD, 2012). These national policies are influenced by norms and ideas disseminated by transnational and regional organizations, as well as by other states (Drori et al., 2003).

International organizations are active actors in the dissemination of ideas and standards in this area, as well as in the formulation of science plans in some parts of the world. Such actors include the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD), United Nations Educational, Scientific and Cultural Organization (UNESCO) and the World Bank. Both the OECD and UNESCO support national policy reviews (OECD, 2012; UNESCO, n.d.), and through these reviews also offer technical advice and guidance on the creation and evaluation of national plans. The OECD has been pivotal in disseminating the concept of innovation systems since the 1980s, which has become extremely influential internationally as a framework for science policy (Sharif, 2006). Since the turn of the twenty-first century, the World Bank has radically changed its policy towards education, now providing significantly more support for tertiary education and, within this, championing academic science (Collins and Rhoads, 2010). The World Bank today maintains that an appropriate policy framework to support science, technology and innovation is a key plank in the development or creation of a knowledge-based economy in which science plays an important role (Agarwal et al., 2004).

Regional groupings such as the European Union and African Union similarly diffuse ideas about science policy, and support policy development within the remit of regional harmonization. Through both ideational and material inducements, they shape policy thinking and practice. For instance, science in the European Union is increasingly influenced by European programmes such as Horizon 2020, an €80 billion initiative aiming to enhance European innovation and competitiveness (European Commission, n.d.). The African Union’s Science and Technology Division has a mandate to develop and harmonize national education policies both to meet African challenges as well as to place Africa ‘firmly within the core of the global knowledge economy’ (African Union, n.d.).

At a national level, established economies that provide assistance to emerging or evolving economies also transmit norms and scripts about science policy through bilateral financial aid, and technical assistance in the form of supporting national reviews or by providing expertise.

We carried out an analysis of a global sample of 20 national science documents to examine how institutional logics get translated into these strategies and policies. Our focus in this study was on the public policies designed to impact upon academic science as a prominent and highly visible demonstration of scientific authority. Selecting the countries analysed in this chapter drew on the existence and public availability of a national plan for science or similar strategic document. 1^{1} We aimed to select countries from different continents that reflected a large base of the world’s population, and a mix of established, emerging and evolving economies. Considering widespread interest in the BRICS consortium, home to more than 40 per cent of the global population, we specifically included Brazil, Russia, India, China and South Africa as part of our country selection (Schwartzman et al., 2015). Details of the countries and plans selected can be found in Table 9.2.

Overall, in terms of the size of these 20 countries, nearly 60 per cent of the world’s population is covered by our sample. The six African countries (Ethiopia, Ghana, Morocco, Nigeria, South Africa and Tanzania) represent around 11 per cent of the total population of our sample, compared to 16 per cent of the world’s population. The five countries in Asia (China, India, Saudi Arabia, Singapore and South Korea) cover nearly 3 billion people, from the world’s two most populous nations of China and India to the city state of Singapore. In total this represents 66 per cent of the total population in our sample, compared to 60 per cent of the world’s population. In Europe the countries included are France, Germany, Russia and the United Kingdom (UK). The total population of these states is around 350 million, similar to the two North American cases of Canada and the United States (US), and both reflecting 8 per cent of the study size compared to 10 per cent (Europe) and 8 per cent (North America) of the world’s population. Less populous South America has 6 per cent of the world’s population; the two selected countries of Brazil and Colombia make up 6 per cent of our study size with a combined population of around 260 million. The smallest continent by population, Oceania, is reflected in our selection of Australia (population: 24 million), or 1 per cent of the study sample and also the world’s population.

In analysing the documents, we categorized the major cross-cutting public policy trends in the types of science being promoted, the funding instruments and regulatory levers being employed, and the states’ prioritization strategies. We then identified the discourses, missions and beliefs they communicate as they articulate goals and strategies. We sought to identify normative elements underpinning these factors, employing a constant comparative analytical approach across documents.

A COMMON VOCABULARY

A striking convergence is perceptible in the logics guiding national science policies expressing the desire to become (or remain) globally competitive, and in the importance placed on science (usually expressed as science, technology and innovation) to attain that goal. The policy rhetoric of these documents include statements such as ‘Strengthening Australia’s competitiveness is the key to our future prosperity’ (Department of the Prime Minister and Cabinet, 2014), ‘Canada is well-positioned to seize its moment on the world stage’ (Government of Canada, 2014), and ‘Science is one of our [UK] clear advantages in the global race’ (HM Treasury and Department for Business, Innovation and Skills, 2014).

It would not be surprising to see a degree of similarity among these majority Anglophone countries with a shared colonial history, but a similar theme runs through a diverse set of nations: ‘We [China] shall embrace the new era, meeting both opportunities and challenges brought on by the new science and technology revolution with a global vision’ (State Council, 2006); ‘[The strategy has the aim of] moving Germany forward on its way to becoming a worldwide innovative leader’ (Federal Government, 2014); and ‘India’s global competitiveness will be determined by the extent to which the science, technology and innovation enterprise contributes social good and economic wealth’ (Ministry of Science and Technology, 2013). Similar statements arise in countries such as Nigeria, Saudi Arabia and South Africa: ‘We are going to run our economy based on science and technology because nowhere in this world now [can you] move your economy without science and technology’ (Federal Republic of Nigeria, 2011); ‘Given its pivotal role in world politics and economics, it is essential for the sustainable development of the country [Saudi Arabia] to promote and develop the fields of science and technology’ (Council of Ministers, 2002); and ‘South Africa’s prospects for improved competitiveness and economic growth rely, to a great degree, on science and technology’ (Department of Science and Technology, 2008). Individually, these kinds of statements might be dismissed as empty political rhetoric. Taken together, they demonstrate a shared assumption about countries participating in the global landscape, articulated through similar rhetorical devices across starkly different contexts.

Our analysis shows a remarkable similarity across the countries in embracing this positioning, and in articulating national goals and visions through a common vocabulary. Tellingly, 15 of the 20 national policies we examined include the word ‘innovation’ in the title of the policy, whereas only 12 refer to ‘science’. In Brazil, the priority areas for future development are defined in terms of industry sectors rather than scientific fields (Ministry of Science, Technology and Innovation, 2016). In the French policy – one of the national plans that does not mention science at all in its title – science is clearly important, both as a factor causing change in the world and as a means of taking knowledge forward (Ministry of Higher Education and Research, 2013). Nonetheless, the policy also calls for the weaving together of basic technological research with a range of types of innovation. This is evident in the Moroccan policy too, which envisions Morocco as a society based on knowledge and innovation, where the role of research is to foster innovations (Ministry of Higher Education, Scientific Research and Training, 2009). As different as the current capabilities of the sampled countries are, the aspirations reflected in strategic visions and blueprints build on the notion of autonomous states competing in a global innovation economy, fuelled by the strength of their scientific infrastructures.

Prescriptions and cause-and-effect relationships buttressing national science strategies converge substantially. The orientation towards human capital development and the growing role for the private sector in academic science is widely shared. Table 9.3 highlights the rationales given for investing in science as a means of developing human capital in the policy documents: to skill up the labour force, generate jobs, make science an attractive career prospect, or more generally to lead to national economic benefit. Some of the science policies highlight national socio-cultural aspects, such as the need to sustain a certain level of scientific capacity, literacy and interest in the country. Evocations of scientific globalism are not as common here, but Saudi Arabia deviates from the norm by considering the impact of science on people’s ability to contribute towards building ‘a harmonious human civilization’ (Council of Ministers, 2002, p. 1).

Similar schema and justifications underscore policy problems and alternatives. Regardless of the substantial differences in the character of national industry and the nature of the scientific research base, we identified convergence on the notion of closing the gap between science and industry. Researchers are encouraged to exploit intellectual property and to partner with firms. Two examples from quite different contexts illustrate the commonalities in this area. The Ethiopian government seeks to create a conducive environment that will strengthen business in technology transfer and bring competitiveness in learning about and utilizing technology (Federal Democratic Republic of Ethiopia, 2012). This also forms part of the government’s broader development strategy that seeks to grow the role of the private sector in order to spread risk, diversify sources of economic growth and reduce pressure on the public sector (International Monetary Fund, 2011). In Singapore, which considers itself to have a strong scientific base, the starting point for the latest ‘research, innovation and enterprise’ plan is the ongoing alignment of national research with industry demand. According to the plan, these public–private partnerships have generated research and development jobs, created joint laboratories and generated a vibrant start-up ecosystem. Investment in academic science is justified as it leverages industrial research and development capabilities, nurtures innovation and meets national needs (National Research Foundation, Prime Minister’s Office, 2016).

The logic of scientific nationalism encompasses a range of regulatory frameworks governing academic science. Focal points can include national intellectual property regimes and patenting laws, higher education policies, and accountability and evaluation frameworks. These tools are designed to assert the importance of national parameters over academic science in terms of both how science itself is regulated and how its benefits are managed. Across the national policies that discuss the regulation of academic science, we found clustering around measures to enhance university-industry cooperation, and in accountability and evaluation frameworks. In South Africa, for example, ‘new mechanisms’ to monitor important indicators such as patents will be introduced under the country’s decade-long innovation plan (Department of Science and Technology, 2008, p. 32). These mechanisms are intended to contribute to the formation of human capital and thus the eventual transformation of the country into a knowledge-based economy. In Colombia, an intellectual property framework is presented as a new priority to exploit research for commercial and innovation purposes (Colciencias, 2008).

The conception of priority-setting is a strong touchstone across national policy documents. An emphasis on science-based technology has been part of the orientation of science towards innovation in recent decades, and this focus was clearly expressed in our study. Table 9.4 provides an overview of some of the scientific priority areas found in the national plans. Prioritization strategies have been found to be common across a sample of OECD countries since the early twenty-first century (Dill and Van Vught, 2010). Such strategies rest on the appeal of rationalizing the allocation of public resources, fitting well within the assumption of a cross-national innovation race. Winners will be those who can excel in some key areas of strength, the script goes, even if the eventual priorities are too broad to be discriminating, or if all relevant competitors place their bets on the same fields.

As the national plans were often designed to reflect political aspirations or to recommit to existing visions, not all were explicit about the funding instruments they intend to leverage. Where this was specified, as summarized in Table 9.5, we found that states employ a wide range of funding tools in order to sustain and advance academic science, but also to shape it politically towards the state’s political and economic agenda. In Germany, for example, the international nature of intellectual property protection illustrates how major scientific performers and innovators operate within the institutional context of rule-setting. Measures to enhance the legal framework and technical standards domestically are presented as the rationales for ratifying the European Unitary Patent, a European patent guaranteeing protection for inventions across 26 countries in Europe (European Patent Office, 2016; Federal Government, 2014). The key policy objective of adopting transnational norms specifically to ‘strengthen Germany’s competitiveness as an industrial nation and exporting country’ (Federal Government, 2014, p. 42) underpins ‘plans to work for international harmonization in these areas’ (Federal Government, 2014, p. 42).

The national science plans also converge on certain policy tools, which themselves embed theories of cause-and-effect relationships. Our sample provides further evidence of the growing popularity of competitive or project-based funding (OECD, 2014), which is believed to enhance research performance. Another lever is the use of funding to stimulate public–private partnerships to augment the innovation capacity of national industry, which appears to have become embedded in all of the countries to some extent. This policy also links to support for commercialization-related activities such as technology transfer and entrepreneurship programmes in universities. These have gained wide appeal as means of deriving local benefits from investments in the research base.

The logic of scientific nationalism is employed across settings with different political cultures, regime types and institutional arrangements. Liberal market economies such as the UK and Australia have pursued deregulation policies and commercialization agendas in academic science since the 1980s and continue to do so. In the Australian policy, this is explicitly stated as seeking to ‘profit from ideas’ (Department of the Prime Minister and Cabinet, 2014, p. 16). Conversely, other countries employ the same nationalistic logics to drive science by increasing direct state intervention. This type of vision can be seen in Russia, for example, where global competition underlies a host of state-led initiatives to increase scientific innovation, such as nearly doubling state funding for research and development in higher education by 2020, establishing a new ‘national research university’ status, and creating a government commission charged with identifying national science and technology priorities (Ministry of Economic Development, 2011; Russian Government Analytical Centre, 2014). Across these countries, the policy levers being employed are quite different in terms of the involvement of the state in steering science. Despite this divergence, these approaches are arguably all constructed through the logic of scientific nationalism.

TENSIONS AND CONTRADICTIONS AT THE INTERFACE OF GLOBALISM AND NATIONALISM

The dominant logic of scientific nationalism is well anchored in a global institutional order that secures academic science within a framework of nation-states. Nonetheless, scientific globalism provides a competing frame of reference for discussions around the nature and purposes of academic science. The interplay between these different sets of beliefs and normative frameworks populates science policy documents. In part because of the policy processes in which these logics are negotiated, fragmentation and contradiction are manifested within and between different institutional logics (Friedland and Alford, 1991). Thus, many of the countries we surveyed employ the narrative that science is global – much as Merton argued with his four norms of scientific universalism – even as they emphasize science as a national asset bound by legal regimes of intellectual property and economic appropriation.

Tensions and contradictions are manifested as ideas enter the policy process when a range of policy actors with disparate value systems and normative orientations are brought into science policy-making. This is becoming even more complex as science policy is increasingly enmeshed across the government apparatus, and is perceived as a core policy concern. In South Korea, for instance, the national science plan was developed across ministries and departments. The planning committee organized by the South Korean government worked in conjunction with the Korean Academy of Science and Technology, the Korean Academy of Engineering and more than 60 associations and individual advisers to come up with a long-term blueprint for national scientific and technological development (Government of Korea, 1999). In Tanzania, the Research and Development Policy calls for the development of a national framework to facilitate greater coordination between different ministries, government departments and agencies, as well as to support the identification of research priorities. The government also notes that the policy is relevant to non-governmental actors such as the private sector, nongovernmental organizations, development partners and the Tanzanian diaspora (Ministry of Communication, Science and Technology, 2010, p. 9).

A common tension across the science plans is that between input and output, where academic science is posited as both the realm of unfettered discovery, and as the bounded pursuit of intellectual property. In the French policy, for example, the two central stated aims are to respond to societal challenges and to safeguard basic research. The underpinning logics for these aims are national pride and bringing knowledge forward (Ministry of Higher Education and Research, 2013). In the British strategy, this tension is overt: ‘While the importance of science and innovation go beyond the economic – they elevate and improve mankind and offer rewards that are beyond price – any nation should ask itself how it will earn its living in the future’ (HM Treasury and Department for Business, Innovation and Skills, 2014, p. 5).

A recurring theme of our analysis comprehends the rationales for international and global dimensions in science policy planning. While many of the national policies discuss the importance of bilateral and multilateral international cooperation and the need to tackle increasingly complex global challenges, only a few policies offer clear measures to manage these issues in pursuit of competitive outcomes. In South Africa, the science plan makes a direct connection between the global nature of science and the need for its scientists ‘to be well connected with the world body, not only developing collaborations across the African continent but also tapping international resources’ (Sooryamoorthy, 2015, p. 226). Other countries, particularly those that have relatively recently built up their scientific capacity, use ‘internationalization’ as a strategy towards greater incorporation into global science. Singapore has actively sought to increase international presence in the country in order to develop national intellectual capital (National Research Foundation, Prime Minister’s Office, 2016). This has been done through direct outreach, such as in recruiting high-flying non-Singaporean researchers to work in the country, significant investment in facilities and equipment, and in policies that promote local innovation (UNESCO, 2015). This is a very similar strategy to that employed by Saudi Arabia, which seeks to derive benefit from international and regional organizations, especially in fields that are priority areas for the country (Council of Ministers, 2002). States thus seek to compete and to collaborate, not always resolving issues that arise at the interface of these efforts.

The collaborative nature of science also underscores the emergence of cross-national research networks. This is a major policy measure in China, for example. In environmental sciences in particular, the Chinese policy expresses the need to ‘vigorously participate in international cooperation’ in order to strengthen research, develop technologies for monitoring the environment globally, and to support the implementation of international conventions (State Council, 2006). In the case in India, the national policy very clearly lays out the economic rationale for expanding partnerships and involvement in international projects to enable access to facilities that may not be available in all parts of the country, or indeed in the country at all. Participation in transnational projects helps to share the large cost of resource-intensive modern science as well as enhance India’s prospects of gaining global experience and competitiveness by sharing access to cutting-edge large-scale international facilities (Ministry of Science and Technology, 2013).

The idea of academic or scientific mobility is another area that features strongly in many policies. This refers to the unrestricted flow of science, as embodied by the movement of people as well as ideas. The upward swing in the number of mobile scientists has ‘markedly increased the pool of skills and resources each country can expect to tap into’ (OECD, 2014, p. 39). At the same time, economic conditions around the world are improving, with many countries seeking to establish themselves as ‘middle-income’. From our sample, Ghana and Morocco are good examples. Ghana’s ambition to become middle-income ‘requires a vision of development which fully applies and integrates STI [science, technology and innovation]’ (Ministry of Environment, Science and Technology, 2010, p. 5); the vision for Morocco in 2025 is of a Muslim and modern country with ‘an open and competitive economy strengthened by a strong society’ (Ministry of Higher Education, Scientific Research and Training, 2009, p. 2). In many other countries, temporary outbound mobility is encouraged, with scientists ultimately returning to utilize the skills and experience gained abroad for the betterment of their home country. This is the rationale behind many international exchange programmes and similar initiatives.

For Nigeria, the goal of improving international cooperation through exchanges and partnerships is to become an active global player in science activities and to benefit from global expertise. Along with some other countries that have previously experienced ‘brain drain’, the Nigerian policy also aims to engage the Nigerian diaspora with a view both to tap into their international experience and, potentially, also to attract them back to Nigeria (Federal Republic of Nigeria, 2011). Through such policies, the advantage once enjoyed by more established economies is shrinking as participation expands, quality increases and mobility grows in more countries. For a country like the United States, the world’s largest recipient of international students (OECD, 2015) and a major importer of international scientists, this has created a ‘policy problem’ of a perceived threat of eroding global leadership (National Economic Council and Office of Science and Technology Policy, 2015). This suggests a continuing requirement for the national talent pool to be enhanced as it has in the past by internationally mobile scientists.

A final trend we found was an emphasis on specialization permeating science policy narratives. In countries that are choosing to specialize in particular areas of science and that also have a stated aim of reducing poverty and/or inequality, a tension emerges. To reduce poverty and inequality requires a general infrastructure of scientific expertise to be built, not only in particular areas of research but across the board. Without investment in basic science, such as through the training of researchers in core disciplines, the link to a springboard towards applied science and technology is difficult to make. In many emerging and evolving economies, ‘the focus of scientific discovery has shifted from basic research to “relevant” science, in order to solve pressing developmental challenges’ (UNESCO, 2015, p. 6). Some find in the very weaknesses of the scientific base a liability to achieving that goal. The Ghanaian strategy expresses a long-term objective to create endogenous science, technology and innovation capacities and ‘science and technology culture’. However, those aspirations are based on guiding principles defined by cost-effectiveness, relevance (that is, selectivity) and pragmatism: ‘whereas Ghana desires to reach the frontiers of STI application and development, there is the need for realism in aspirations and efforts’ (Ministry of Environment, Science and Technology, 2010, p. 18). This duality is also evident in the Colombian plan, which emphasizes university–business collaboration while acknowledging, but not addressing, the need to build much greater scientific research capacity (Colciencias, 2008).

CONCLUSION

Scientific nationalism has been the dominant institutional logic in the science policy field internationally for the last 70 years. Both historically and in the present, the scientific nationalism logic has coexisted with the scientific globalism logic, rooted in the very constitution of the idea of modern science. Policy orientations and frameworks that emphasize the nation and its capacity to innovate within a competitive global landscape provide the overarching paradigm within which policy actors operate in today’s world. This institutional order is so ingrained that it is arguably taken for granted as a global way of thinking about science policy. The convergence between the wide-ranging country settings we examined pays testament to this authority. This contemporary expression of scientific nationalism nevertheless continues to thrive alongside the logic of scientific globalism, which is rooted in the ideas that underpin what academic science is and how it is performed. These normative orientations are part and parcel of scientific culture and are often articulated by researchers in policy discussions. Our analysis of science policy documents demonstrated the tensions and contradictions between these logics that are manifested not only within but between widely varying settings.

Future research on the sense making of policy actors across national settings, particularly those that are currently less central to global academic science, would provide a valuable contribution to our understanding of the local interpretations and instantiations of scientific nationalism. Equally helpful would be detailed empirical accounts of the cognitive and social processes whereby different policy actors embrace, adapt and manipulate institutional logics in national settings, constituting and reconstituting scientific nationalism over time. Further work on the role of the scientific community in articulating and bringing about major initiatives in global academic science is also needed.

Previous research shows how global networks of scientists and sponsors mobilize around different ways of doing science across national borders (Collins et al., 2003; Sá and Tamtik, 2011). Examining the strategies that are employed to make these major undertakings viable from an institutional logics perspective would render interesting conceptual and empirical insights. Finally, the mid-2010s politics of neo-nationalism or nativism, expressed in major events such as Brexit in the UK and the election of President Trump in the US, provide fertile ground for the study of the interplay between the politics of nationalism and policy-making for academic science. The rise of xenophobic right-wing populism may be setting a social and political context that challenges relatively stable ideas present in contemporary science policy discourse and introduces new agendas and policy issues. In particular, future research might explore whether such politics will reconstitute or reinforce notions of scientific nationalism, and in so doing cast a shadow over the logic of scientific globalism.
