Sumptuary Labor: How Liberal Market Economies Regulate Consumption

Abstract

Liberal market states promote the responsible consumption of potentially dangerous commodities. But the work of enforcing sumptuary law is in fact delegated to service employees in the private sector. In this article such work is termed sumptuary labor. Although the ability of states to privatize sumptuary enforcement is a remarkable accomplishment, it is by no means a seamless one. Drawing on ethnographic fieldwork among bartenders and casino dealers, the article elaborates patterned conflicts of interest that arise during the performance of sumptuary labor. Both firms and workers may fail to ensure eligibility for consumption, prevent overindulgence, or guarantee the purity of the problematic commodity. The article concludes by theorizing how liberal market states manage the potential decoupling of sumptuary policy and practice.

Keywords

governance, consumption, labor, liberalism, markets

In his book Governance of the Consuming Passions, Alan Hunt describes an English statute from 1533, issued during the reign of Henry VIII, specifying that

[N]o person or persons of [any] estate, dignity, degree, or condition . . . shall wear in any manner of their apparel or upon their horse, mule, or any other beast any silk of the colour of purple, nor any cloth of gold tissue, but only the King, the Queen, the King’s mother, the King’s children, the King’s brothers and sisters, and the King’s uncles and aunts.1

Today no citizen of Britain risks arrest should she wear vestments sewn of cloth dear to Her Majesty the Queen. Yet on walking into a cinema to view a film, she will encounter a statute as byzantine as any produced in medieval Europe. The British Board of Film Classification (BBFC) slots all films into one of seven categories—U, PG, 12A, 12, 15, 18, and R18—in order to “protect children and vulnerable adults” from inappropriate media.2 So should our moviegoer desire to see the 2015 film The Revenant, she will find that, because of the film’s “strong violence, bloody injury detail, and strong language,” she must first present a state-issued identification document establishing that she is at least fifteen years old.3

The two statutes described above, though separated by half a millennium, are both instantiations of sumptuary law, that is, of state regulation of private consumption. It is commonly assumed that sumptuary laws are going extinct. Unlike medieval aristocracies or paternalistic welfare states, liberal market societies have no business dictating what we eat, drink, wear, or watch. Yet the empirical example of how intensely Britain—a prototypical liberal market economy—regulates film viewing suggests otherwise. We argue that rather than having gone extinct, sumptuary laws have evolved, and in two directions. First, toward a new goal of regulation. States today are less concerned with preserving long-standing status hierarchies than with ensuring the responsible consumption of potentially dangerous goods. Second, the means of enforcing sumptuary law has transformed. Whereas during the medieval era “sumptuary regulation was . . . a typical top-down form of government,” increasingly it is delegated to the private sector.4

At the confluence of these two trends is what we call sumptuary labor. By this term we refer to any element of a private-sector labor process that involves regulating consumption in line with the liberal goal of responsible indulgence. The cashier in the cinema above, when she checks a potential film viewer’s age on her identification card, is a private-sector employee performing sumptuary labor. So too is any worker whose job involves vending alcohol, tobacco, lottery tickets, lap dances, firearms, fireworks, body art, fatty foods, pornography, violent video games, cold medication, marijuana (where it is legal), consumer credit, or any other commodity deemed potentially harmful or dangerous.

The ability of liberal market states to delegate sumptuary regulation to privatesector workers is a remarkable achievement. But our thesis is that it is not a seamless one. Such delegation generates patterned conflicts of interest and hence a potential decoupling of policy and practice. Our elaboration of this thesis proceeds as follows. After placing sumptuary law in historical context, we enumerate three essential components of contemporary sumptuary labor: establishing a patron’s eligibility for consumption, guarding against overindulgence, and guaranteeing the integrity of the commodity. Next we explain why, in practice, each of these three duties may be neglected because of a double delegation of enforcement, from states to firms to frontline employees. Finally, we postulate three mechanisms for recoupling sumptuary policy and practice—panoptic policing, mock professionalism, and mock regulation— and consider variation in the use of such mechanisms across and within sectors. The analysis throughout is grounded in empirical studies of service work, including our own ethnographies of bartending and casino dealing.

Sumptuary Law in Context

A vibrant literature exists on sumptuary law in feudal society. However, research on such laws in market economies is scant. In this section we recount the origins of sumptuary law as a tactic by which sovereigns sought to affirm status distinctions. Though such laws proliferated on paper, feudal states lacked a regulatory apparatus to enforce them systematically. The rise of market societies in turn witnessed not a demise of sumptuary law but a radical change in its aims and methods.

Origins of Sumptuary Governance

Scholars trace the origins of sumptuary governance back to feudal Europe’s society of orders.5 The express purpose of these laws derived from the overarching logic by which feudal society was organized. In brief, authority was centralized with and embodied in the person of the sovereign, whose legitimacy to rule was established through reference to traditional prerogatives such as divine right and noble descent. The sovereign ruled his territory as though it were his household and he its patriarch, leading Bloch to depict such a system as “manorial” and Bourdieu as one rooted in a reason of the “king’s house.”6

Given how authority to rule was legitimated in feudal society, statutes of all sort were designed to reinforce traditional status boundaries between the nobility and commoners. Penal law, for instance, demanded that crimes against the sovereign, his representatives, or his property be punished more severely than standard criminal acts.7 Sumptuary laws were in turn designed to make visible and affirm categorical differences in status. It was thus dress and other forms of personal adornment—that is, the most mundane and visible manners of displaying status—that came to be the default target of early sumptuary laws. Medieval society generated endless and elaborate codes prescribing in detail the fabrics, colors, materials, and insignia appropriate for various status groups and especially the nobility (i.e., the sovereign and those who could claim blood relation to him or her).8

Feudal regimes, though perpetually preoccupied with preserving the status order, lacked the means for doing so effectively. Like status itself, authority to enforce sumptuary (and other) laws was centralized in the hands of the crown and its representatives. Yet all historical evidence suggests that enforcement was unsystematic in that punishments, though intense, were sporadic.9 The best explanation for this pattern is that sovereign power lacked a regulatory apparatus with which to comprehensively monitor private consumption. This view accords with Hunt’s assessment of medieval Europe: although sumptuary laws proliferated on paper, there is an “absence of evidence of systematic enforcement” and we must thus conclude that they “failed to penetrate significantly into the everyday world.”10

Goals of Sumptuary Governance in Market Economies

It is reasonable to hypothesize that sumptuary laws did not survive the demise of feudalism. It is especially hard to see what purpose they would serve in liberal market economies, that is, in societies wherein free markets are entrusted to coordinate production and consumption.11 Indeed, a central tenet of liberal thought is that, so long as no harm is done to others, the right of the individual to make his or her own decisions—regarding consumption, among other activities—is sacrosanct.12 Correspondingly, liberal states should allow commodities to be sold and bought without external intervention.13

However this hypothesis would be incorrect. The demise of a feudal society of orders did not eliminate but rather altered the nature of sumptuary governance. Liberal states today do regulate consumption, often quite extensively.

Consider the express goal of sumptuary laws in liberal market economies. It is true that consumption is no longer regulated so as to reinforce traditional status distinctions. In fact, most contemporary states prohibit discrimination in the provision of goods based on ascriptive characteristics of consumers such as their race, gender, or religion.14 The goal of status preservation has been replaced by one of responsible consumption. This new logic of governance divides the world of commodities into the harmless or normal (think toothpaste or paper towels) and the dangerous or pathological (such as alcohol or violent media).

Some of these latter, problematic goods are banned entirely or circumscribed for particular consumers. Examples would include preventing the consumption by anyone of child pornography, or the banning of internet access for convicted sex-offenders. In such cases, consumption is unambiguously criminal and responsibility for regulating it resides with law enforcement. Other problematic goods are legal but can be distributed only by established professionals, such as doctors, psychiatrists, and pharmacists. Examples here would include pain medications such as prescription opioids or medical cannabis. It is generally accepted that the professions should hold a monopoly on the legitimate administration of such goods, insofar as they have their own codes of conduct that serve to discipline practitioners.

The majority of problematic goods today, however, are regulated by neither the police nor professionals, but by front-line employees in the private sector. The work that they do to ensure responsible consumption has heretofore been overlooked. But it is essential to the functioning of market societies, in three ways.

First, it enacts a key liberal assumption concerning human subjectivity: consumers are homo oeconomicus, rational decision makers with given preferences who will weigh the costs and risks associated with a commodity against its anticipated utility.15 This assumption falters, however, if the consumer is for any reason incapable of making a reasoned and thus responsible decision about her behavior. Children, for instance, are today prohibited from consuming just about all potentially harmful commodities.16 In fact, age has become the most common (though by no means only) basis for categorizing consumers in market economies: only adults are taken to be autonomous subjects capable of making reasoned decisions.

Second, liberal assumptions regarding consumption falter when a personal choice to indulge in a commodity does cause harm to others.17 Market societies in general have trouble dealing with externalities and as a result are highly litigious.18 This certainly holds for markets of commodities deemed problematic by sumptuary law. For example, even a cursory examination reveals a plethora of rules, regulations, and standards regarding the use of alcohol, many of which are designed in anticipation of future disputes within the judicial system.19 An intoxicated driver has caused an accident—who is ultimately responsible? The driver herself, the genetic makeup that predisposed her to alcoholism, or the bartender who served her six drinks the hour before? Even alcohol manufacturers, in response to lawsuits and criticism, have developed an internal policy whereby retailers must ensure “the responsible consumption of our products” and drinkers are urged to “consume responsibly.”20

And third, even the most liberal of states must regulate some aspects of personal behavior. At a minimum, it is in the interest of such states to ensure that enough of the citizenry is alive and healthy enough to engage in productive labor. Market states thus routinely collect data on their populations and propose interventions to increase health, longevity, and productivity.21 Among these are attempts to reduce irresponsible consumption—that is, consumption that, in the aggregate, threatens public health or economic growth. An example is recent restrictions on cigarette smoking across the United States.22 Once the science linking smoking to various health ailments and premature deaths was unequivocally established (and after years of dissembling by big tobacco firms), government agencies at the local, state, and federal levels began to place restrictions on cigarette consumption.23 The emergent norm is that smoking is responsible and thus legal only if the smoker is at least eighteen years of age, is fully aware of the risks associated with smoking, and is smoking away from nonsmokers.24

Intensifying and Privatizing Sumptuary Enforcement

Having established that sumptuary laws survive even in liberal market societies, we now consider how liberal states seek to ensure responsible consumption. In brief, we argue that there has occurred a simultaneous intensification and privatization of enforcement.

Under feudalism, sumptuary laws were enforced sporadically insofar as sovereign rulers lacked an administrative apparatus to accomplish systematic enforcement. This description could not be further from how sumptuary laws are enforced in liberal market societies, insofar as states are directly involved with nearly all aspects of trade in problematic commodities. Via legislation, judicial opinion, or executive decree, states decide whether a given commodity can legally be traded in the first place. They establish the criteria by which rights to vend a problematic commodity are allocated (as when the US Supreme Court ruled in 1987 that Native Americans could operate casinos on their lands).25 States inspect and certify the authenticity and potency of problematic commodities, they adjudicate disputes that arise over the irresponsible consumption of these commodities, and they establish penalties for irresponsible consumption. In short, states directly regulate market actors at various points throughout the commodity chain.

There is one important exception: the routine work of policing the sale and consumption of problematic goods. Countless thousands of times each day, consumers are monitored to make sure that they are meeting the standards of responsible consumption. But it is rarely agents of the state who do such work. The power—indeed the duty and responsibility—to monitor consumption is devolved to the private sector.26 For instance, across market societies state-run “bottle stores” are disappearing as responsibility for ensuring the responsible consumption of alcohol is assigned to private establishments, ranging from mom-and-pop groceries to franchised restaurants to huge sports stadia.27 It is the proprietors of such establishments who design and implement procedures for ensuring responsible consumption. Tellingly, state officials who today do police problematic goods do so less by monitoring consumers directly than by auditing the policies and practices of firms that vend these goods.28

In summary, sumptuary law in liberal market societies differs in two important respects from its ancestors in feudal Europe’s society of orders. Its underlying goal is now the promotion of responsible consumption, and its enforcement has been delegated to private-sector firms. In the following section we describe what is in fact a second delegation, with firms requiring their front-line employees to do the work of monitoring consumption. We specifically consider three key components of the sumptuary labor that these workers are required to do.

Three Facets of Sumptuary Labor

In liberal market economies, front-line service workers are de facto deputies of sumptuary law. For them, the larger mandate to ensure responsible consumption translates into three routine job requirements: ensuring eligibility for consumption of problematic commodities, monitoring against overindulgence, and ensuring the integrity of dangerous commodities. How firms design work processes and socialize workers to follow these procedures matters a great deal for how sumptuary laws are enforced.

The majority of our data elucidating these three facets of sumptuary labor describe the work of bartenders and casino dealers—two occupations in which we have performed participant observation. To be clear, we do not claim that these occupations are representative of all service jobs. Service labor processes vary in the degree to which they require the performance of sumptuary labor—in some cases a little, in some a good deal, and in others a lot. What makes these two occupations illustrative is that sumptuary labor constitutes the predominant component of the overall labor process. They are extreme cases, but as such can depict in sharp relief the nature of sumptuary labor. As we argue below, an increasing number of workers are being asked to spend an increasing amount of time doing sumptuary labor, and so in this sense the work of bartenders and dealers is a harbinger of the service labor process of the future. Nevertheless, we take great care to include throughout our analysis examples drawn from empirical studies of other service occupations.

Ensuring Eligibility for Consumption

Even in liberal market societies, trade in problematic goods such as alcohol, cigarettes, and violent films is restricted. They can be consumed only by those who have processed all available information concerning their risks and then made an informed and responsible decision. By far the most common proxy used to signify such competency is age. Only once one has crossed the threshold demarcating childhood from adulthood, commonly set at ages sixteen, eighteen, or twenty-one, is one allowed to consume problematic goods. Furthermore, we find today only a single valid way to prove one’s age: a state-issued identity document (ID) such as a passport or driver’s license.29 Workers in private establishments that sell problematic goods must thus become experts in scrutinizing IDs. They are trained to tell fake or borrowed IDs from real and valid ones, to differentiate minors from adults in a group of customers, and to deny service to ineligible patrons.

Such skills are a standard component of the curriculum in bartender training programs.30 Aspiring bartenders are schooled in various procedures for determining a potential patron’s age, and thus his or her competency to imbibe spirits responsibly. For instance, trainees are presented with twenty-one common versions of fake IDs and taught specific techniques for discerning them. As one instructor directed, bartenders should scrutinize as many details of the ID as possible, including its condition and expiration date:

Some students tell me, “I feel awkward carding people.” What? That is your job! Don’t just see the ID flashing from the wallet. Hold it. Feel it. The texture should be bendy and pliable. Look carefully. Look at the expiration date. If it’s expired, ask for a second photo ID.

If still in doubt, bartenders should pose potentially tricky questions to verify the ID’s authenticity. For instance, they can ask the patron his or her astrological sign to crosscheck the date of birth.

Bartending trainees are also taught to observe customers directly. “See how they react to your questions,” an instructor and veteran bartender told her students, “if they are touching their hair, looking around and are like ‘oh my god,’ you know they are lying.” It is also important to identify those who are flanking their friends as they walk into the bar as well as those who may be buying drinks for others: “If it’s a group of people, and one looks old enough, card them all. If I take three orders, I need to see three IDs, because adults buy drinks for minors. The minors might be sitting at the back of the bar. Keep an eye on what’s happening back there.”

Across alcohol-serving establishments, policy is that those who fail eligibility checks should be denied service in a polite yet assertive manner.

Age may be the main criterion for determining eligibility for consumption of problematic goods, but it is not the only one. Like minors, those who have a mental health disorder are often considered incapable of making responsible decisions. For example, it is possible for someone to be diagnosed as a pathological gambler, meaning that their gambling is heavy, compulsive, and out of control.31 Such persons can be either voluntarily or involuntarily placed on a roster of those who are banned from entering casinos in a given city or region. Increasingly, it is common practice throughout the industry for casinos to post photos of those individuals in the “back of house” and require dealers (along with security guards and other personnel) to recognize and deny service to those potential players.32 Dealer training programs in turn feature modules on how to identify these individuals even if they are wearing disguises such as wigs or fake moustaches.

Analogous to a mental health disorder, a criminal background can signify that one cannot responsibly consume certain goods, such as firearms.33 The same holds for a history of bankruptcy or delinquent payments on one’s debt when one seeks consumer credit.34 It is also the case that the consumption of certain problematic goods may be reserved for those with special conditions, most often medical. Purchases of pseudoephedrine increasingly require the presentation of a photo ID, and medical marijuana a state-issued eligibility card.35 Like the bartender checking IDs, the clerk in the drugstore or cannabis dispensary must guard against forgeries and ensure that the individual in front of her is who the ID card claims he is.

Preventing Overindulgence

Sumptuary laws specify who is eligible to consume problematic goods: the competent adult. But they also imply how such goods are to be consumed: by individuals who are in control of their actions and thus consuming responsibly and in moderation. A second facet of sumptuary labor is thus monitoring a patron’s behavior, determining the limits of her consumption, and enforcing these limits. The lingo of the bartending profession captures perfectly this facet: when is it time for a patron to be “cut off”?

Preventing overindulgence aligns with the larger goal of promoting responsible consumption in three ways. First, it is a logical extension of the eligibility issue discussed above: after a certain amount of consumption, an individual may enter an altered state of consciousness which no longer permits responsible decision making. Even those who hold a medical marijuana card are limited in the amount of cannabis they can purchase daily, and tattoo parlors post signs stating that they will not ink patrons who are drunk or high.36 A second alignment is that the state may consider the curtailment of consumption to be directly linked to certain public health goals. As awareness grew of the dangers posed by secondhand smoke, for instance, establishments were required to post and enforce bans on smoking inside the premises. And third, not preventing overindulgence may expose providers to liability. Bar owners are subject to tavern keeper laws, which hold them responsible for the behavior of patrons even after they leave the establishment;37 pathological gamblers have sued casinos for allowing them to gamble for too long.38

A key difference between ensuring eligibility for consumption and guarding against overindulgence is the absence of universal standards for doing the latter. Procedures for checking state-issued IDs are uniform across industries and jurisdictions. In contrast, criteria for recognizing and procedures for handling overindulgence are more idiosyncratic.

Bartenders, for instance, are taught to recognize various degrees of inebriation among patrons. Depending on how they comport themselves, drinkers are classified into three categories which the occupational lingo captures using traffic light metaphors. Someone who has just entered the bar and who appears to be sober in their speech and bodily movements is given a “green light;” if a customer has consumed several drinks or appears “tipsy,” they receive a “yellow light,” meaning that the bartender can continue service but should keep an eye on her; a “red light” is in turn assigned to patrons exhibiting slurred speech, emotional outbursts, or hostile behavior. Bartenders should immediately cut off red-lighted patrons. As Jim, an instructor, explained: “If a customer can’t speak, can’t walk, has gone from being everyone’s best friend to picking fights with people, it is time to cut them off.”

Similar procedures are in place throughout the casino industry. Patrons who have suffered heavy losses and who as a result seem agitated should be encouraged to take a break from gambling. Rather than forcibly cut them off, however, casino employees are taught to essentially “cool them out.”39 They should discretely call over a pit boss to the table and make a comment such as “our friend at third base [the seat immediately to the dealer’s right] is having some tough luck tonight.” Theoretically, the pit boss would then strike up a friendly banter with the player and offer him some sort of “comp” such as a meal in one of the casino’s restaurant. The point of such procedures is to nudge an excessive gambler away from the tables.

Ensuring Integrity of Commodities

Responsible consumption of potentially dangerous commodities is only possible if the consumer knows precisely the nature of the commodity and can make reasoned decisions accordingly. In some cases, that certification process is done in advance and from afar, as when the BBFC establishes an age-appropriate rating for a film, a lottery agency prints the odds of winning on the back of a ticket, or a brewery stamps the alcohol-by-volume percentage on its bottles. In other cases, however, problematic goods are produced and consumed simultaneously, such that delivery and quality control are fused. In those cases, establishing the product’s quality or potency is part and parcel of the service labor process. Exotic dancers, for instance, must be aware of precisely which parts of their body they are allowed to reveal and how closely they are permitted to come to clients during performances; gas station clerks must be able to verify that a winning scratch-off lotto ticket has not been doctored in any way.

Our ethnographies of bartending and dealing reveal that ensuring the integrity of the product is an essential component of sumptuary labor. Aspirant bartenders are drilled daily on their pouring skills and required to memorize an entire dictionary of drink recipes. The rationale is that a consumer can make a responsible decision only if the bartender includes all the alcoholic and nonalcoholic ingredients each in their right proportion and amount. Students must thus develop a natural “feel” for all the common measuring counts ranging incrementally from 0.25 ounce to 2.5 ounces. As Jim, the lead trainer at a bartending school, explained:

We control liquor. Liquor is the backbone of our business. To make sure that your pours are precise, “pour checks” will be used to test your performance. If less than ten out of fourteen are correct, you are going home. Accuracy is important.

Furthermore, because spirits vary in terms of quality and potency, bartenders must make drinks precisely according to customers’ requests. If a customer requests a premium vodka such as Grey Goose in his drink, the bartender cannot “marry it” with cheap vodka from “the well.”

The labor process of casino dealers is also organized so as to standardize a problematic commodity. Here what matters is not the product’s potency as much as its integrity, that is, the randomness of a given game of chance. Just as a patron can responsibly consume a double Grey Goose martini only if the bartender has delivered it to her with two ounces of Grey Goose vodka, a gambler at a blackjack table can make a reasoned decision about her betting only if the cards are randomly mixed and the game is not in any way compromised. Dealer training schools thus dedicate a full week to instructing students on the proper technique for shuffling a deck of cards. Dealers learn to, first, remove a third of the deck from the bottom and place it on top. Then, the dealer splits the deck exactly in half and gently fans each pile together, such that cards from each pile alternate in their mixing. This fanning process must be accomplished without bending the cards, as a bent card is considered “marked” and the game compromised. Thus, to qualify as a dealer, one must be able to shuffle a deck noiselessly, as cards audible flapping indicates that they are being bent. Once the process has been completed three times, a deck of cards is considered to be fully randomized and a “square” game of chance can commence.

Drawing on various examples, and case studies of bartending and casino dealing in particular, we have shown in this section how sumptuary labor can be an extensive part of a service occupation. According to the broad logic of sumptuary governance, service workers should establish eligibility for consumption, guard against overindulgence, and ensure the integrity of the dangerous commodity. A bartender, for instance, should check each patron’s ID, make sure that his drink contains a precise amount of alcohol, and deny him further service should he become intoxicated. Only by doing so does the bartender ensure responsible consumption, and for this reason bartending schools teach such procedures as part of their core curriculum. The question arises, however, whether service workers such as bartenders always dutifully perform these three facets of sumptuary labor. Might the actual performance of these work-tasks diverge from how they are formally designed? If so, why?

Sumptuary Law in Practice: Doubly Delegated

That liberal market states are able to enlist private sector workers to monitor an untold number of sumptuary transactions every day is an astonishing accomplishment. But it would be naïve to assume that it is a seamless one. Much empirical evidence, including our own ethnographic studies, establishes that sumptuary labor is not always performed to the letter of sumptuary law. IDs go unchecked, problem gamblers are dealt to, and some drinks are poured stiffer than others. We argue that such decoupling is not idiosyncratic, but rather derives from conflicts of interest inherent to the liberal regulation of consumption. In this section we elaborate how the double delegation of sumptuary enforcement can lead to a decoupling of policy and practice.

Liberal market states promote responsible consumption, yet delegate responsibility for enforcing sumptuary law to private firms, who in turn delegate it to their employees. Figure 1 depicts this process as a chain in which sumptuary labor is passed down from the state ultimately to front-line service workers. It also allows us to conceptualize the intermediaries in this chain as institutional actors with their own interests. On one hand, firms operate in competitive markets the dominant logic of which is revenue-maximization rather than the restriction of consumption.40 On the other hand, workers have their own interests separate from those of both their employers and the state.41 To the extent that firms and workers act strategically, all three facets of sumptuary labor may potentially be compromised.

Firms Contra State

Sumptuary regulations, which by their nature restrict consumption, should conflict with an individual firm’s interest in increasing revenues so as to secure and maximize profits.42 Incentives to disregard sumptuary law should be particularly salient in service industries, where most sumptuary labor is performed, because of the competitive nature of this sector. Entry costs are low, such that the average firm is a small “mom-and-pop” establishment.43 Sustained profitability is elusive, given that consumer tastes are fickle and interproducer competition on price the norm.44 Given these factors, rates of business failure in service industries are very high. In short, there are structural reasons to expect that service firms will have short time horizons, and a good deal of research suggests that they do feel pressure to “noncomply” with the dictates of sumptuary law.45

This misalignment of interests should influence how seriously firms take the mandate to ensure eligibility for consumption of problematic goods. Requiring employees to check clients’ IDs prior to vending goods introduces slight but not insignificant costs to each transaction (namely, the time it takes the employee to do the ID check plus the firm’s initial investment in training the worker in comprehensive ID checking procedures). There is also the possibility of false exclusions and thus sales foregone. The video game store that refuses to sell a violent game to a thirty-year-old gamer who has forgotten his ID risks losing the sale. And, by fastidiously enforcing ID requirements, firms miss an opportunity to generate a comparative advantage. Studies have shown that college and even high school students can readily report which bars and convenience stores in their areas are lax about checking patrons’ IDs.46

The firm-state conflict of interest should also moderate firms’ efforts to prevent overindulgence. Many goods and services are designated problematic precisely because they are addictive. But what official discourse would label an “addict” many service industries consider their “bread and butter” insofar as regular users of problematic goods generate the majority of revenues.47 In the casino industry, for instance, “two-thirds to 80% of gambling revenues come from the 10% of the population that gambles most heavily.”48 As established above, practitioners in psychiatry and public health argue that heavy gamblers should be screened for evidence of a gambling pathology and, if so diagnosed, be excluded from gambling establishments. In reality, however, casino firms go to great lengths to identify and lure in just such heavy gamblers. At one large corporate-owned casino, individual gamblers’ wagering patterns were covertly tracked and each was assigned an EP (estimated play) score—basically the average amount of money they lost per visit. The higher a gambler’s EP, the more advertisements, coupons, and free drink coupons were mailed to him or her.49

Service firms, perpetually concerned with staying in the black, should also have an interest in contravening state standards regarding the purity of problematic goods. For instance, it has been documented that casinos will require experienced dealers to manipulate game outcomes in high-stake situations;50 hostess bars will falsely imply that sexual acts are performed within;51 taverns will marry bottles and even “mix rubbing alcohol with caramel food coloring and serve it as scotch.”52 Such actions prevent losses, increase revenues, and cut costs. Yet they also deprive consumers of information necessary for responsible consumption.

Workers Contra State

Workers have their own interests—distinct from those of both the state and the firms they work for—that may compromise their performance of sumptuary labor. A broad range of scholarship establishes that, on the whole, workers value jobs that provide a living wage, dignified working conditions, and employment security.53 But service workers are typically drawn from the low end of the labor market, and so lack human capital (such as higher education) and bargaining power (such as union representation). In liberal market economies, neither the state nor firms guarantee such workers material security nor more than a modicum of protection from inhumane treatment.54 The results are not surprising: low levels of commitment to one’s employer, high levels of job dissatisfaction, and frequent job turnover.55 Given these conditions, we can postulate why service workers may be poor enforcers of all three aspects of sumptuary law.

Both our own research and existing empirical studies reveal that service workers often find it all too tempting to contravene requirements to ensure eligibility for consumption. Restaurants and bars, for instance, routinely attempt to reduce labor costs by understaffing and demanding that employees be flexible about their work hours.56 As a result, servers and bartenders feel continuously “slammed,” that is, harried and expected to do more than is possible.57 In such circumstances, one can save time by checking only cursorily the IDs of those who request drinks—or not checking them at all. Furthermore, it has been documented that service workers are often drawn from the same local social milieus as customers.58 As friends, family members, neighbors, or members of a common subculture, clients may pressure front-line service employees to forgo standard procedures for checking identification documents. An ethnographic study of night clubs, for instance, found that underage patrons mobilized social capital—such as personal connections with particular bouncers—to get in to the clubs.59 In cases such as these it is clear that employees are putting their own interests above those of the government’s sumptuary regulations

By so doing, employees may also neglect to guard against overindulgence. To pronounce publicly that a customer is no longer capable of making a responsible decision concerning his or her consumption is to infantilize him or her. It is by its very nature a power move within a face-to-face encounter. But service workers are otherwise expected to adopt a deferential demeanor in their interactions with clients (by addressing them as sir or ma’am, for instance).60 In addition, they are likely to be of a lower social standing than those whom they serve (the modal interaction on a Las Vegas gambling table, for instance, is that of a female immigrant dealer talking to an older white male).61 Under such conditions it can be extremely uncomfortable to deny a patron service. This was certainly the case among bartenders, who reported that mustering the fortitude to cut off inebriated drinkers was one of the most difficult tricks of the trade to master. As Serena, a new bartender, lamented: “It’s what makes me feel most awkward. It’s the hardest thing for me as a bartender. I hate shutting people off. Customers will follow up with the question ‘why?’ and then what do I say?” Cutting off an inebriated patron entails shifting one’s demeanor from informal and friendly to assertive and confrontational, making it all too tempting simply to pour the drunk another drink.

Finally, employees may perceive it to be in their interests to alter the purity of the good. Consider the situation of service workers who earn tips. In many jurisdictions, firms pay tipped employees less than half the minimum wage, such that the majority of one’s take-home income comes from clients rather than one’s employer. Not surprisingly, empirical studies have repeatedly shown that service workers generate myriad strategies for procuring gratuities, a standby of which is rewarding tippers by violating procedures for standardizing the product.62 Exotic dancers, for instance, have been documented to reward generous tippers with illicit forms of sexual contact.63 Bartenders often have leeway to pour stronger drinks for customers who tip them well. And in the casino business, gamblers who follow up big wins by tipping their dealer are referred to as “Georges.” Dealers try to help Georges win by, for instance, offering them advice on how to play their hands or reshuffling the cards to improve their odds.64 In all these empirical examples, the employee is acting sensibly. To increase her earnings, she violates procedures for enforcing sumptuary law.65

Recoupling Policy and Practice: Variations on a Theme

Drawing on extensive empirical material, we have demonstrated that service workers in liberal market economies have an interest in not fastidiously enforcing sumptuary laws. Our findings should not, however, be interpreted as evidence of a Hobbesian world of widespread unlawful behavior. More often than not, private sector employees such as bartenders and casino dealers do check IDs, pour proper shots, and deal square hands. This suggests that liberal market societies are, on the whole, able successfully to counter the decoupling tendencies inherent in the double delegation of sumptuary enforcement. How, we ask in this section, are incentives and norms arranged so as to bring practice into line with sumptuary law? Three general recoupling mechanisms can be identified: panoptic policing, mock professionalism, and mock regulation. As we elaborate these mechanisms, we also specify those sectors in which they are most likely to be successfully deployed.

One mechanism by which liberal market states can manage the conflicts of interest inherent to the double delegation of sumptuary enforcement is through panoptic policing. Here, relevant state agencies make a sincere effort to enforce sumptuary laws by directly policing transactions in private sector establishments. The most common such policing mechanism is to send undercover “decoys” into service establishments and have them attempt to purchase problematic goods or services without proper identification.66 Theoretically, such random audits, coupled with severe penalties for noncompliance (such as revocation of the establishment’s liquor license) should create a panopticon effect, whereby firms and their employees self-discipline out of fear that any customer could be a police decoy.67 Existing research suggests that panoptic policing practices, when fully funded and properly executed, do reduce incidences of noncompliance with sumptuary laws, such as sales to underage patrons.68

Panoptic policing is more common in some sectors than in others. We expect such variation to reflect the degree to which consumption of the commodity in question is considered dangerous to others beyond the user herself. Alcohol and tobacco would seem to be clear cases, definitively linked as they are to externalities such as drunk driving fatalities and health ailments for nonsmokers via secondhand smoke. Indeed, we found that most bartenders are careful about serving minor-looking and intoxicated patrons, because of widely discussed penalties (such as immediate job termination) for servers who neglect to card a state decoy. In contrast, ethnographic work among casino dealers did not reveal a single instance of an undercover police agent’s posing as a gambler, nor did dealers report worrying about such decoys. The likely reason is that the negative externalities of underage or heavy gambling are largely confined to the consumer himself.

A second potential mechanism for resolving sumptuary conflicts is what we call mock professionalism. Here, the state requires that workers who vend problematic goods or services undergo specialized training, certification, and licensing—much like practitioners in professional fields such as medicine, law, and science. Service workers who perform sumptuary labor, however, are not true professionals. They are not embedded within fields of generalizable expertise controlled by self-policing bodies.69 They do not earn high salaries, are not members of professional associations, do not subscribe to specialist journals, and so on. By putting into place many of the entry procedures found in the true professions, however, authorities attempt to foster in workers a professional ethos that internally motivates them to take seriously their responsibly to ensure responsible consumption, even in the absence of state supervision.

Unlike panoptic policing, mock professionalism potentially recouples without the expense of direct monitoring on the part of the state itself. It is a quintessentially normative form of control. And if anything, its allure as a mechanism of enforcement seems to be growing. The proportion of service workers who are required to achieve some such certification has been rising for decades and now stands at nearly a third of the workforce.70

In our assessment, however, mock professionalism stands a chance of success only in sectors wherein the provision of problematic goods (a) is the essence of the occupation (as in bartending), (b) retains an aura of craft (such as casino dealing), or (c) requires some semblance of medical expertise (as with cashiers in medical marijuana dispensaries). Furthermore, the overall efficacy of mock professionalism remains questionable, given the ongoing lack of meaningful incentives (such as good salaries, social status, and job security) for front-line private-sector service workers. A certificate from a dealing academy is simply not a JD from a top-tier law school.

We propose one further strategy by which states can manage the systematic decoupling of sumptuary law and its enforcement via sumptuary labor. All parties in the production chain can agree, tacitly or otherwise, to tolerate decoupling—we label this mock regulation. In this scenario, states and firms both make symbolic but ultimately perfunctory attempts to regulate problematic consumption. Signs are always posted at theater entrances specifying the appropriate age ranges for films, for instance, but after purchasing a ticket and entering the cinema complex, as a recent FDA study established, underage moviegoers are easily able to enter cinemas showing films earmarked for adults.71 Theater owners do not systematically monitor patron movement past the point of entry, and cinema workers do not much worry about undercover state shoppers or about violating a professional code of ethics.

Why would consumption be regulated so perfunctorily in a given industry? The state, along with various publics and interest groups, may not consider regulation of the problematic commodity to be of high priority relative to others. (A binge film viewer is objectively less likely to cause an automobile accident than is a binge drinker.) State interests may also be aligned with firms in promoting indulgence and even overindulgence. For instance, since the late 1970s US state governments have increasingly relied on revenues from casinos and lotteries to fund public services. Within policy circles, gambling is referred to as a form of “painless” or “voluntary” taxation.72 The regulation of alcohol appears to be moving in this same direction. 73 Given that liberal market states experience recurring budget crises, there is a temptation to collect “voluntary” taxes from users of problematic commodities, while making only symbolic gestures toward regulation and responsible consumption.

Conclusion

We commenced from the thesis that in liberal market societies the governance of problematic commodities—sumptuary law—is beset by a fundamental tension and thus a potential decoupling. Market economies rely on the ideal of a rational consumer, whereas in reality consumers regularly make irresponsible and potentially dangerous decisions. The work of bringing practice into line with policy, however, is twice delegated. First, to private firms that, by the logic of the market, should seek to maximize rather than restrict sales and, second, to front-line service workers for whom the enforcement of sumptuary law often entails costs in terms of income and dignity. Although the resulting conflicts of interest do not result in total anarchy, we presented enough empirical data to establish that the extent of decoupling is not insignificant. In particular, the occupations of bartending and casino dealing illustrated both the crosscutting pressures that sumptuary labor entails and the mechanisms by which states manage the tensions inherent to sumptuary law in a liberal market context. We conclude with three warrants for further study of sumptuary labor.

The first is that, assuming it is in our collective best interest to prevent “irresponsible” consumption of dangerous commodities, it behooves us to know how sumptuary laws are enforced—or not—in situ. Are minors easily able to buy violent video games? How fastidious are bartenders about checking IDs before vending liquor? Will a pathological gambler who is recklessly betting away his family’s grocery money be required to cease gambling? To answer such questions, it helps greatly to be familiar with basic insights from the sociology of work. Front-line service workers, for instance, often rely on tips for income, and so can reasonably be expected to violate sumptuary responsibilities when they conflict with strategies for earning gratuities. Bars and restaurants regularly understaff, putting pressure on servers to devise shortcuts, such as being lax about checking identification documents. We cannot understand modern forms of governance such as sumptuary regulation without some theory of the work process of those who enforce them.

The second warrant for further study of sumptuary labor is that it is increasingly standard duty for a large and growing number of workers. Above we laid to rest the assumption that market societies shy away from regulating private consumption; if anything, there is an explosion of sumptuary legislation in such societies. As problematic commodities proliferate and as states delegate more and more functions to the private sector, the number of workers who are required to enforce sumptuary laws as part of their routine duties should grow. The bartender has always vended drinks and the casino dealer a shot at a big win, but sumptuary labor is now performed by the gas station attendant who inspects and pays winning lottery tickets, the cashier who must verify that a teenager is old enough to purchase a violent video game, the barista whose coffee shop has decided to start serving wine, and many other private-sector workers. Nor should the growth of e-commerce arrest this trend, as the online sale of most problematic goods is either restricted (as with gambling) or impossible (as with drinking shots). In short, as the service sector extends its lead as the main source of employment in industrialized countries, more and more workers will be entrusted to serve as regulators, at least some of the time.

Our final warrant for further research is that there surely is variation in the enactment of sumptuary labor beyond that which was discussed in this article. Because we had primary data from two different industries—bars and casinos—we focused on explaining intersectoral variation in enforcement. The greater the perceived threat of a given commodity to public health, the more likely it is that the state will directly monitor firms and their employees (via panoptic policing) to ensure compliance. If an occupation retains an aura of craft or medical skill, states may be able to credentialize (mock-professionalize) it and hence induce normative compliance. And finally, industries that provide liberal market states with significant tax revenues should be susceptible to sustained decoupling (via mock regulation).

Our data did not allow us to address the question of intrasectoral variation in the enforcement of sumptuary law. But it would be surprising if such variation did not exist. Studies have shown that, within a given industry, establishments catering to high-income clients are more likely to allow workers to be lax in their enforcement of sumptuary laws.74 All else being equal, tipped employees should be more willing to break with official sumptuary policy than are nontipped employees.75 And union versus nonunion service workplaces should entail different norms and incentives about adhering to the letter of sumptuary law.76 This article has been an initial attempt to isolate sumptuary labor as an object of inquiry and to identify its essential components. The terrain for further exploration of this important phenomenon is very much wide open.
