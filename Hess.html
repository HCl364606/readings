Introduction: Fields of Knowledge and Theory Traditions in the Sociology of Science

David J. Hess, Vanderbilt University

Scott Frickel, Brown University

Citation: David J. Hess and Scott Frickel. 2014. Hess and Frickel. “Introduction: Fields of Knowledge and Theory Traditions in the Sociology of Science.” Political Power and Social Theory, 27: 1-30.

This is a draft version of the article.

Abstract. This Introduction gives a historical and theoretical overview of this volume on Fields of Knowledge: Science, Politics and Publics in the Neoliberal Age which showcases original research in political sociology of science targeting the changes in scientific and technological policy and practice associated with the rise of neoliberal thought and policies since the 1970s. We argue that an existing family of field theoretic frameworks and empirical field analyses provides a particularly useful set of ideas and approaches for the meso-level understanding of these historical changes in ways that complement as well as challenge other theory traditions in sociology of science, broadly defined. The collected papers exhibit a dual focus on sciences’ interfield relations connecting science and science policy to political, economic, educational and other fields and on the institutional logics of scientific fields that pattern expert discourses, practices, and knowledge and shapes relations of the scientific field to the rest of the world. By reconceptualizing the central problem for political sociology of science as a problem of field- and inter-field dynamics, and by critically engaging other theory traditions whose assumptions are in some ways undermined by the contemporary history of neoliberalism, we believe these papers collectively chart an important theoretical agenda for future research in the sociology of science.

The essays in this volume were first presented at a workshop that was held at the University of Wisconsin in June, 2012, and organized by Daniel Lee Kleinman. The workshop brought together a network of scholars who study the political sociology of science and technology, an approach to the sociology of science that draws systematic attention to power dynamics and structures of inequality within research fields and in the “extrafield” relations among science, the state, industry, social movements, and in civil society more generally. Recent research in this tradition has been influenced by the edited volume The New Political Sociology of Science (Frickel and Moore 2005) and by a conference on the political sociology of science held at Rensselaer Polytechnic Institute in 2011 (Hess 2011). The aim of the 2005 book was to sketch a broad framework for the sociological analysis of power and inequality as they are influenced by and impact science, technology and society. Toward that end, the volume was general in its topical focus, theoretical scope, and methodological preferences, with authors contributing chapters on a range of issues, concepts, and approaches. The 2011 conference at Rensselaer, designed primarily to take stock of newer research in the area, was similarly general in its attention to theory, scope, and method.

By contrast, the papers collected for the present issue of Political Power and Social Theory focus on a historically specific problem area: the changes in scientific and technological policy and practice associated with the rise of neoliberal thought and policies since the 1970s (e.g., Moore et al. 2011). Four decades on, ascendant neoliberal ideologies and the policies associated with those ideas continue to have major impacts on the practice, organization, and uses of scientific research. In turn, the neoliberalization of science is complicating interactions among states, industries, and civil society.

All of the papers presented in this volume take the historically changing configuration of fieldlevel relationships emergent under neoliberalism as their collective starting point. In doing so, these papers extend current work on neoliberalism and science in important new directions. To date, much of this work has focused on the neoliberalization of the university, with special emphasis on universityindustry relationships (e.g., Slaughter and Rhoades 2004). The research presented in the present volume significantly broadens this perspective, from the impacts of neoliberalism on universities (and universitybased science) to newly emergent institutional configurations involving multiple fields that nurture and constrain scientific production in new and often troubling ways.

Such changes often call into question assumptions of scientific autonomy and the entrepreneurial independence of scientists that underlie many of the most influential works in the sociology of science and scientific knowledge. The present volume engages with and builds on other theory traditions in the sociology of science, such as the focus on the reward system and the normative regulation of the scientific field in the functionalist tradition and on the social negotiation of knowledge claims among networks of scientists in the constructivist tradition. However, in maintaining a wider focus on the dynamic interaction of enduring structures of power and inequality in society with the agency of both scientists and nonscientists as they circulate within and across diverse social fields, authors of the current volume are drawn to and in some cases synthesize a range of meso-level frameworks from field theory, neo-institutional theory, and governmentality theory. By reconceptualizing the central problem for political sociology of science as a problem of field- and interfield dynamics, and by critically engaging other theory traditions whose assumptions are in some ways laid bare by the contemporary history of neoliberalism, we believe these papers collectively chart an important theoretical agenda for future research in the sociology of science.

Overview of the Articles

All of the articles in the volume take as a point of reference some version of field theory, including Bourdieusian, strategic action, and organizational field theories. Some of the researchers find field theory to be helpful for the problems that they address, whereas others have used it more as a foil for the articulation of alternative theoretical frameworks, especially frameworks that draw on neoinstitutional theory. The papers fall roughly into two main groups that have to do with ways in which one might conceptualize field theory: the problem of interfield relations and the problem of institutional logics.

Interfield Relations

In the first group of articles (by Albert and McGuire, Lave, Hess, and Suryanarayanan and Kleinman), the researchers focus on a problem in field theory that has been identified as an area that Bourdieu tended to underplay in his writings on science: the study of the relations among fields (Camic 2011). Although Bourdieu developed a theory of the relationship between the field of power and the intellectual field, there is general sentiment among scholars sympathetic to field theory that it needs to do more work to address issues of interfield relations, and this general issue in field theory becomes acute when one is concerned with the neoliberalization of the scientific field. In “Understanding Change in Academic Knowledge Production in a Neoliberal Era,” Mathieu Albert and Wendy McGuire work from within a Bourdieusian field theory framework to develop the concept of the autonomous-heteronomous polarity of the scientific field. They use the changing weights of the producer-oriented and user-oriented poles of the scientific field to modify the “Mode 2” thesis that scientific research has increasingly shifted toward mission-based research (Gibbons et al. 1994). In contrast, Albert and McGuire argue that “Mode 1” and “Mode 2” science operate simultaneously and that individual scientists negotiate their relationship with both (Gibbons et al. 1994). Tracking changes in the research funding priorities of the Canadian government in favor of agendas consistent with the country’s economic strategy, they draw on Bourdieu’s concept of the convertibility of capital to suggest the fluidity with which scientists may move between a user-oriented pole of research to a producer-oriented pole. Although many scientists in their sample adopted a strategy consistent with the traditional vision of science as production for producers, Albert and McGuire found significant generational differences in how scientists responded to the changes, with younger scientists more prone to employ a hybrid strategy. There were also differences between the vision or ideal of the scientists and their actual practices, and in some cases scientists grappled with a misalignment between the vision and practice.

Like Albert and McGuire, Lave also uses Bourdieusian field theory for her study of regulatory policy and science for stream restoration, and she builds on her book (Lave 2012), which also used the distinction between the heteronomous and autonomous poles of the scientific field. In the essay in this volume titled “Neoliberal Confluences: The Turbulent Evolution of Stream Mitigation Banking in the U.S.,” Lave develops an analysis of both interfield relations (the stream mitigation industry, regulators, Congress, scientists, and the administration) and intrafield relations within the regulatory field between mitigation bankers and regulators. Stream mitigation banking is a market-based practice that allows people who relocate or culvert a stream to make amends by restoring a stream elsewhere. The growth of stream mitigation banking as an industry has shaped the agendas of the research field, which developed a new science of ecosystem services. Thus, Lave argues that the market-oriented form of regulation created a new industry and science, but she also notes that the regulatory policy is a hybrid of market-oriented, neoliberal elements with older command-and-control approaches to environmental policy. Although not originally developed with a neoliberal intent, the valuation of ecosystem services facilitated the commodification of ecosystems that was needed to develop stream mitigation markets.

In “Beekeepers’ Collective Resistance and the Politics of Pesticide Regulation

in France and the United States,” Sainath Suryanarayanan and Daniel Lee Kleinman study the different regulatory outcomes to the problem of accelerated honey bee die-offs in France and the U.S. At one level, the study is a comparison between the more social liberal policy regime of the French government, in which the state is more insulated from industrial influence and more interventionist in economic matters, and the more neoliberal regime of the U.S., in which industrial influence on regulatory policy is generally stronger and government regulation of industry is often weaker. However, they reject simplistic formulations that would explain the difference as a result of general differences in political culture, and instead they approach the comparative question through the analysis of interfield relations that takes into account the relations among industrial firms, scientists, regulators, beekeepers, and farmers. They argue that the more interventionist regulatory response to the problem of pervasive honey bee deaths in France is due to a confluence of higher social movement mobilization, different epistemic cultures of honey bee biology, and different structures of government that enable the state to be more or less responsive to movement mobilization. The French beekeepers were more highly mobilized and unified than their American counterparts, but Suryanarayanan and Kleinman suggest that the effectiveness of the mobilization depended in part on differences in epistemic cultures for gauging honey bee health. In France there was greater willingness to accept the results of laboratory studies as a sound basis for a precautionary policy, whereas in the U.S. industry prevailed with the view that only controlled field studies produced definitive knowledge and that laboratory studies were inadequate because they did not reflect real-world conditions. In turn the structure of government interacted with the other two explanatory factors: the more centralized and hierarchical structure in France made it easier for authorities to respond quickly to popular mobilization, and the more fragmented structure in the U.S. government favored policy stasis.

In “When Green Became Blue: Epistemic Rift and the Corralling of Climate Science,” David Hess also addresses the problem of interfield relations by examining a case in which the standard circulation or “metabolism” of science and the state breaks down. In parallel with discussions of ecological rift in environmental sociology, Hess argues that the rise of climate denialism among elected political officials in the United States has led to an “epistemic rift” in which a relatively strong consensus among a research community is no longer accepted as the politically neutral epistemic doxa, that is, as the basis of “what is the case” upon which normative debate about “what should be done” can take place. The breakdown occurs because a threatened industry has funded a scientific “contrapublic” of scientists and media pundits who attack the consensus of the research field that contemporary climate change is fundamentally shaped by anthropogenic greenhouse gases. Thus, the scientific research community is “corralled” or cut off from its public education and public policy functions, political officials can openly pronounce anti-science views without losing legitimacy at the polls, and, once in office, the denialist politicians can also target climate science research for defunding. Under these circumstances, the scientific community becomes part of the counterpublic of a coalition of environmentalists, green businesses, and political progressives who advocate continued reform.

In summary, this group of four studies suggests some new ways to think about science from the perspective of interfield relations. As Albert and McGuire note, there is continued value in developing the heteronomous-autonomous pole concept from Bourdieu and for understanding how scientists negotiate changes in the valorization of the polarities of a research field, and as Lave argues, there is also value in viewing regulatory policy from a field perspective and in relationship to emergent sciences. Suryanarayanan and Kleinman also suggest the value of a comparative analysis of the interfield relationship in the sociology of science, with attention to the relations among social movements, epistemic cultures, and characteristics of the state. Hess uses the negative case of the breakdown of traditionally understood interfield relations to develop an explanation of the conditions under which epistemic rift and the corralling of science occur.

Institutional Logics of Fields

The second group of papers works with another problem that has sometimes been identified in field theory: the need to develop a level of cultural analysis that goes beyond the concepts of habitus and skills in Bourdieusian and strategic action versions of field theory. The authors in this group have mostly turned to neo-institutional theory, which in its early formulations borrowed heavily from Bourdieusian field theory, and within neo-institutional theory they focus on the concept of institutional logics. In “The Cultural Role of Science in Policy Implementation: Voluntary Self Regulation in the UK Building Sector,” Libby Schweber focuses on green building standards in the U.K. as an example of the liberalization of industrial regulation through voluntary standards. She draws on the analysis of organizational fields in sociological neo-institutionalism, and she is particularly interested in multiple institutional logics, such as the contrast between environmentalism and commercialism, which firms and other actors in the organizational field must negotiate. She then makes the bold theoretical move to synthesize neo-institutional theory with governmentality theory by arguing that institutional logics are embedded through multiple mechanisms elucidated by governmentality theory (e.g., visibilities, knowledge, techniques and practices, and identities). In addition to extending neo-institutional theory by studying the micro-dynamics of techniques and instruments (rather than rules and sanctions), she also argues for an expansion of the focus of the new political sociology of science from the role of science in policymaking to its role in policy implementation.

In “Field Theories and the Move toward the Market in U.S. Academic Science,” Elizabeth Popp Berman examines the theory of Fligstein and McAdam (2011, 2012), who draw on Bourdieu, organizational field theory, and social movements theory to develop a theory of strategic action fields. One of the advantages of their approach, she notes, is its explicit emphasis on interfield relations, but she also draws out some shortcomings based on her analysis of the changes in academic science in the U.S. since the 1970s. She notes that in any field there will always be multiple institutional logics available, but some prevail over others (i.e., the market logic over the science logic in academic science during the period of neoliberalization). One factor that affects the change in fortunes of an institutional logic is the environmental conditions of the field, a factor that is emphasized in Fligstein’s and McAdam’s discussion of exogenous shocks as a factor that leads to episodes of contention between challengers and incumbents (similar to the emphasis on institutional entrepreneurs in neo-institutional theory). However, Berman suggests that challengers within the scientific field were not the primary driver of field change; instead, changes were shaped by the shift of policymakers’ preferences toward research that met broader political goals of innovation and economic competitiveness. In turn, the changes in policy direction were shaped in part by representatives of large companies that had substantial research-and-development operations and were pushing policymakers to alter the policy landscape in favor of innovation research. She argues that although some episodes of field change do involve dramatic instances of contention led by challengers within the field who may take advantage of exogenous shocks, the case of the transformation of academic science in the U.S. was a more gradual process that is characterized by ongoing contention and instability. Thus, she points to the need to consider various pathways toward field change, of which the Fligstein and McAdam model of exogenous shocks followed by a mobilization by a challenger is only one possibility.

In “‘The Tip of The Day’: Field Theory and Alternative Nutrition In the U.S.,” Kelly Moore and Matthew Hoffmann study the circulation of knowledge associated with nutritional science to suggest a method for studying field change that attends to multi-decade changes in the relations among government policy, the food and health industries, and social movements. Finding Bourdieu’s focus on capital too confining, they turn instead to neo-institutional theory and its openness to a broader set of motivations and multiple institutional logics. They then review the history of nutritional policy in the U.S. to show how the rise of the Civil Rights movement led to new concern with hunger among the poor, especially African Americans. The concern led to anti-hunger policies as well as shifts in the research agendas of the nutritional science field toward issues related to hunger and malnutrition. However, by 1977 the field of nutrition policy had changed again to focus on the problem of overconsumption, and new models began to circulate that emphasized individual responsibility in the face of chronic disease and overconsumption. At the same time social movements and an emerging wellness industry emphasized the advantages of individual decisions that would lead to a healthier lifestyle, and the field of nutritional research began to shift toward issues involving chronic disease and health. Through detailed case studies of two examples of nutritional experts who also wrote for a popular audience, Moore and Hoffmann show how these hybrid scientist-popularizers blend institutional logics from biomedicine, American self-reform traditions, and neoliberal entrepreneurialism. Like Berman, who also focused on the problem of multi-decade change, Moore and Hoffmann draw attention to shifting institutional and cultural logics and changing interfield relations, and they also highlight the importance of the circulation of scientific knowledge from academic researchers through policy regimes, industrial change, and social movements.

In “What is Volunteer Water Monitoring Good For? Fracking and the Plural Logics of Participatory Science,” Abby Kinchy, Kirk Jalbert, and Jessica Lyons examine the institutional context in which citizen science has risen to prominence as a method of investigating the impacts of shale gas development by hydraulic fracturing, or “fracking.” Now widespread in the U.S., volunteer watershed monitoring reflects, on one hand, broader neoliberal shifts to cut government costs by increasingly relying on civil society organizations and volunteers to provide important services, and, on the other hand, public demands for increased participation in environmental science and decision-making. Three institutional logics have historically shaped volunteer water monitoring practices: popular education, science, and environmental policing. However, the introduction of fracking—an exogenous shock—has driven multiple changes in the interdependent fields of academic science, environmental regulation, and participatory water monitoring, leading to the mixing and reordering of these logics. In comparison to many water monitoring projects of the past, water monitoring organizations are adopting practices from academic science and environmental advocacy organizations that reflect the scientization of the regulatory field as well as the pressures on academic scientists to remain politically neutral.

In summary, the second group of four articles develops a neo-institutional approach to the scientific field by examining the changing relations among institutional logics that elucidate long term changes in a scientific field. Schweber suggests the value of governmentality studies to enable a concrete connection between broad institutional logics and specific techniques and practices, and Berman argues in favor of a multiple pathways approach to changes in institutional logics. Thus, whereas the Fligstein and McAdam approach of exogenous shocks and relatively short-term episodes of contention led by challengers may characterize some examples of field change, she argues that the study of field change should explore other pathways, including ones based on policy-driven change that, at least in her case, was partially instigated by industry. Moore and Hoffmann also take a long-term perspective on changes in the scientific field, and they draw attention more to the circulation of scientific knowledge through the state, industry, and social movements and the multiple cultural logics at play. Kinchy and colleagues also attend to multiple institutional logics, and they show how a relatively depoliticized field of citizen science has changed in response to the threat of hydraulic fracturing, the lack of government response to citizen concerns, and the mobilization of activist organizations.

As a group, the eight essays suggest the integrated nature of a next generation of field and neoinstitutional approaches to the sociology of science. The focus on interfield relations inevitably involves multiple institutional logics because of actors who move across fields or interact with actors in other fields. These institutional logics are not bounded by agents or by fields, but they tend to transect them. However, in any specific point in the history of a field, some institutional logics are more and less dominant. Thus, they do not float like a superorganic entity above social relations and social structure, and they require sociological analysis and explanation.

Institutions, Norms, and Interests

Collectively, these articles make the general argument that there is a role for theoretical frameworks in the sociology of science that address the issue of political power and draw on theory traditions associated with social field, neo-institutional analysis, governmentality studies, and social movement studies. The articles also make a more specific argument about the need to pay greater attention to interfield relations and institutional logics within these theory traditions. But how do these arguments fit into the broader social theory traditions in the sociology of science and the interdisciplinary conversations of STS (science and technology studies)? The history of theory traditions within the sociology of science (a term used here in the broad sense, to include the sociology of scientific knowledge) suggests that the fit may be uncomfortable but also potentially generative.

Because several of the essays make reference to institutional theory, a good starting point for an answer to the question is to review the historical basis of the concept of the institution and institutional analysis in the sociology of science. Although Max Weber makes frequent reference to institutions, Talcott Parsons is generally credited with developing the social theory of institutions, and his theory was in turn indebted to various predecessors, including institutional economists such as Thorstein Veblan. Charles Camic uncovered an unpublished early manuscript by Parsons on the topic, and the manuscript and several commentaries, including one by Camic (1990), appeared in a 1990 issue of the American Sociological Review (Parsons 1990). In the essay Parsons rejected behavioralist approaches and linked the general theory of institutions to normative rules that were associated with sanctions and moral authority. In the subsequent 1942 essay on “Propaganda and Social Control” (Parsons 1954), Parsons developed the analysis of institutions by situating it in the context of the question of Japanese and German military aggression. He described institutions as “patterns governing behavior and social relationships” that are based on a system of statuses and roles, the associated norms that govern behavior, and the broader cultural traditions of which the norms are a part. Adherence to norms in turn depends on a combination of a system of positive and negative sanctions and on the state of mind of the actor, which includes a belief in the legitimacy of the norms.

In the same year Robert Merton published a parallel essay originally title “Science and Technology in a Democratic Order” that was later retitled “The Normative Structure of Science” (Merton 1973[1942]). Also situated in the context of World War II, the essay was concerned with the need for the autonomy of science as an institution and the importance of that autonomy for the maintenance of a democratic society, with Soviet and Nazi science looming as counter-examples. Although the primary concern at the time was fascism, if one substitutes his concern with the domination of the scientific field by authoritarian politics with the current problem of the domination of the scientific and political fields by large corporations, in many ways the essay is quite contemporary. Like Parsons, Merton’s institutional analysis drew attention to norms and values, but for the case of science as an institution he recognized the importance of both technical norms (e.g., logical consistency, valid prediction) and moral norms. The latter included the much-debated list of universalism, communism, distinterestedness, and organized skepticism. By the time of his 1957 paper “Priorities in Scientific Discovery: A Chapter in the Sociology of Science,” which was based on his presidential address before the American Sociological Association, Merton had made several important changes in his thinking about science as an institution. First, his discussion of priority disputes led to an emphasis on the norm of originality, which was more closely tied than the other norms to the reward system, and he focused on how the reward system bestows recognition from one’s peers on work deemed original and important. This point was developed later in Hagstrom’s discussion (1965) of science as based on competition for recognition, rather than on the lingering effects of the socialization of norms, and in Bourdieu’s analysis (1975) of the particular form of capital that, as in other intellectual fields, can only emerge from the field itself. But Merton also recognized that the norm of originality could lead to dysfunctional struggles over priorities, and hence he suggested the existence of a counter-norm of humility, with the resulting condition of sociological ambivalence. The most well-known of the conflicts between norms and counter-norms, the issue of the extent to which universalism or particularism better describes the behavior of scientists and the functioning of science as a social institution, led to a fruitful, long-term competition of research programs between Merton’s network and a group of other American sociologists that is sometimes known as the “fair science” controversy (see Hess 1997). The controversy also led to increasing recognition of the difficulties of applying the analysis of norms and counter-norms to describe episodes in the history of science (e.g., Mitroff 1974). For Merton the shift to the analysis of the reward system, rather than the conflicts among norms in the decisions of individual scientists, led to a fruitful research program that included his influential work on cumulative advantage theory. In turn, the discussions of cumulative advantage, particularism and universalism, and other aspects of the fairness of science, shared affinities with the broader political programs of social liberalism, such as concern with equality of opportunity and with corrective policies, such as affirmative action, that improve universalism (Hess 2013).

Although Merton had already recognized the difficulties of applying norms to the actions and decisions of individual scientists, an influential and more systematic critique of norms emerged from the emerging networks of the sociology of scientific knowledge. Barnes and Dolby (1970) argued that the analysis of norms was troubled by the inconsistencies among a wide range of scientific practices across time periods, countries, and disciplines, and they suggested that a better approach would rely on a methodology based on Kuhn’s paradigm concept (1970) but carefully configured to allow for the clash of different paradigms among different “consensus groups.” Thus, they recognized the crucial role of competing networks that in some senses predated field theories of science. They also argued that under the circumstance of scientific controversy, professions of norms (both technical and social) may be commonplace, but Barnes and Dolby saw those statements as part of the polemical context. In effect, their shift to the importance of paradigms meant the excision of social norms from the analysis of science and, in Mertonian terms, the focus more exclusively on technical norms that were embedded in specific disciplines, subdisciplines, and paradigms.

Mulkay (1976, see also Mulkay and Gilbert 1982) developed a similar argument based on the empirical claim (especially developed in Mitroff 1974) that scientists in fact frequently deviate from general social norms such as rationality, emotional neutrality, universalism, distinterestedness, impartiality, communality, humility, and organized skepticism. Instead, the only relevant norms are “furnished, not by this set of social norms, but by the scientific frameworks and technical procedures in terms of which the research community is internally differentiated” (1976: 639). Mulkay then drew on historical work on the political context of the articulation of the pure science ideal (Daniels 1967) to develop the argument that social norms were in fact an occupational ideology of scientists used to justify their autonomy and support in their relations with the state or, to use our terminology, the extrafield patrons of science. Drawing on ethnomethodology, Mulkay argued that norms are repertoires that scientists select from and perform based on their social interests. In effect, they are outcomes of scientists’ action rather than causes. This agent-centered approach to the analysis of science was then taken up in many instances in the subsequent history of the sociology of scientific knowledge, including more recent work on performativity and economics (e.g., Callon 2007).

More generally, a consensus emerged that the analysis of social norms was inadequate for the emerging task of a sociology of scientific knowledge (Bloor 1991), which studied the “content” of knowledge (such as the controversies over theories, methods, and empirical claims) rather than the institutions of knowledge production (e.g., the reward system). However, although Mulkay had suggested the potential for an analytical strategy grounded in the interests of scientists, similar problems occurred with interests analysis, the first main research program in the sociology of scientific knowledge in the strong-program tradition. The problems occurred mainly with the attempt to link controversies between networks within a research field (such as rival groups of statisticians, where an “interest” in a network’s success was visceral) to broader social statuses and political ideology, where connections with broader social interests were more problematic (MacKenzie 1981, 1983, 1984; MacKenzie and Barnes 1979). These studies provoked a firestorm of criticism, again suggesting the tenuousness of sociologies of scientific knowledge that attempt to forge a linkage to extrascientific statuses, and the complaints about scientists as “interest dopes” were memorialized in Woolgar’s phrase, “instead of norms we now have interests” (1981: 375).

It might have been possible for MacKenzie to find archived statements by the statisticians he studied that showed, in their own minds, the connections between their intellectual positions within the scientific field and their broader social and political positions. But the difficulty of finding such needles in haystacks helped to motivate the turn toward contemporary ethnographic methods in studies of laboratories and controversies. Of these studies, Harry Collins’s work was one of the most sociological research programs, and his empirical program of relativism (which was also the precursor of the social construction of technology program in technology studies) recognized the value of relating the mechanisms of closure of a controversy to “the wider social and political structure” (1983: 96). In his description of this third phase, he suggested that alignments between one side of a controversy within the scientific field with positions outside the field (such as industrial power) could explain how one side of a controversy had access to greater resources that helped it to close a controversy in its favor. However, this methodological insight remained largely undeveloped in subsequent work in the sociology of scientific knowledge.

Whereas Collins’s method suggested a way to solve the problem of imputation of interests through a causal analysis of the effects of extrafield alignments with resource availability, there was also a solution to the problem of the third phase through cultural analysis. Traweek’s ethnographic study (1988) of physicists were exemplary in this regard, and one really needs to follow her whole corpus (including work on women Japanese physicists) to see the import of the method that was being developed in cultural anthropology and feminist cultural analysis. Cultural analysis could link extrafield statuses (that is, the status of physicists as Japanese or American, men or women, and so on) to technical distinctions within the field (preferences for research programs and detector designs) without having to run the gauntlet of causal explanation. In other words, through the analysis of “cultural logics,” or homologies of difference (Japanese is to American as one type of physics is to another), one could bring to light the alignment of distinctions in the broader social structure (nationality, race, gender, class, political affiliation) and distinctions in technical culture without making claims that scientists intentionally imported an allegiance to a specific extrascientific position into their intellectual positions in the scientific field. Interfield homologies, like other semiotic structures, may be visible only to the social scientist, but as noted above one must exercise caution in the analysis so as not to postulate relationships without some empirical evidence. Thus, it is important to pay attention to shifters, to mediating categories, that are identical and enable translation across different domains. Bourdieu (1981) used a similar method in his studies of misrecognition and the analysis of Heidegger’s philosophy, and he added that the method was necessary to avoid the “short-circuit effect” of interests analyses associated with the early applications of the strong program (1990). Thus, cultural analysis (a term that Bourdieu did not use but essentially was what he was doing in his analysis of Heidegger and the philosophical field) could accomplish the goals of interests analysis and Collins’s third phase without treading into the murky water of the explicit motivations of individuals (Hess 1995).

Cultural analysis of one form or another became widespread in the broader STS field (at least if one defines the boundaries of the field broadly), and it was especially prominent in work by anthropologists and feminists (e.g., Haraway 1989, Harding 1998, Martin 1994, Rapp 2000), but it never took root in the sociology of scientific knowledge. Traweek’s work suffered a dismissive review by Latour (1990), who argued that she was part of a tradition in anthropology running from Durkheim and Mauss through Lévi-Strauss, who all took for granted the distinction between nature and culture that his analysis of hybrids avoided. Although perhaps convincing to those unfamiliar with the anthropological tradition that he referenced, to those more familiar with the research tradition the argument misses the more complicated discussion of the coconstitution of natural and social categories that emerged in Lévi-Strauss’s Totemism (1963) and was widely recognized in the field, including in many analyses that applied anthropological structuralism to modern society (e.g., Sahlins 1976). In any case the article had the effect of dampening the engagement between cultural anthropology and the sociology of scientific knowledge. It was not until the thirty-year retrospectives of laboratory studies at the annual meeting of the Society for the Social Studies of Science that Traweek earned the field’s recognition in the form of a place at the table of a plenary section. But in general, the two fields went their separate ways, and from comments that we heard during the 1990s, the influx of anthropologies, feminisms, and cultural studies into the STS field during that decade was perceived as a threat.

Another “problem” (depending on one’s viewpoint) with cultural analysis was the ease with which it could be turned back onto the history of the sociology of science in the form of a critical, reflexive sociology. Thus, one might ask why the turn toward individualized, agency-based frameworks, of which actor-network theory was the epitome, took root during the 1970s and 1980s. The beginning of an answer to the question might take advantage of the comparative perspective of changes that were going on in other fields as well, such as the turn toward ritual and performance in cultural anthropology (Geertz 1973) and toward resource mobilization theory in social movement studies (McCarthy and Zald 1973). It is not hard to develop an analysis of the cultural homologies between the rise of agency-based theories in the social sciences and the turn toward individual responsibilization described by Foucault (2008) in his studies of neoliberalism (see also Moore and Hoffmann, this volume; Hess 2013; and Mirowski and Nik-Khah 2007). This is not to say that any particular defender of an agency-based theory in the social sciences had neoliberal political leanings (a claim that would fall victim to the “short-circuit” problem), or that political sociologists are all socialists. In fact, participation in the STS field for decades has taught us that there is a striking similarity of political positions among social scientists in the field, with most endorsing some form of social liberalism. But cultural analysis is interested in another level, the homologies of distinctions between political ideologies and social theory positions that can be related through common grounds (the shifters), such as the focus on individual action.

Despite the opportunity offered by cultural analysis to develop one type of a sociology of scientific knowledge that takes into account extrafield relationships, the focus of most of the early research programs in the sociology of scientific knowledge remained on the microsociological problem of how networks of scientists negotiate contending claims about theories, methods, research results, and agendas for future research. This focus was, like all research programs, both productive and restrictive; the sociology of science has benefited from a wide range of highly useful concepts such as interpretive flexibility, experimenter’s regress, closure, enrollment, and obligatory points of passage. However, in the strongest versions of this tendency, the work became an anti-sociology in which social structure was viewed as an epiphenomenon of the action of individuals or heterogeneous networks, and the emergence of such positions occasioned a division within the sociology of scientific knowledge (Bloor 1999, Yearley 2005). The pitched nature of these debates tended to subside over time, especially after the field shifted toward studies of sciences and publics and began to engage more with questions of power and inequality in the circulation of knowledge among expert communities and their counterparts in the state, industry, and the media.

This brief review is helpful to situate the potential reception of a term such as “neoinstitutional” analysis within the context of the history of the sociologies of science. Given the freighted history of concepts such as norms and interests in the sociology of scientific knowledge, there is a tendency in the field to police their re-emergence. For example, we witnessed a young naïf who, at an annual meeting of the Social Studies of Science, invoked an interests explanation, and a senior figure quickly rose to remind the audience that the field views interests as outcomes of action rather than causes of it and that such issues were settled long ago. Likewise, an article in the journal Social Studies of Science criticized sociologists who advocate the inclusion of structural explanation in the analysis of scientific change (e.g., Kleinman 2003, Frickel and Moore 2005), argued that “coherent cultural and institutional logics are nowhere to be found” (Jones 2009: 843), and concluded that Mertonian norms were still a dead horse. In a subsequent article published in the same journal that might be read as a nonpolemical rebuttal, Suryanarayanan and Kleinman (2013) retained the discussion of norms and showed how research norms (or “technical norms” in terms of the discussion above) such as a preference for false negatives came to dominate the research field that links pesticides to colony collapse disorder. The authors also showed that the dominance of the norms led to the marginalization of the epistemic claims of commercial beekeepers, and they added, “The ways in which honey bee scientists approached insecticide issues were influenced by the broader agricultural organizational and scientific communities within which they were situated, where the dominant agro-entomological knowledge framed insects as pests that they needed to be targeted through chemicals” (2013: 14). Here, then, is an example of delivery on the deferred promise of Collins’s third phase, but without the problematics of social norms and the short-circuit of Edinburgh interest analysis. Suryanarayanan and Kleinman use the concept of norms to draw out a comparative analysis of differences within a research community and its effects on the status of lay knowledge in the interfield relationship among bee researchers, pesticide manufacturers, regulators, farmers, and beekeepers.

Although some researchers no longer use the term “norms,” even technical norms, due to the history outlined above and the risk of being accused of Mertonian atavism, a family of related concepts continues to emerge. Like the return of the repressed, there appears to be a need for some kind of theoretical concept that does at least some of the theoretical work of norms. We suggest, following the article by Suryanarayanan and Kleinman in this volume, that the intellectual space (or the undone social science) occupied by the absence of the term “norms” involves comparative analysis in the broadest sense. Many terms have come to occupy this intellectual space. For example, the early Foucault used the term “episteme” (what could be viewed as a system of technical norms) to describe continuities across disciplinary differences at a particular time period and likewise differences across those continuities over time, Knorr-Cetina (1999) used the term “epistemic cultures” to describe differences across scientific research fields, and Jasanoff (2005) developed the concept of “civic epistemologies” for her comparative studies of modes of public reasoning and science. It is very likely that the use of the term “institutional logics” in several papers in this volume also emerges in response to comparative work, whether it is across social fields, across time periods, or across forms of public engagement in science. Such comparative work is necessary if one views social science as a nomothetic project that develops generalizations, albeit often limited in scope in the tradition of Merton’s middle-range theory, rather than an idiographic project akin to history and ethnography. One could make the argument that all of these social scientists are talking about norms of some sort, but as Hess (1995) suggested, it might be better to think about the diverse approaches in this family as utilizing some variant of the underlying concept of culture in the anthropological sense, that is, as systems of meaning that are both models of and for action. Of course, in contrast to Geertz (1973), who like Merton emphasized uniformity, one must view culture as always a plural field of cultural systems, ideologies broadly defined, or institutional logics that actors continually reformulate, challenge, undermine, and defend. In this sense, a central aspect of culture is the possibility for actors, including collective actors, in subordinate positions in a field or social structure to change it, even if the effects of their actions are not what they originally intended.

Fields of Contention

The discussion up to this point has identified the triangle of structure, agency, and meaning as central to the broad social theory currents in the sociology of science. As the sociologists of scientific knowledge pointed out, the use of social norms and extrafield interests is vexed by complex methodological problems because of the relative autonomy of the field and the tendency for controversies to be settled according to local, technical norms rooted in the standards of subdisciplines and paradigms. (If one does not believe in technical norms at this point, then we challenge the denier to submit an article outside his or her area of methodological competence to a major sociology journal and see what happens.) But we are interested here in a set of research questions that involve a different kind of sociology of science and scientific knowledge than the negotiation of epistemic claims within a research community. Those questions include which research fields become favored for resources and which ones are left to wither away, how what counts as valid science changes in the circulation of knowledge across social fields, and how and why the reward system for scientific research and career attainment has changed over time. These questions can involve attention to how knowledge is interpreted, shaped, and constructed, and the study of the contours of research fields, the patterns of done and undone science, certainly involves a type of sociology of scientific knowledge. However, there is also a need for explanatory resources that take into account long-term historical changes in the relationship between the scientific field and other social fields. In this sense, there is a need for an analysis of the changing relations of structure, agency, and culture.

Arguably the most systematic and comprehensive attempt in twentieth-century social theory to bring together the triad in one theoretical framework is the work of Pierre Bourdieu. His background in anthropology and fieldwork in Algeria enabled him to have a semiotic sense of culture, but his ongoing conversation with Marx and Weber also enabled him to situate that understanding in a sociology of action. Whatever one thinks about Bourdieu, it is difficult to accuse him of structural determinism in the sense of a Marxist analysis of science such as Hessen (1971), but his sociology also does not reduce social structure to an epiphenomenal outcome of individual and collective performance.

However, there is a problem in turning to Bourdieu in the sociology of science. He did not write much about science per se, and his sparse writings also reveal significant gaps in his knowledge of the field that to some degree are covered over with polemic. Thus, one’s reading of Bourdieu, at least in the sociology of science, needs to be a somewhat recuperative exercise. It does not facilitate matters to find that Bourdieu also changed his views over time. For example, in his mature writings on science (2001), he criticized his own criticisms of Merton as outcomes of his own youthful ambitions and lack of understanding, and he developed a more sympathetic interpretation of Merton based on his biographical background and habitus. Although Bourdieu (1991) had earlier followed Mulkay by arguing that Merton presents “little more than a record of the normative rules which are officially professed by its members” (1990:298), in his later work Bourdieu has a more tempered appreciation of Merton, but he also recognized a fundamental difference between his field sociology and Merton’s institutional sociology: whereas Merton emphasized the functionality of the reward system and an irenic notion of scientific community, Bourdieu emphasized the scientific field as a site of relations of conflict and cooperation characterized by inequalities of position.

At a more technical level, Bourdieu also expanded on the Mertonian reward system by arguing that scientists accumulated various types of capital, and he developed the convertibility thesis to argue that different types of capital could be translated. For example, a scientist with high symbolic capital (recognition) and cultural capital (knowledge about the field) is in a good position to bring in a grant (financial capital), acquire a new organizational position with more resources (temporal capital), and expand the social network of colleagues (social capital). In turn, as Latour and Woolgar (1979) noted in their extension of Bourdieu’s convertibility thesis to their analysis of cycles of credibility, the new levels of financial capital from a grant would enable the scientist to produce a new stream of publications that would lead to new levels of symbolic capital.

Although this example suggests that Bourdieu had influenced some of the early formulations of the sociology of scientific knowledge (see also Knorr 1979), the sociologists of scientific knowledge did not embrace the concepts of field, capital, and habitus, at least not explicitly, and he became increasingly critical of what he called the “new sociology of science.” For example, with respect to Harry Collins’s work on interpretive flexibility and the experimenter’s regress, Bourdieu writes:

Remaining within the confines of the laboratory, he does not at all consider the structural conditions of the production of belief, with for example what might be called “lab capital,” brought to light by the Mertonians, who showed, for example, as we have seen, that if a discovery is made in a reputed laboratory at a prestigious university it has more chance of being validated than if it emerges in another, less well-regarded one (2001: 21).

Whether such criticisms are fair—and against the vast writings of Collins on the gravitational wave controversy, there is much more attention to the positionality of scientists than Bourdieu is willing to admit here—is a question that can be bracketed for the present purposes. Instead, the comment is symptomatic of the tension that emerged between Bourdieu and the leaders of the sociology of scientific knowledge. In a similar vein, he goes on to praise Gilbert and Mulkay (1984) for drawing attention to the different registers of scientific rhetoric (one geared toward the contingencies of the research process and the other toward the impersonal rules of publication), but he disagrees with Knorr-Cetina (1983) over the use of the latter as conscious and, in his terms, “cynical stratagems, oriented toward the glory of researchers” (2001: 25). With respect to Latour’s Pasteur (Latour 1988), Bourdieu writes:

Rather than seeking the principle of actions where it really lies, in positions and dispositions, Latour can only try to find it in conscious (even cynical) influence and power strategies (thus regressing from a Mertonian collective finalism to a finalism of individual agents)—and the science of science is reduced to the description of alliances and struggles for symbolic “credit” (2001: 29).

His perhaps at times unmeasured criticism, or what he reflexively acknowledged as a righteous wrath, especially for actor-network theory, led to predictably hostile reviews of his last writings on science (e.g., Gieryn 2006, Mialet 2003, Sismondo 2005).

Whatever one thinks of Bourdieu’s evaluation of the sociology of scientific knowledge (and it was not very detailed), one can separate that issue from the question of the utility of the broad concept of fields in the sociology of science. One of the important contributions of Bourdieu’s work methodologically is to situate the question of positions and dispositions at the center of the analytical framework. The social positions can be assessed by examining various levels of capital, but it is the broader relationship among networks of researchers in a field that is of greater interest, that is, the relations of dominant and subordinate networks in a field and the analysis of power that is enabled by the perspective. Although it is possible that some research fields lack such polarities or are characterized merely by a pluralism of camps with relatively equal positions, it is the attention to the polarities of subordinate and dominant positions that is the hallmark of a field sociology of science.

This methodology has many implications, including for research methods. The approach suggests that the social scientist pay attention to the wallflowers of the party, those scientists who are still in the game but scarred by years of shunning and social exclusion. In other words, one would look at the rejected and alternative research programs, including the ones that are still recognized as part of the field and the ones that are pushed out of the field. Whereas scientists at the top of the field either refuse to recognize their domination or view the existence of subordinate networks through the lens of symbolic violence, those at the bottom of the hierarchy often prove to be the best insider sociologists of their own field and its political contours of done and undone science.

Perhaps the most important implication of a notion of fields that is based on relationships of dominant and subordinate networks is the ready possibility of a cultural analysis of the homologies of field positions with distinctions in other social fields. For Bourdieu the habitus, the network of dispositions, was the causal mechanism by which the extrafield distinctions became overlaid on the technical distinctions of the field. To make the rather abstract point concrete with a well-known example, when women were finally allowed into primatology, the theories of human evolution (e.g., the “man the hunter” thesis) altered as did the findings and methods of primatology. However, as Haraway (1989) also showed in the significant last section of Primate Visions, there were woman-inflected methodologies that took multiple and unpredictable forms. In effect, when the research field became opened to new entrants (the exogenous shock, to bring in one element of the field sociology of Fligstein and McAdam 2011), the dispositions of the researchers changed and the configuration of networks of research programs underwent contestation. Although new female entrants to primatologyembraced the illusio of the field (the inclination to be part of the field and the ability to participate in it on its own terms), and they also tended to receive patronage support from one of the “big men” of the field, they embraced the illusio in new ways that led to scientific change and, in Harding’s terms (1998), also to a condition of stronger objectivity of the science itself.

Thus, one can draw out of field theory a method for analyzing field changes and transitions in the configuration of dominant and subordinate networks of researchers and programs that allows for at least an implicit recognition of interfield relations. As the social composition of the field changes due to new entrants (which can include new social categories based on exogenous statuses, in-migration of scientists from other research fields, and simply new generations of students), intellectual tastes change along with the transitions of habitus. However, Bourdieu’s general focus on the relative autonomy of the scientific field and its internal dynamics, like the similar focus of both Merton and the early microsociologies of scientific knowledge, was both a source of great insight and a constraint on theory, especially as the broader field of STS has moved on to study issues of the circulation of scientific knowledge in broader public arenas.

One area for the improvement of field theory involves the tendency to link the habitus to the extrafield biographical trajectories of individuals such as their class culture (Swartz 1997). But as the previous example suggests, changes in the scientific field are articulated through clashing research programs (or paradigms, or theory-methods-problems packages), and field theory could be improved by having a more explicit formulation of the relations among these research programs. They form a separate cultural field that transects the field of social positions, and the social networks of dominant and subordinate positions of scientists are not necessarily congruent with the intellectual networks of allegiance to dominant and subordinate research programs. In effect, there are two fields at play: a field of social positions based on the capital of individual scientists and their social networks, and a cultural field of diacritically related research programs. These research programs are both intellectual forms and objects of intensely passionate support and criticism, a feature of fields that can prevent field analysis from devolving into a kind of rational-actor theory of capital accumulation.

In the political field, there is also a need to think in terms of a double field, but here the clashes of ideas and policy programs appear as ideologies, which are not always completely homologous with the positions of political parties, social movements, trade associations, and other significant collective actors in the field (Hess 2014). Bourdieu (2005) recognized, at least in passing, this level in his study of the neoliberalization of French housing policy. Although he focused on the habitus of the agents in the housing policy field, he could not have accomplished his analysis without also making reference to the broad clash of political ideologies that motivated the passions of the participants and oriented the alliances and coalitions in the political conflict.

When one seeks to examine the level of the cultural field of contrasting cultural systems that appear in various social fields, the need for a general term emerges. Here, several authors in this volume have opted for the term “institutional logics,” borrowed from neo-institutional theories in organization studies. It is possible that the use of the term “logics” may be misunderstood to imply that such systems are necessarily only cognitive and always formally codified, as opposed to the much less restrictive interpretation of “logics” that understands the systems as defined relationally. For example, in the ideological field one understands neoliberalism as what it is not: not social liberalism, not socialism, not localism, and so on. Likewise, it is also possible that against the context of the sociologies of science reviewed here, the term “institutional” will invite misreadings as reversions to Mertonian social norms. Thus, the combination of the two words into “institutional logics” may invite misreadings. But Berman’s contrast (this volume) of a science logic and market logic is not about norms that turn scientists into marionettes, just as it is not about well-defined and rationalized systems of thought; rather, the term “institutional logic” refers to a contrastive set of configurations of the discourses, organizations, and everyday practices of knowledge production. This field of contrasting cultural systems also is not an unmoved mover of social action; rather, it is also the object of explicit and implicit contestation. In this sense there is a continuity with field theory, because Berman, like Kinchy and colleagues (this volume), is attending to the issue of which logics come to dominate particular fields of action, and their analysis is sociological because they seek to elucidate the conditions under which some logics come become dominant and others marginal.

The Circulation of Science

Perhaps the peace in the feud—the doxa that unites Merton, Bourdieu, and the microsociologies of some of the early laboratory and controversy studies—was the focus on science as a relatively autonomous social field 𝗈𝗋,\mathsf { o r } , in Bourdieu’s phrase (2001), as a world apart. But STS as a research field has long ago left the laboratory, and it has followed science and scientists as they circulate through society. Indeed, the focus on the intrafield dynamics of science in Bourdieu was one of the shortcomings in Bourdieusian field theory that led Knorr-Cetina (1981) to the discussion of transcientific fields, just as the idea of following scientists through society became part of the allure of actor-network theory, which moved back-and-forth through the fields of science, politics, industry, and public health (Latour 1988). The concern with science outside the laboratory is also expressed in the “public turn” of STS, led by Wynne’s work on lay publics (1996) and the blossoming on research under the broad rubric of the public understanding of science, as well as in the increasing attention to the issues pioneered by Jasanoff (1990) in studies of science and the state.

Notwithstanding the turn to the study of science “at large” rather than as a “world apart,” there is, as yet, no general theory of the interfield dynamics of science. Some elements of an interfield sociology of scientific knowledge in the research programs are outlined above, and there are also elements of an historical and organizational sociology of science and industry in the study of the asymmetric convergence of industrial and academic science (Kleinman and Vallas 2001), the shifting position of institutional logics in academic science (Berman 2012), and the shifting valuation of the producer and consumer poles of the scientific field (Albert and McGuire, this volume). Furthermore, these changes are part of the broader historical sociology of advanced capitalism, with the growth of power of multinational corporations, the corporatization of the political field, and the financialization of the economy.

One of the new areas of interfield theory suggested by the articles in this volume might be developed by starting from the different types or species of knowledge, not in the sense of disciplinary differences or even the basic and applied difference, but instead more in parallel with Marx’s discussion (1992) of the different species of capital (merchant, industrial, financial, petty) and their inter-relations, and within STS Collins’s and Evans’s (2009) discussion of types of expertise. From this perspective an interfield theory of science involves the relations among academic science, including its applied or consumer pole; industrial science and the knowledges embedded in technological standards and innovatoins; the lay and local knowledge of patients and communities; and occupational expertise. The interest here is not in developing a normative model of how these different types of knowledge should interact; rather, the problem is to develop a sociological analysis of the conditions under which different versions emerge for the circulation of science, other forms of expertise, and scientists across the social fields. We identify two primary types of circulation as an example of one way of moving the theoretical discussion forward.

In the advisory-patronage pathway, scientists provide expertise to identify and solve political and industrial problems, and in turn they receive financial support for their work. As many studies of science policy have documented, scientists work hard to guard their autonomy and also their political neutrality, and when they are successful, their research can provide the epistemic basis, the agreedupon conditions of reality, for political conflict. However, the authority of scientists to establish the doxa of the political field—the conditions of what is the case upon which debates about what should be done take place—can rapidly break down when their research identifies problems that offend industrial or other political interests. Under those conditions, epistemic rifts emerge, even to the point that scientists are attacked and research programs are defunded (see Hess, this volume). In a similar but less conflictual way, industrial science can emerge to displace the authority of academic science, as Lave (2012) shows in the case of the Rosgen approach to stream restoration. Although the industrial science of Rosgen was a simplification of what academic science had to offer the industry and regulators, it was also preferable because of its consistency with the market requirements of standardized units that could be commodified as financial products in mitigation banking.

The second pathway of circulation, appropriation-scientization, involves the exchange with everyday practices, lay knowledges, and bureaucratic rules and routines. In the original appropriation pattern, the primitive accumulation of modern scientific knowledge, scientific knowledge grew significantly through colonial expeditions, botanical gardens, and museum collections. However, as the scientific field became more esoteric, quantified, and laboratory based, the appropriation of lay and non-Western knowledges became less important in many research fields. Nevertheless, in some cases these other forms of knowledge travel back into science, sometimes through the habitus of new entrants and the diversification of the social composition of science, and sometimes through direct interactions of scientists and publics, as Kinchy and colleagues suggest (this volume). In the scientization side of the cycle, scientific knowledge becomes embedded in everyday practices, lay knowledge (for example, about health behavior), and bureaucratic routines. As Kinchy (2012) has analyzed, the rule making regimes of regulatory agencies have also undergone scientization, which can create barriers to participation by social movements. Furthermore, as Moore and Hoffmann (this volume) suggest, in the health field the messaging of government public-health initiatives and the marketing efforts of privatesector health industries lead to transitions in everyday practices that incorporate new knowledges about healthy behavior that are often enveloped in a politics of neoliberal responsibilitization. Likewise, as Schweber (this volume) describes, regulatory policies and voluntary standards also embed scientific rationality and analysis in industrial practices.

Under conditions of epistemic modernization, this second pathway waxes; that is, the scientific field becomes more open to the questions raised by social movements, community organizations, and other sources of lay knowledge, which bring issues of research agendas and undone science to the attention of scientific researchers. However, there is also the countervailing condition of hermeticization, where the scientific field is so esoteric and laboratory based (as in advanced physics) that the trafficking with lay knowledge (except in a few areas, such as cosmology) is highly limited.

These two examples of the circulation of scientific knowledge, the advisory-patronage pathway and the appropriation-scientization pathway, suggest one direction for an interfield theory of science as well as for sociological questions about causal conditions that can be framed from within that theory. In other words, under certain conditions, these cycles can become augmented or weakened. One can study the conditions that facilitate the appearance of epistemic rift in the advisory-patronage cycle or epistemic modernization in the appropriation-scientization cycle. We are in a theoretically uncharted terrain, but one that can build on the studies of classifications of expertise, science-advisor relationships, and lay-expert interactions.

We end here with the beginning, the essays of the volume, and the hope that readers will, like us, find great potential in the essays that have been selected for inclusion, not only because they develop new empirical contributions to the field but also because they help to open up new terrain in the social theory of science, its interfield relations in the contemporary world, and political power.
