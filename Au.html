Introduction

Chinese Scientific Ascendancy

Science does not know national boundaries, but scientists have motherlands.

—Xi Jinping, 2020 Symposium of Scientists

The existence of transnational fields (scientific ones, in particular) creates specific common senses which call the national common sense into question and it favors the emergence of a scholastic view of the world that is (more or less) common to all the scholars of all countries.

—Pierre Bourdieu, Pascalian Medications

China’s dream of national rejuvenation—or the “Chinese Dream,” a term coined by Xi Jinping, general secretary of the Communist Party of China and president of the People’s Republic of China—is inseparably tied to its scientific ascendancy. The first quotation comes from a speech Xi gave to a gathering of elite scientists in 2020 to solicit their ideas for China’s Fourteenth Five-National plans such as the Five-Year Plans hold some sway over what happens across the vast fragmented machinery that is the Chinese state.2 In the same speech, Xi advised that “we must more actively integrate ourselves into global innovation networks,” promote “openness and tolerance and shared benefits,” and recruit “foreign scientists [to fill] positions in vast stage for open, global S&T cooperation.” As the sociologist Pierre Bourdieu observed in Pascalian Meditations, this transnational orientation of scientists creates shared sensibilities 4 among researchers globally. At the same time, Xi pointed to the critical role of science and technology in helping China build “a modernized socialist country,” “give impetus to domestic circulation [boost domestic demand and consumption],” and “realize the great rejuvenation of the Chinese nation.” These goals are not contradictory, as these global and national ambitions are

tightly intertwined.

Xi’s stated goal for the nation to “ascend the peaks of science” appears frequently in contemporary policy documents in China. Much like the mountains on the cover of this book, Xi has urged scientists in China to climb to the mountain summits of their respective fields, and to overcome the many challenges and struggles that await them on the journey. In the Thirteenth Five-Year Plan, Chinese policymakers stated their desire for their country to become an “innovation-oriented country,” “a world S&T superpower,” and an “S&T talent superpower. ,5, 5 Likewise, Healthy China 2030, ostensibly a plan to improving the health and well-being of Chinese society, includes the following goal: “Strengthen breakthroughs in key technologies such as chronic disease prevention and control, precision medicine . . . and modernization of Chinese medicine. . . . These dreams, aspirations, and imaginaries of global science matter. The science and technology studies scholars Sheila Jasanoff and Sang-hyun Kim have written about how the socio-technical imaginaries shared by members of a nation-state shape the deployment of scientific and technological innovations.12 Imaginaries can be made real and concrete. These dreams guide and coordinate action across different social groups when written into national plans. Scientists, businesspeople, patients, policymakers, doctors, and university administrators listen to the exhortations from the state and implement these new dictates in their localities. Even before policymakers formulate concrete implementation models and comprehensive policies, their slogans and buzzwords drive action.13 While the ideal of global science may mean one thing to some scientists, it can hold different meanings for other scientists, depending on their social, institutional, and physical locations. To become a leader in global science, the Chinese state needs to experiment.

That global science is, in fact, truly global is the ideal that underpins the assumption that China can one day indeed become a scientific superpower. Yet one only has to talk to any scientist to find that the reality is more complex.14 The ideal of an open, meritocratic, and inclusive science—where the best and brightest scientists succeed, no matter where they are—is a far cry from the lived realities of scientists worldwide. This is particularly true for those in the Global South. The sociologists Charles J. Gomez, Andrew C. Herman, and Paolo Parigi found in a large-scale analysis of twenty million papers in over 150 different fields that scientists in the Global North (particularly those in the United States) are far more likely to be cited than Global South scientists doing similar work (as measured by textual similarity).15 There are many reasons for this, including language, institutional prestige, the ability to disseminate one’s research through journals and conferences, and access to funding. A particularly compelling explanation, however, comes from the sociologist Kathrin Zippel, who argues that each scientist and researcher has an “academic nationality” tied to their training and current institutional affiliation. This gives US and European academics distinct advantages over their international counterparts outside these areas.16 While other social statuses such as gender also shape researchers’ participation in global science, academic nationality remains one of the most salient

categories.

Chinese scientists,17 in particular, have complained about discrimination for doing “Chinese science”—a term that is sometimes invoked pejoratively. Many Chinese scientists have frequently encountered stereotypes and accusations of plagiarism, research misconduct, and censorship.18 In a slightly different vein of criticism, Chinese science has been portrayed as being less innovative and simply copying what is done in the West. The implication of this portrayal is that Chinese scientists are not seen as serious actors in global science. But as the anthropologist Silvia M. Lindtner argues, Chinese innovators do not simply want to copy the West, but want to create a new model of a global China. Chinese academic science does have real structural issues, including a promotion and bonus system that relies too heavily on publication and thus incentivizes cheating. These features of Chinese science are widely acknowledged and spoken about by scientists in China. But it is likely that actual misconduct is perceived as occurring more frequently than it actually does. These perceptions shape how journal editors and conference organizers outside of China evaluate research produced by scientists in China. This, in turn, affects the career prospects of Chinese scientists. These dynamics shape Chinese scientists’ aspirations when it comes to seeking out international collaborations and applying for academic positions and opportunities overseas. The sociologists Joy Y. Zhang and Saheli Datta Burton aptly describe this predicament as one of “epistemic injustice,” which has shaped the “national scientific habitus” of Chinese scientists, who find themselves having to do more work to prove that what they are producing is, in fact, good and innovative science.19 Such an enduring position of inferiority likely will cause lasting damage not just to the careers of individual scientists but also to the global community of scientists.

In the life sciences, a series of high-profile scientific controversies—such as the scandal that arose when He Jianjui used CRISPR gene-editing techniques on human embryos—has further framed Chinese scientists as amoral “rogue” scientists.20 Even though elite scientists within China quickly disavowed He’s actions, the incident soon became a symbol of how unchecked ambition and a lack of ethics seemed to pervade China’s scientific system. But as the bioethicist Haidan Chen argues, this perception that China is a “Wild East,” lacking regulation for biomedical research, is a cliché. The reality is that China has allowed a wide range of regulatory frameworks to proliferate in many emerging areas of biotechnology.21 In recent years, China has centralized its regulation over the sector and has enacted more stringent regulations and laws governing biomedical research.22 In some instances, these rules are among the strictest in place anywhere. Even without these formal regulations, however, most Chinese biomedical researchers with global ambitions have sought to align their work with globally shared values and priorities. The informal incentives of scientific recognition and inclusion in the global community of scientists serve as powerful motivations for Chinese scientists to conform to shared norms. Despite the disadvantages and stigma of Chinese science, the dream of global science has attracted hundreds of thousands of Chinese students to study abroad to gain exposure to how science is conducted there.

This book is about China’s scientific ascendancy. It traces the country’s dreams of global science through the experiences of life scientists engaged in the new program of precision medicine (I provide a brief background on this term later in this introduction). These dreams provide scientists with goals, priorities, norms, scripts, routines, and access to social networks where they can advance their ideas with a global community of peers. But these dreams are unevenly distributed. Access to global science is stratified and unequal across different social categories, including academic nationality. This, I argue, has consequences for the types of science produced and the content of scientific discovery. Paying attention to where a scientist is located within the global field of science allows us insights into their orientation toward different scientific goals and norms. One broad conclusion that I draw from this study is that the global scientific field must become more inclusive in order to sustain shared equitable and just visions of what science ought to be.

A Brief History of Science and Technology in China

Before venturing further, a bit more background is needed on science and technology in China. The Five-Year Plans described at the beginning of this introduction only scratch the surface of China’s dreams of scientific ascendancy. One could go back many years. More than seventy years ago, the prolific historian of science Joseph Needham meticulously documented the “limitless caverns of Chinese scientific history.” He showed how Chinese technological discoveries frequently predated those in Europe (and perhaps even seeded innovation there).23 At the beginning of the twentieth century, science played a central role in Chinese imaginaries about modernity. The New Culture Movement and the May Fourth Movement in the 1910s and 1920s rallied around the figures of Mr. Science and Mr. Democracy, which movement leaders hoped would one day replace Mr. Confucius in the new China.24 This linking of science and modernity continued with the founding in 1928 of Academia Sinica, the Republic of China’s top scientific organization. In 1949 during the Civil War, several of its institutes relocated to Taipei and continue their work to this day. Within the People’s Republic of China, the remaining institutes were renamed the Chinese Academy of Sciences in the 1980s.25

This reverence for science was lost during the Cultural Revolution (1966 to 1976), a period in which intellectuals and scientists were persecuted and college entrance exams (gao kao) were canceled.26 The tumult of the Cultural Revolution devastated Chinese science and traumatized a generation of scientists. Many aspiring scientists had their career ambitions cut short or derailed when universities were shuttered and intellectuals were persecuted and sent to the countryside to perform hard and menial labor. The resumption of science diplomacy in the late 1970s, despite the Cold War, was part of what sustained Chinese science after this period.27 The death of Mao Zedong in 1976 was followed by a period of reform and opening under Deng Xiaoping, as universities resumed operations and exchanges and interactions with foreign scientists once again became permissible.

Soon policymakers recognized the need to strengthen China’s scientific system if the country was ever to become strong and prosperous. The Chinese state saw technology as tied to national power, the ability to compete with foreign countries, and the cultivation of indigenous innovation. Much of what the political scientist Evan A. Feigenbaum refers to as China’s “techno-nationalist” orientation has military origins in this historical period.28 Yet there exists a palpable tension between China’s desires both to cultivate openness toward global science and to maintain closedness to further techno-nationalist ambitions. Kathryn C. Ibata-Arens, also a political scientist, has referred to this emerging strategy of striving to strike a balance between encouraging the transnational flows of ideas, technologies, and people while simultaneously 29 protecting core nationalist interests as “networked techno-nationalism.” Ya-wen Lei, a sociologist, has more recently described the relationship among science, state, and capital in China as a “techno-developmental regime” layered on top of previous social, economic, and political relationships.30 Lin Zhang, a communications studies scholar, has likewise pointed to such contradictory and overlapping institutions as kinship- and place-based social networks in shaping China’s emerging digital economy.31 The imaginary of science in China is thus contentious and multiple, mapped onto institutions that are a legacy of previous eras of political struggle and economic development. And as I argue, global and national scientific ambitions are tightly intertwined and often inseparable: In order to achieve national scientific ascendency, scientists must play in the game of global science.

In an attempt to strike a balance between openness and closedness, China in 2006 issued a new, fifteen-year plan for science, the Medium- to Long-Term Plan for the Development of Science and Technology (MLP).32 The sociologist Cong Cao, along with colleagues, has meticulously chronicled the changing landscape of science and technology policy since then. The MLP contains concrete technological goals in certain key areas and frontier technologies, including biotechnology, drug innovation and development, and information technology.33 A loftier techno-nationalist ambition also motivated these investments in science and technology: to see a Nobel Prize awarded to a Chinese scientist in China.34 This dream was realized in 2015 when Tu Youyou (someone we will discuss in chapter 3) received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for her work in traditional Chinese medicine (TCM), identifying artemisinin as a treatment for malaria. Tu’s prize was a symbolic triumph for Chinese science, yet it also reveals some of the discontent among Chinese scientists over their lack of inclusion in the global scientific community.

To achieve its goal of global recognition, the Chinese state has increased its research and development spending. It has also reformed its science and technology system, including its universities, national research labs, and funding mechanisms, which was seen as plagued by corruption, an overemphasis on publication metrics, and a lack of coordination among various governmental agencies.35 These reforms mimicked changes in US academia, which sought to increase innovation by promoting translational work and entrepreneurialism among academic scientists. Another important strategy has been to pursue overseas talent through the Thousand Talents Plan and similar schemes, particularly in the life sciences.36 Policymakers have been preoccupied with reversing China’s brain drain, occurring since the 1990s and 2000s, in hopes that returnee scientists can help kick-start innovation and contribute to China’s scientific rise. This emphasis on luring back returnee scientists—or “sea turtles,” as they are colloquially called—may be paying off. A 2020 analysis by Cao and colleagues found that research produced by returnee scientists in China was cited more frequently when compared with research by Chinese scientists who did not have overseas training or work experience. They found that the greater impact was attributed to returnee scientists’ social capital and collaborations with other scientists overseas. There is evidence, in other words, to suggest that openness toward global science has helped advance the more techno-nationalist ambitions of China’s policymakers.

But the pendulum can also swing the other way. Tai Ming Cheung, a political scientist, has pointed to the increasing securitization of science and technology within China and the dominance of military objectives in science funding and policymaking. 37^ { 3 7 } The securitization of science may be overriding other scientific imaginaries, such as openness to global science. Biomedical researchers have come under increased scrutiny, with fines levied on researchers,

such as those at BGI (a genomics and biotechnology powerhouse in Shenzhen), who share their data with collaborators abroad. These and other incidents have produced a chilling effect on international scientific collaboration. Yet, in the life sciences, an approach that overemphasizes security and techno-nationalism risks jeopardizing the health and well-being of people in China and beyond. As the Covid-19 pandemic powerfully demonstrated, the sharing of viral genomic data with the right people (through a database open to scientific collaborators overseas) at the right time (in the early days of the pandemic) is critical to developing lifesaving technologies (such as the mRNA vaccines created in the United States and Europe).38 As I discuss next, precision medicine is one site of biomedical innovation where ties with global science are particularly important and consequential. Furthermore, as I discuss in the conclusion to this book, the ongoing attack on and dismantling of science in the United States may accelerate China’s scientific ascendancy.

A Primer on Precision Medicine

Precision medicine is a site where the national and global imaginaries of science are contested, perhaps more so than in other scientific fields. But what is precision medicine? The term “precision medicine” refers to an approach to medicine that uses genomics and big data to better tailor the diagnosis and treatment of patients’ diseases. It promises that by knowing more about how individual genes, mutations, and other indicators are associated with health outcomes and responses to drugs, we will make medicine more effective and health care more efficient.39 Precision medicine was defined in the United States, appearing first in the National Research Council’s 2011 report titled Toward Precision Medicine: Building a Knowledge Network for Biomedical Research and a New Taxonomy of Disease.40 Four years later President Barack Obama’s administration launched a US Precision Medicine Initiative. Central to the US push for precision medicine is the All of Us Research Program, which plans to sequence and study the genomes of one million individuals to advance knowledge about various health outcomes.41

This coordinated program seeks longitudinal engagement with participants to cultivate trust, which (as I explain later in this book) stands in stark contrast to the experimentalist approaches in China that prioritize quantity and speed.

Critical scientists, bioethicists, and social scientists have pointed to some potential pitfalls of precision medicine. Barbara Prainsack, a political scientist and bioethicist, argues that precision medicine’s rhetoric of patient and individual empowerment can overpromise a form of personalized biomedical innovation accessible to all.42 The narrow focus on technical fixes to human health can also ignore the social and political factors that contribute to ill health. In addition, members of the Precision Medicine and Society Program at Columbia University have warned that an uncritical approach to precision medicine may end up hurting and disempowering patients in the patient-physician relationship as more power is ceded to physicians to interpret the increasing amount of data gathered on each patient.43 Concerns about precision medicine are not limited to those involved in privatized health care systems such as that in the United States, 44 but also in settings where there is universal and government-funded healthcare. The sociologist Shirley Hsiao-Li Sun warns that precision medicine’s interest in classifying people into medically distinct subpopulations risks reinforcing notions of race-medicine.45 In contexts where biological and geneticized understandings of identity may proliferate without checks from critical bioethicists and social scientists, precision medicine can have insidious consequences for social inequality.

More broadly, sociologists have used terms such as “anticipatory discourse” and “promissory science” to describe the types of hype so often present in claims of novelty and innovation in biomedicine.46 In this book, I try to strike a balance that recognizes not only that precision medicine differs from what has come before but also that it remains one in a series of attempts to reform and revolutionize medicine to make it more efficient, accurate, and individualized.47 In many cases, this involves having to take what I am told by my respondents with a grain of salt, even if they are the experts in the science. In precision medicine, as in so many other fields of biomedical research, the barriers to innovating and improving health

outcomes for patients most frequently take the form of mundane organizational, bureaucratic,and institutional hurdles, as well as the more vexing and complex social inequalities that cannotbe fixed by technology alone. At the same time, it should be emphasized that precision medicineis not yet fully realized. It is a promise and a label that have yet to be fully institutionalized. It isaspirational. Its goals may not (and probably will not) be fully met for many years to come.Many of its promises will go unfulfilled.48 For these and other reasons, the discourse onprecision medicine in China emphasizes basic science rather than clinical applications. We cansee this in how the term is translated: The preferred term in China is jing zhun yi xue(精准医学), which conveys the meaning of “the medical discipline of precision,” rather thanjing zhun yi liao (精准医疗), which implies “precise medical treatment.” There is, of course,also the question of positionality: As someone who studies the field of precision medicine, I havebenefited from the interest around it. But I hope, in this work, to take a critical look at precisionmedicine—to consider not just how to implement it but also whether we should fund precisionmedicine as an enterprise—and to recognize the hype and marketing involved whenentrepreneurs and scientists brand themselves as precision medicine researchers.

Despite the gap between hype and reality, governments and organizations around the world have hopped on the bandwagon of precision medicine. This is particularly true in China, where the term was incorporated in high-level national plans in 2016, including the Thirteenth Five-Year Plan and Healthy China 2030 (I discuss the specifics of how this term was introduced to China in chapter 2). Scientists and policymakers have imagined precision medicine, along with other forms of biomedical innovation, as aiding the state in addressing health issues associated with China’s aging population and helping Chinese scientists become leaders in global science. In the state’s language, precision medicine became a “strategic emerging industry” to invest in and a “key strategy” to address China’s societal ills.

Having committed itself to the idea of precision medicine, the Chinese state pledged a substantial amount of money to finance the initiation of precision medicine projects at various research organizations, including the Chinese Academy of Sciences in Beijing and BGI in

Shenzhen. Precision medicine has become a mass movement and bandwagon of sorts that has invited a wide range of actors to join the cause of revolutionizing mainstream medicine. But precision medicine is also a distinctly US project that required translation for the Chinese context. The scientists I spoke with played key roles in creating distinct versions of precision medicine with Chinese characteristics that resonated with local needs and realities.

In this book, I investigate three interrelated questions: (1) Why was precision medicine so popular among scientists and policymakers in China and globally in the 2010s? (2) How did proponents of precision medicine amass so many allies in the state and in the private sector in China? (3) What were the various imaginaries of precision medicine that different stakeholders subscribed to—and how legible were these ideas to other scientists?

First, the why. Precision medicine’s advocates consistently offer a technological explanation for the sudden popularity of precision medicine. In his remarks at the launch of the Precision Medicine Initiative on February 25, 2016, for example, President Obama cited the new capabilities made possible by advances in genomics, wearable electronics, electronic medical records, and computational power. Accordingly, then, “the time is right to unleash a new wave of advances in this area, in precision medicine, just like we did with genetics 25 years ago.”49 In other words, technological advances are important, but they are also rooted in previous attempts to revolutionize and disrupt medicine, and they depend on contingency, sequence, and timing in order to unleash their true potential. The popularization of precision medicine needs to be understood in relation to the proliferation of scientific frameworks and technological tools through transnational scientific networks. As I argue in chapter 1, specific types of policies, alliances, and arrangements enabled the global spread of precision medicine.

Answering the second question, how precision medicine gained its allies globally and in China, requires a closer look at the types of promises that precision medicine makes to different stakeholders. In other words, how did precision medicine become so compelling? This can be traced in the discourse and rhetoric. For example, in the Chinese context, precision medicine’s ability to develop more effective and targeted treatments for diseases like cancer has a specific appeal to policymakers worried about the demographic transition that will see the populationwide burden of cancer increase over time. Scientists I spoke with learned to speak the language of the state in order to justify their research and gain legitimacy. These promises of improved treatments are rhetorical strategies designed to enlist the support of pharmaceutical companies, scientists, clinicians, hospital administrators, insurers, and public health advocates. Furthermore, as I show, a wide range of scientific actors adopted such rhetoric to market their work. While these promises have yet to be fulfilled, they play a constitutive role in shaping the design of infrastructures for biomedical research and health care systems. How precision medicine is imagined plays a critical role in shaping its realization.

Third, I examine what precision medicine means to actors located in different positions in the emerging field. These meanings vary and are unstable due to the rapidly changing dynamics of a growing scientific field. It is nevertheless possible to identify different clusters of meanings among similarly oriented groups of scientists. These groupings are necessarily fuzzy and overlap, creating the possibility of linkages and alliances between different groups. Specifically in China, I identify two versions of precision medicine adapted to fit local concerns. First, some scientists are invested in what I call Chinese precision medicine. These more globally oriented scientists frame their work in precision medicine as the search for Chinese DNA, with the ultimate goal of curing Chinese diseases. A second group of scientists working in TCM is invested in what I call precision Chinese medicine. They believe in using the tools of precision medicine, including genomics and big data, to unlock the “black box” of TCM. These two imaginaries of precision medicine, while distinct, are both products of the hopes and dreams of scientists about China’s past—and its future.

I discuss at the end of the book how the logic of precision, as seen in the push for precision medicine, has seeped into other social domains in China. As I explain later, the rollout of precision medicine in China is tied up with promises to help address China’s demographic, economic, and geopolitical concerns. But beyond precision medicine, the virtue of being precise has found resonance in other policy domains. From the imaginary of precision public health that guided China’s pandemic response with tools such as the Health Code, smart cities, and precision poverty alleviation in China’s countryside, precision has become more than a buzzword. It now references an emerging form of politics that I call “precision governance,” a form of statecraft that is bound up with the promissory nature of emerging technologies and big data. Much like precision medicine’s claim to be able to intervene in disease at the right moment, precision governance imagines the state as being able to identify and address the needs of the people by amassing more and more data, which are then used to tailor policy responses to social needs.50 As with the promises of precision medicine, these technological aspirations are likely to disappoint and to be prone to failure.

Bringing Back Bourdieu

My approach to answering the three questions I posed in the previous section starts with taking Chinese scientists’ dreams of global science seriously. Although the Chinese Dream is impliedly singular, dreams of global science are multiple. Throughout this book, I critically examine the different symbolic meanings of science in China. Of course, such an analysis risks othering Chinese scientists as different, as well as essentializing what counts as Chinese and Western. But it Is nonetheless true that science does takes on new meanings in different national and institutional contexts. I find compelling the argument of the anthropologist Aihwa Ong that biomedical knowledge and technologies have become central to national projects of modernity in Asia, even as Asian scientists and policymakers see biomedical sciences as originating in “the West.51\mathrm { W e s t . } ^ { 5 1 } The question of how to innovate in a way that is distinctive and uniquely Chinese looms large in many discussions about science and technology in China. Another useful framing comes from the anthropologists Susan Greenhalgh and Li Zhang, who have described this phenomenon in China as “scientism,” or the belief in the ability of science to tackle a wide array of national social, political, and economic problems—a Swiss army knife of sorts.52 As noted earlier, this imaginary of science as something that can help save China is deeply held and

historically rooted. And yet, as I argue, these imaginaries about the value of science are deeply contested among Chinese scientists. The politics of science in China ultimately shapes what science actually becomes. Consequently, Chinese scientists operating in China must use different scripts to strategize, plan, and act.

In my account of China’s scientific ascendancy, I draw heavily on the field-theoretical analysis of science by the sociologist Pierre Bourdieu to develop tools for talking productively about the politics of science and the differences in scientific cultures.53 I find his theory useful in addressing the double bind that faces Chinese scientists: the need to conform to the norms and ideals of global science while also acting to advance the techno-nationalist ambitions and policy goals of the Chinese state.54 In my telling of the story, while Chinese scientists are undoubtedly subject to the dictates of the state and constrained in their ability to work freely with “pure” scientific intent, the scientists I encountered still aspired to something more. In his essay titled “The Specificity of the Scientific Field and the Social Conditions of the Progress of Reason,” Bourdieu portrays scientific fields as semiautonomous spaces where scientists struggle to accumulate the scientific capital in the form of scientific authority and recognition and are socialized through the cultivation of scientific habitus that orients them toward these sensibilities. As he writes, every “scientific ‘choice’ ” is “a political investment strategy, directed, objectively at least, towards maximization of strictly scientific profit, i.e. of potential recognition 55 by the agent’s competitor-peers.” Subsequently, scholars have used this framework to explore how scientists deploy these strategies in their work. A common theme of this research is how scientists differentiate among “do-able,” “interesting,” and “innovative” research questions.56 But despite the attempts of scientists to do good science and gain recognition from their peers, the work of all scientists is still subject, to some degree, to other social logics due to the semiautonomous character of the scientific field.

A strength of Bourdieu’s analysis of science lies in its focus on power, including how power is distributed in the scientific field.57 Other approaches in science and technology studies have used different vocabularies to richly describe how power operates within and beyond

science.58 More generally, the language of fields is useful in describing the dynamics of strategic action and contention in various social arenas, where dominant and subversive actors vie for the accumulation of capital.59 Critics of Bourdieu’s approach point to his overly strategic depiction of science and his neglect of the other social processes that shape science. ${ \bf 6 0 } _ { \mathrm { I n } }$ contrast, those examining the sociology of expertise have convincingly reasoned that thinking about the alliances between scientists and nonscientists provides a way out of this impasse. As the sociologist Gil Eyal has argued, any analysis of experts should be supplemented by an analysis of expertise, involving the wider networks of actors who possess varying forms of relevant knowledge and experience.61 Collectively, these insights tell us that applications of field theory in the study of scientific fields should be sensitive to social values and nontraditional actors in science.

I extend Bourdieu’s theory of scientific fields by looking at how scientists strategically mobilize various forms of global scientific capital and national scientific capital to create new scientific hybrids. Global scientific capital refers to scientists’ ability to publish in globally recognized scientific journals, participate in international conferences, and engage in transnational collaborations. Access to this global form of scientific capital often depends on the academic trajectories and careers of individual scientists—whether or not they have received training abroad, have established connections with overseas researchers, and have the ability to sustain costly and labor-intensive transnational scientific collaborations. This international circulation of scientists has created a shared habitus and sensibility of what counts as good science. Policymakers’ desire for global scientific capital is also high, as it aligns with the state’s aspirations to have its scientists published and recognized in global venues. In addition, universities reward the accumulation of global scientific capital through promotions and bonuses for publications in highly ranked international journals. Because of the structure of the global scientific field, many of the venues that are considered “global” are based in the United States, Europe, or other parts of the Global North.

In contrast, national scientific capital refers to the ability of scientists to tailor their research to address the temporal concerns of their society and polity. A national scientific field is less autonomous than its global counterpart. Its boundaries are more blurred, allowing nonscientific actors to influence the direction of scientific research. Conversely, scientists tend to become actors in adjacent social fields. For Bourdieu, the national form of scientific capital is tied to the social, political, and economic issues of the day within a given country. These issues usually change with shifting policy priorities, social change, and current events. More concretely, national scientific capital in the Chinese context refers to the ability of individual scientists to receive funding from the state, publish in Chinese journals, and present at conferences that draw researchers from around the country. While global scientific capital can be exchanged for national scientific capital, the exchange rate is less generous when converting national scientific capital into global scientific capital.

As a result of this need to address nation-specific interests, national scientific fields often enjoy less autonomy. In China, the alignment of science with these interests typically entails adopting slogans that align with edicts from the center. As discussed previously, the boundary between science and politics is particularly porous in the Chinese context. One could even argue that the national scientific field, in this case, is nested within the political field, where its autonomy is severely limited. The ability to accumulate national scientific capital therefore depends on the ability of scientists to align their work with the interests of the state. In order to accrue this form of capital, they learn to sell their research as relevant to those who control the purse strings of state scientific funding. In addition, the blurred boundary between science and market shapes the work of scientists. Scientists, in many cases, are market actors as well. In the Chinese context, the push to translate basic science to commercializable technologies that serve the interests of industry largely orients the work of scientists to the interests of the economic field.62 The close alignment of industry with the political field adds another way in which science is entangled with the state, mediated by market logics.63

Yet national and global forms of scientific capital coexist and are tightly linked. To successfully mobilize and exchange forms of capital, scientists must carefully choreograph a sequence of actions that allows them, for example, to use a specific capital in another field. They must often enlist allies from within the scientific field as well as in adjacent social fields, enabling them to form a broader network of actors who can help advance their research agenda. Scientific hybrids can arise when scientists concurrently mobilize global and national scientific capitals. Hybridity can also result from the intermixing of ideas, technologies, and things associated with different fields. This hybridity can confer certain advantages, such as the ability to speak to multiple audiences and gain support from a wider range of actors. Hybrid science, however, can be unrecognizable to scientific journals and funders beyond national borders because of how they amalgamate two different kinds of logic. The degree of hybridity can shape the ability of hybrid science to gain recognition in different venues. The global and national logics are at times incompatible and incommensurable, and the illegibility of resulting hybrids runs deep. The disjuncture between the values, norms, and priorities of these scientific hybrids and the broader global scientific field creates a form of illegibility that fuels a crisis of recognition. Scientific hybrids can then be seen as pseudoscience or not good science. In the specific case of China, the crisis of recognition arises when the scientific output and labor of Chinese scientists are devalued by their global peers. As a result of this hybridity, the scientific dreams of Chinese scientists are unrealized and denied.

Many of the geopolitical tensions that shape debates about cooperation between China and the West on science and technology can be understood through this lens of a crisis of recognition. First, the deep inequalities in the scientific field, as well as accusations of spying and investigations into foreign influence, do much to explain the increasing distrust between Chinese scientists and their global counterparts. The dismissal and devaluation of Chinese science by gatekeepers of global scientific capital has deepened this chasm. As Chinese scientists lose faith in the meritocratic character of global scientific institutions due to these experiences of exclusion, they will divert their energy and aspirations elsewhere. This growing rift will likely exacerbate existing challenges in getting scientists from different parts of the globe to come together and collaborate on pressing social and scientific problems.

Second, the hegemonic influence of US and European institutions on the global scientific field is more precarious than it may initially seem. Their dominance thus far can be attributed partly to the mechanisms of exclusion that make global scientific capital inaccessible to researchers from other countries. But as global science is intermixed with national science in unexpected and contradictory ways, this dominance may come into question. For example, US scientific institutions rely in large part on overseas talent, as well as access to international data and collaboration, to fuel innovation. By limiting visas for international scientists and subjecting them to unmerited security checks, the United States risks losing these critical opportunities for contact. Meanwhile, China is finding its own ways to disrupt Western hegemony in science. One strategy that China is pursuing involves establishing its own transnational scientific networks through the Belt and Road Initiative (BRI) which has resulted in the construction of as many as one hundred new laboratories and the convening of a number of international scientific conferences in BRI countries.64 These investments will likely take years to yield a return, but the groundwork, infrastructure, and relationships will undoubtedly bolster China’s position in global science.

Third, the dismissal of potentially good scientific ideas due to their origins in Chinese science can be deleterious to the interests of patients worldwide. In the United States, the rise of techno-nationalism, where geopolitical competition overrides other interests, is increasingly driving science and technology policy, creating more reluctance to engage in international scientific cooperation, particularly with China.65 This strain of techo-nationalism exists beyond the United States and is growing in Europe and elsewhere in the West, where using homegrown science rather than imported technologies is increasingly preferred.66 But conceivably, the cures, treatments, and tests developed within China and using Chinese data can benefit patients elsewhere. Indeed, many Chinese scientists I encountered had set grandiose goals in spite of the crisis of recognition. A common refrain that I heard from many of my interlocutors was the hope that their scientific research could benefit the rest of the world. Already, some drugs developed in China have outperformed their US counterparts: For example, ivonescimab, developed by the

Chinese biopharmaceutical company Akeso, is outperforming Merck’s Keytruda for non-small cell lung cancer.67 A version of global science that addresses the crisis of recognition by taking seriously the ideas and knowledge generated by Chinese scientists can perhaps benefit all of humanity.

Outline of the Book

In this book, I have drawn on a range of data and methods. For an elaboration of methods, access to China, and the impact of the Covid-19 pandemic, see the appendix. The documentary sources on which this text is based include news reports retrieved from databases, websites of key institutions affiliated with proponents of precision medicine, policy documents from government repositories, and entries in China’s lively science news and blogging industry. A close reading of these sources allowed me to chart changes in precision medicine over time and to identify the institutional arrangements that helped facilitate its rise. I also relied on a bibliometric analysis of scientific publications associated with precision medicine and other related movements in biomedicine. This, too, enabled me to compare the dynamics and networks of knowledge production in precision medicine and thereby identify essential topics, journals, and institutions involved in stabilizing these networks into fields.

To learn how Chinese researchers understood the field of precision medicine and their place in it, I conducted eighty interviews with biomedical researchers in Mainland China, Hong Kong, Macau, and Taiwan.68 These interviews were largely conducted in person between 2017 and 2020, with the last handful of interviews conducted remotely during the early months of the Covid-19 pandemic. These interviews, in particular, allowed me to capture the meanings that scientists in China ascribe to precision medicine. They also afforded me informal opportunities to observe scientists in their context, as I could observe my respondents’ places of work, which included hospitals, laboratories, universities, science parks, and companies. I supplemented these encounters with participant observations at conferences related to precision medicine in China

and in the United States, at which elite scientists formalized meanings ascribed to precision medicine.69 Last but hardly least, I incorporated social media data from websites such as Weibo and Zhihu to trace the public’s perceptions of precision medicine and, specifically, of a recent controversy over the use of genetic testing for identifying talents in children.70 While certain segments of the Chinese internet are heavily censored, discussions around topics related to science and technology are somewhat freer and more vibrant, as they veer somewhat away from conventional notions of the political.

This book is structured to provide readers with a view of how global science becomes entangled with national forms of science by tracing how scientists in different global and national settings accumulate and deploy scientific capital. In centering the agency and strategic action of scientists, I show how science becomes hybridized as a result of these transnational interactions, thereby challenging the global/national dichotomy.

In chapter 1, I make the case for Bourdieusian field theory and its associated concepts in analyzing the dynamics of global science. Drawing on recent advances in theories of global fields, I examine the processes of acquiring global scientific capital and national scientific capital. In this analysis, when global scientific capital becomes inaccessible to some on a basis other than merit—for example, status and social categories like academic nationality—inequality in science is the result. I argue that the various combinations of these different types of capital shape the content and conduct of science, as well as what scientists consider to be “good science.” This chapter draws primarily on two sources of data: (1) a social network analysis of bibliometrics of precision medicine publications and (2) a documentary analysis of precision medicine policy documents from around the world. Through these two types of analysis, I show the construction of the global scientific field of precision medicine.

In chapter 2, I discuss how some Chinese scientists, partly because of how they are denied global scientific capital, have pivoted to accumulating national scientific capital. The result is a scientific hybrid that I call Chinese precision medicine, a style of science that refracts global concerns around genetic diversity within China’s social and political system. Researchers who embrace Chinese precision medicine explicitly desire to document Chinese DNA in order to improve the “population quality.” Their work also addresses broader political and social anxieties around demographic changes in China: Specifically, China has an elderly population and the rates of chronic diseases like cancer are rising. Both scientists and policymakers associated with the Chinese state present precision medicine as a solution to these problems. I also discuss briefly the role of scientists on the Chinese periphery—Macau, Hong Kong, and Taiwan—in the making of Chinese precision medicine. In precision medicine, the “Chinesenesss” of these locales, as well as quality assurances provided by the reputation of their scientists, has allowed researchers to present data from Macau, Hong Kong, and Taiwan as representative of the mainland Chinese population.

In chapter 3, I tell the story of how researchers in TCM formed an alliance with those in precision medicine. This requires a little bit of backstory about the Chinese state’s drive to modernize TCM. Several schools of thought within TCM are competing over how to create a scientific form of TCM. Some, best represented by Nobel Prize laureate Tu Youyou, see TCM as a site for mining the Chinese materia medica for single active ingredients that can be subjected to pharmaceutical testing for efficacy. Others, primarily clinicians, prefer to work under the rubric of evidence-based medicine to standardize treatment protocols so that they can be tested in clinical trials. In contrast, those who advance another scientific hybrid that I call precision Chinese medicine have argued that the tools of Western medicine are inadequate to capture the complexity of TCM but that genomics and big-data analysis can potentially shed light on the individualized and multimodal therapeutic strategies used in TCM. For instance, precision Chinese medicine researchers have used studies of the gut microbiome to track the effects of herbal medicine, thereby claiming to unlock the “black box” of TCM. This hybridization has created new scientific forms that are legible to neither strict adherents of TCM nor global science.

For chapter 4, I use the curious case of genetic talent testing in China to examine how global and national forms of scientific capital are deployed in direct-to-consumer genetic testing.

Detached from the fields of production, scientific capital takes on new interpretations and contradictions as it interacts with various publics in new fields of reception. This is particularly true in a context where scientific capital can be portrayed as “foreign” and “imported”—and thereby trustworthy. Through an analysis of the marketing materials for these genetic testing companies and their social media posts, I show how Chinese parents come to make sense of the false claims pushed by Chinese scientists that genetic testing can help identify areas of “innate talent” in their children. I also argue that the inability of China’s scientific elites in precision medicine to rein in the proliferation of genetic talent tests is a result of the blurred boundary between science and market interests.

In my conclusion, I look at how the Covid-19 pandemic has renewed interest in the future of global science and how it will be impacted by China’s ties with the world, as well as the future of US-China relations. Political developments in both countries have exacerbated divides in global science. For instance, the heightened scrutiny of US scientists’ relationships with Chinese counterparts by US federal agencies and the restrictions on sharing biological materials and data by the Chinese governments have constrained the ability of scientists to collaborate internationally. The ongoing cuts in scientific funding to US universities also pose an existential threat to US dominance in global science. These developments have provided an opening for China to further assert itself as a scientific powerhouse. In addition, I draw on the study of precision medicine to understand the emerging paradigm of precision governance. Finally, I offer several lessons for the future, such as the pivotal role that transnational scientific networks play in sustaining biomedical innovation, the need to provide inclusivity at all levels of science, and the importance of confronting differences in science to understand the positionality of different scientists.
