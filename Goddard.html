INTRODUCTION

STACIE E. GODDARD, GEORGE LAWSON, AND OLE JACOB SENDING

On November 19, 2023, as we were writing this introductory essay, Argentinians elected Javier Milei, a self-declared “anarcho-capitalist,” as their president; three days later, Geert Wilders, a more established far-right figure, won the Dutch elections. Milei and Wilders join a number of current or former leaders—Viktor Orbán, Giorgi Miloni, Jair Bolsonaro, and Donald Trump amongst them—whose rhetoric, performances, and practices constitute a global movement of the “radical right” (see Drolet, this volume). We are also writing six weeks into a desperate conflict between Israelis and Palestinians in Gaza and the West Bank, some 18 months into the vicious war that has followed the Russian invasion of Ukraine and several years into devastating internationalized civil wars in Yemen, Sudan, Libya, Myanmar, Syria, and elsewhere. In Latin America and the Caribbean, large numbers of migrants go through nightmarish experiences in attempting to move within and across borders. Earlier this month, the Earth’s temperature briefly rose above the threshold that scientists have warned could have catastrophic, irreversible impacts on the planet. Over the past few years, we have experienced a shattering pandemic that has questioned the very basis on which people live and interact. Technologies of various kinds appear to be transforming the nature of everyday life and, in the process, helping to usher in a new form of twenty-first-century capitalism. Protest movements, many of them transnational in orientation and organization, challenge these developments, leading some to argue that we are living in a “new age of revolution” (see Selbin, this volume). No wonder this feels like a world in crisis.

An avalanche of work is concerned with analyzing the sources and consequences of this “polycrisis” (Morin and Kern 1999; Tooze 2022). For several reasons, International Political Sociology (IPS) is particularly well placed to contribute to these debates. First, IPS scholarship treats world politics as a social space of meaning-making, boundary-drawing, and contestation. Rather than assuming, for example, that actors have fixed identities and capacities, it asks questions about how these actors are constituted and change over time. A “crisis,” in this view, is less the result of shifts in the “objective” character of world politics than it is the result of changing interpretations and experiences of events. Second, IPS scholarship has always been open to the idea that this is a “world on the move.” Rather than treat world politics as a static arena made up of the interactions of states that possess predetermined interests, IPS scholarship examines the flows that lie behind the dynamics of emergence, reproduction, and contestation. As such, IPS is concerned with the push-and-pull dynamics that sustain processes of ordering, struggle, and crisis. Third, if IPS is keenly interested in elite politics and performances, it is also concerned with the “everyday” ways in which people around the world make sense of, and challenge, global crises. As a result, it challenges taken-for-granted assumptions about what “matters” in world politics, asking why some aspects of crises are well documented and others more subterranean. In the process, IPS scholarship opens up distinct understandings of power, actor-hood, and agency. Finally, IPS challenges the substantive issue areas through which disciplinary International Relations (IR) is organized. Although IPS scholarship contributes extensively to debates about security, political economy, and global governance, it is also home to novel sites of enquiry, including those within which understandings of crisis are often most acutely experienced: mobilities and borders, performances and practices, logistics and surveillance. Often, IPS is concerned with the “transversal,” or intersecting, dimensions of these dynamics (Basaran et al. 2016).

No wonder, then, that over the past two decades or so, IPS has become a major sub-field within IR. The IPS section of the International Studies Association (ISA), established in 2006, is one of the largest in the association. IPS is also the subject of a major journal published under the ISA umbrella, a prominent book series, and a number of research groups. By any measure, IPS is a success story. Given this, why is there a need for a handbook of IPS? At the most basic level, a stock-taking exercise of a burgeoning, but still emerging, field can make a significant contribution, providing an overview of the field and its major work and offering a first port of call for curious students and scholars. This Handbook joins efforts to explore and map the multiple contributions of IPS research (e.g. Huysmans and Nogueira 2016; Guillaume and Bilgin 2017; Lisle, Squire, and Doty 2017; Grayson and Grove 2022). However, this Handbook also seeks to present and discuss IPS with something of a twist. The space occupied by IPS is, potentially, vast—in principle at least, the domain of enquiry opened up by sociological approaches to world politics is huge. If that presents one difficulty for a handbook of this kind, a second arises from the fact that the features of IPS noted in the previous paragraph bring it into conversation with a wide range of research, from feminism to political geography. Given this open canvass, it is little surprise that, as it emerged, IPS became oriented around a “hardcore” set of assumptions (e.g. self-consciously critical work) and issue areas (e.g. borders and migration). As we outline below, this take on IPS has been institutionalized in many of its practices, including most of its collective surveys and the house journal of the sub-field: International Political Sociology. For us, this has produced something of a mismatch between the (somewhat narrow) institutional terrain of IPS and its (extremely wide) intellectual agenda.

In recent years, this mismatch has begun to recede. As we discuss in the following sections, IPS research is increasingly free-range in terms of the questions it asks, the topics it engages, and the approaches it takes. There is a range of topics being published in the journal International Political Sociology and elsewhere that suggest an engagement with sociological theories beyond those that have historically marked the core identity of the sub-field. Examples include work drawing on Randall Collins’ (2008) micro-sociological approach that engages the practice theoretical approaches that have been central to IPS scholarship over the past decade or so (Bramsen 2023, comp. Adler and Pouliot 2011; Bigo 2011; Adler-Nissen 2016). Forms of network analysis are increasingly being used to empirically investigate relational dynamics, another key feature of IPS scholarship (Ellersgard et al. 2017; Montgomery, this volume). In our own scholarship, we have found value in drawing on insights from a broad array of political sociological approaches, such as Charles Tilly’s work, both single or co-authored, on contentious politics (e.g. Lawson 2019) and repertoires (e.g. Goddard, MacDonald, and Nexon 2019), and Ann Swidler’s understanding of culture as a means to theorize agency and heteronomy (e.g. Sending 2016). We join this work, and other related enterprises (e.g. Guillaume and Bilgin 2017), which have sought both to underline the contribution of IPS as currently constituted and sought to extend its reach. Alongside this burgeoning scholarship, we see engagement with as wide as possible a range of political sociological work as central to the vitality of IPS going forwards. Our view is that there is a danger that an internally driven enterprise will lose some of its intellectual vivacity if its agenda is not sustained—and challenged—by diverse sources, participants, and approaches. This Handbook therefore includes work that is currently central to the sub-field alongside contributions that push at its boundaries.

Our aim with this Handbook, therefore, is to do more than present the state of the art within IPS. Rather, in keeping with its sociological underpinnings, we see IPS as a “relational formation,” by which we mean a field of enquiry that is constituted, reproduced, and contested through a dialogue between a diverse range of approaches rooted in political sociology and applied to “the international.” We do not define IPS in a “substantialist” way, designating the field with a certain disposition, set of properties, or research agenda. To the contrary, we want to challenge IPS to keep being attuned to a diverse set of sociological theories and approaches to further support its multiple agendas. In this sense, while the heart of IPS remains a highly productive, self-consciously “critical” mode of enquiry that has generated important insights into mobilities, migration, borders, and more, there are emerging signs of an auxiliary belt of approaches, themes, concepts, and research strategies that are also contributing in significant ways to the sub-discipline. Taking both together, IPS can be conceptualized as a social space where sites of enquiry—urban spaces, infrastructural hubs, social movements, empires, bordering practices, forms of international administration, and more—are produced through socio-political processes rather than received ex ante. This is a wide-angle lens on IPS. And it is one that we think is rich in its possibilities.

This approach to, and ambition for, IPS differentiates our volume from other surveys of the field in two main ways.

First, we expand the field of IPS to include work that is not always seen as a central part of IPS (such as Marxian-inspired historical sociology) and political sociological scholarship that seeks to contribute to mainstream debates (such as social network analysis). When the sub-field emerged, it made sense for IPS to be a self-consciously critical enterprise. A central part of the raison d’être of IPS was to provide an intellectual and institutional space through which to critique dominant orientations in IR scholarship and cultivate new research agendas. Given the success of IPS and, we would argue, the somewhat more open, global discipline that exists today, we think that treating IPS only as a site of critical inquiry obscures both the wider significance of political sociology to IR and the central role that IPS can play in developing this agenda. Alongside cognate approaches, from feminism to post-structural work, IPS has helped to foster a number of crucial questions: what constitutes power and how power is wielded, organized, and legitimated; what actors beyond elites “matter” in world politics, and how everyday IR is practiced; what sites of enquiry should be considered to be part of IR and what innovative procedures can be used to study them; how we can rethink major issue areas in IR—war, security, diplomacy—through IPS reasoning and procedures; how race, class, gender, and other systems of stratification influence the formation of world orders; how, where, and when bordering practices emerge, become routinized and challenged, and more. These questions inform—and are informed by—an array of political sociological approaches. At the same time, wrestling with the central questions of IPS does not, it seems to us, require a specific mode of theorizing, a particular ontology or epistemology, or a single set of procedures. As such, this Handbook includes a wider range of political sociological approaches than is commonly assumed to be part of IPS. We do not include these approaches out of any desire to uproot IPS from its origins, police the sub-field, or shift the approach in radically new directions. Rather, we do so simply to ask questions of IPS scholarship about how broad it can be and where the edges of the approach reside—after all, as a relational formation, IPS is always being produced, reproduced, and contested.

Second, we hope to demonstrate the breadth and depth of the substantive contributions IPS makes to IR. With this goal in mind, the opening chapters in each section survey the field and discuss how political sociological approaches generate specific insights and avenues for future research. The idea is that encouraging scholars well versed in IPS to explicitly engage a wide variety of viewpoints will clarify where their arguments reside, converge, and differ. To that end, these lead chapters are co-authored, often between scholars with different positionalities within and to IPS, with a view to enabling the critical conversations that we see as the lifeblood of IPS as an intellectual space. Each section includes chapters from those already committed to IPS, plus those who operate at something of a remove from it, again with the intention of teasing out the edges and boundaries of IPS as a field of enquiry. This produces an introduction to IPS that, we think, works both for those already committed to the approach and those who are not—currently—its main audience. Our aim is to push IPS as an intellectual project, including toward approaches and issue areas that may be less familiar to its primary constituencies. We know that this approach will not be to everyone’s taste. But, at the very least, we hope that it stimulates debate and, as a result, helps to energize IPS scholarship.

The following, second, section of the introduction briefly outlines the emergence of IPS as a sub-field and discusses how the international, the political, and the sociological are defined and relate to each another in IPS scholarship. The third section identifies three meta-commitments that, we think, lie at the heart of IPS scholarship: relationalism, intersubjectivity, and historicism. We discuss the logics and contours of these commitments and identify how they constitute the porous boundaries of IPS. The fourth section explores the operationalization of these meta-commitments to “social stuff”: order, power, actors and agency, and resistance. A brief conclusion summarizes our arguments.

THE TWO WORLDS OF IPS

While IPS as a distinct field of enquiry is relatively recent, sociological insights have always been present in the study of IR (Lawson and Shilliam 2010). Indeed, it could be argued that there is relatively little IR theory that stands outside the influence of sociological approaches. The most prominent Realist of the past half century, Kenneth Waltz (1979: esp. ch. 6), borrowed extensively from Emile Durkheim (via Parsons) in generating his conception of international anarchy (Goddard and Nexon 2005). Robert Gilpin (1981), too, engaged closely with sociological approaches, in his case world-systems analysis, in his work on hegemonic stability. Both constructivism and the English School also draw heavily on sociological resources, primarily symbolic interactionism and structuration theory for the former, functionalism and Habermasian social theory for the latter. Max Weber’s notion of rationalization underpins a number of IR theories in ways that go well beyond his identification as a political realist (Barkawi 1998; Jackson 2002; Williams 2004). W.E.B. Du Bois was influential in the development of early IR theory, even if this influence was later obscured (Vitalis 2016). The wide-ranging impact of Marxian-inspired approaches is well established (Teschke 2008). Further encounters between sociology and IR include the overt use of sociological classics by IR scholars, such as Erving Goffman’s (1963) work on stigma, Niklas Luhmann’s (1997) version of systems theory, and John Meyer et al.’s (1997) work on ‘world society’; associations mediated through other approaches and disciplines, such as Michael Mann’s (1986) study of the sources of social power in world history; and the use of concepts that, in their contemporary enunciations, have been previously developed by social theorists, such as “rationality,” “agency,” “class,” “gender,” and “society.”

Despite this rich provenance, for a long time, sociological influences on IR were relatively neglected. And at times, the discipline moved formally away from sociology. As Stefano Guzzini (2017: 368) notes, by the 1980s the study of world politics had become narrow, engaged in a “purely systemic analysis of inter-state politics—and anchored its theoretical underpinnings in a form of utilitarianism: states as value-maximizing agents facing each other in a strategic game of cooperation or conflict under anarchy”. Sociology resurfaced explicitly as a means of problematizing the core assumptions of mainstream IR theory, especially the assumptions underlying the so-called “interparadigm debate” (Bigo and Walker 2007). This was the context within which IPS emerged, albeit (we would argue) as a space with two main currents: first, an explicitly “critical” stream, housed mainly in Europe, which drew largely on post-structural resources (e.g. Bartelson 1995; Hansen 1997; Huysmans 1998; Neumann 1996); and second, a “constructivist” stream, based mainly in North America, which drew on symbolic interactionism and related approaches (e.g. Adler and Barnett 1998; Finnemore 1996; Katzenstein 1996). Despite the largely shared meta-theoretical commitments that lay behind both approaches (Price and Reus-Smit 1998), over time these “two worlds” of IPS diverged and hardened. As they did so, IPS became identified primarily with the critical, European stream of research.

There was nothing inevitable about this development, however. Both forms of theorizing emerged from meta-theoretical reflections about the ontic underpinnings of world politics and the epistemic conditions for understanding it. For example, in the 1980s, there was considerable affinity between the two streams in terms of their critique of rationalist approaches and the promise of understanding IR as a social space. Richard Ashley (1984) argued that conventional IR approaches reduced politics to technique; for Ashley, the state should be understood—with Foucault—as an effect of boundary-drawing and disciplining practices. James Der Derian (1987) claimed that diplomacy was a distinctly social practice of mediation that defined relations between sovereigns. Alexander Wendt (1987) demonstrated the importance of understanding agency and structure as socially produced rather than predetermined through material capabilities. Similarly, Fritz Kratochwil (1989) explored the ways in which legal scholarship could be used to rethink the ways in which rules and norms structured forms of reasoning. Martha Finnemore (1996) showed how insights from sociological institutionalism could be marshalled to re-conceptualize the concept of “state interest,” demonstrating how interests evolved in light of changes in international norms.

The growing distinction between the “two worlds” of IPS, therefore, had more to do with institutional position than with meta-theoretical commitments. North American scholarship had a far greater proximity to rationalist work and was located in an ecology in which IR was a (somewhat marginal) branch of political science. This often generated a defensive posture from those seeking to apply sociological insights to IR. European IR operated in a more free-range space, both intellectually and institutionally—as such, it took up a more assertive stance. Indeed, during this period, European IR sought to formally generate a distance from North American currents, as in the development of the European Journal of International Relations, the reconvening of the English School as a “non-American” theoretical tradition (Buzan 2001), and similar developments. Over time, these differences became more pronounced. Perhaps most notably, norms came to dominate the second, “conventional” stream of research. In Kratochwil’s rendering, norms were associated with practical and legal reasoning, and there was explicit emphasis on the coexistence or conflict between norms. This conceptualization of norms was not necessarily at odds with post-structural contributions about, for example, the evolving features of sovereignty (Bartelson 1995) or the need to rethink the concept of security (Wæver 1995; Hansen 1997). However, the research that came to dominate North American sociological enquiry from the late 1990s drew on a particular interpretation of norms as specifying the intersubjectively shared character of world politics. Here, norms were seen as standards for appropriate behavior, and political change (such as changes in states’ interests) was explained by new norms being internalized by actors, thereby changing their interests (Jepperson, Wendt, and Katzenstein 1996; Adler 1997; cf. Sending 2016). Scholars associated with this form of constructivism aimed to show how a focus on norms could fill in the variation left unexplained by rationalist approaches (e.g. Finnemore 1996; Legro 1996; Tannenwald 1999; Wendt 1999, 142). In particular, debates over which “logics of action”—consequences, appropriateness, and deliberation—dominated world politics became a central axis of research (March and Olsen 1998; Risse 2000; Zürn and Checkel 2005).

Some argued that this attempt to reconcile constructivism and rationalist scholarship was problematic, seeing it as a taming of the intellectual and political possibilities that emerged from the use of political sociology within IR. These concerns included the conceptualization of norms by constructivist work (Epstein 2011) and the failure to take language sufficiently seriously in understanding the constitution of political subjects (Guillaume 2002). For others, critique came from what was seen as a failure to explore the full implications of the meta-theoretical commitments of the social construction of knowledge and the social construction of reality (cf. Guzzini 2000), particularly in terms of unearthing the political stakes in scholarly work (C.A.S.E. collective 2006), the construction of the international as a distinct political space (Jabri 2014), and the relational constitution of actor-hood (Jackson and Nexon 1999).

These critiques formed a central role in the development of a self-consciously “IPS” strain of research, which looked to pursue political sociological commitments in order to rethink core tenets of the building blocks of IR as a discipline: power, the state, sovereignty, the international, and more. The Charter Document of IPS as a section within the ISA, dating from 2007, outlines the ways in which IPS is intended to “foster innovative epistemological, methodological, analytic and theoretical perspectives on aspects of international studies less central to state-centered, economic, diplomatic and other traditional approaches.” For its part, the inaugural editors of International Political Sociology, which published its first issue in 2007, Didier Bigo and RBJ Walker (2007: 2–5), noted that: “thinking sociologically about politics and international relations” will allow a more “reflexive scholarship”; “a renewed emphasis on the study of practices”; and an emphasis on “the way knowledge, including empirical data, is constructed.” Critiquing a discipline that is “recognizable as a product of specifically American and to some extent British traditions of analysis, in ways that raise suspicions about privilege, ethnocentricism, and hegemony,” Bigo and Walker (2007: 1) sought to develop IPS through extensive dialogue with social theory.

These statements are broad and inclusive. And IPS scholarship has been true to its word. Of articles published in the journal International Political Sociology, the most common topics include issue areas that are rarely found in other IR journals: race, necropolitics, gentrification, coloniality, climate change, crime, anthropocene, microfinance, collective memory, and more. The journal has been highly innovative methodologically, particularly around the development of ethnographic procedures and visual forms of research, and pioneering in its attentiveness to novel sites of enquiry. The IPS Best Book Prize has been awarded to a wide range of authors representing diverse sub-fields (from political geography to historical sociology) and substantive issue areas (from tourism to queer politics). All of the teams who have edited the journal highlight its transdisciplinary intent, its commitment to intellectual pluralism, its desire to take IPS beyond the Anglosphere and Euro-Atlantic worlds, and its commitment to diverse scholarly concerns (Bigo and Walker 2007; Huysmans and Nogueira 2012; Lisle, Squire, and Doty 2017; Grayson and Grove 2022). In each of their statements, and in other milestone publications (e.g. Guillaume and Bilgin 2017), IPS is conceived as a field of enquiry that is fluid rather than closed, dynamic rather than static, and emergent rather than prescribed. As Kyle Grayson and Nicole Sunday Grove (2022: 3) write, IPS should not be a space that reflects the “boutique interests of an exclusive group of scholars.” Rather, scholars should expect IPS to “morph and change so that it reflects the scholarship that pushes at its own disciplinary boundaries.”

IPS, therefore, demonstrates considerable diversity in terms of the theoretical traditions it draws upon and the substantive issue areas it addresses, especially when compared to more conventional IR sub-fields. That said, like any scholarly endeavor, IPS scholarship contains hegemonic influences. At times, this is intentional. All of the major statements about IPS make clear its commitment to self-consciously critical, reflexive research. And, when identifying the intellectual roots of IPS, Guillaume and Bilgin (2017: 1) note that the field is “principally identified with the works of Michel Foucault and Pierre Bourdieu.” This is borne out by articles published in International Political Sociology, which show that Foucault is the scholar featured most frequently in reference lists in 12 out of the 17 years of the journal’s operation. Bourdieu is the third most prominently cited figure. In the Routledge Handbook of International Political Sociology, the two most cited figures are Bourdieu and Foucault. Of three forums in International Political Sociology that focus on a specific scholar, Foucault is featured in two of them; the journal’s one special issue was on the work of Bourdieu. At other times, these dynamics are unintentional. For example, despite the publication of calls for papers and abstracts in multiple languages, the acceptance of submissions in some non-English languages, and an IPS Global South Pre-Submission Scheme, two-thirds of the authors who have published in International Political Sociology are based in Europe and one-third have their institutional home in the United Kingdom. Only 10 per cent of articles published in the journal are from scholars with an institutional affiliation outside Europe, North America, and Oceania, figures that are almost identical to those in the Routledge Handbook of International Political Sociology. Despite high-profile attempts to extend the reach of IPS beyond the Euro-Atlantic world, the approach—as with others in IR—is embedded within a field of practice that remains structured around and through these geographies, albeit (in the case of IPS) with a center of gravity residing in Europe rather than North America.

Since its emergence as an intellectual project in the 1980s and its institutional formalization in the 2000s, therefore, IPS has developed into an explicitly critical approach, centered in Europe, and oriented around a particular ethos and set of figures. These operating parameters are being increasingly contested by the emergence of novel insights, issue areas, and methodologies. We hope that this handbook contributes to this conversation about the shape and edges of IPS. As discussed above, the “two worlds” of sociologically informed IR have become distanced over the past generation. This is exemplified by work on practices. Initially, IPS work on practices was linked to the “linguistic” turn and research on discourse (e.g. Neumann 2002). As it developed, it focused more explicitly on the concept of practice generated by Bourdieu (e.g. Pouliot 2008; Bigo 2011; Adler-Nissen 2011; Adler and Pouliot 2011). At times (e.g. Schatzki, Knorr Cetina, and von Savigny 2001), advocates drew from a wider range of theoretical perspectives, including structuration theory (e.g. Dessler 1989), ethnomethodology (e.g. Nair 2021), and pragmatism (e.g. Pratt 2020). If some of this work pitched itself as contributing to what was, at least outside North America, considered to be mainstream IR scholarship (e.g. Adler and Pouliot 2011), at other times, it was more self-consciously critical (e.g. Doty 1997). Either way, work on practices show how similar intellectual points of origin can be, and often have been, mobilized in different ways for different purposes.

Our view is that this does not have to be the case, and it may not be a particularly productive as a way of conceiving IPS. At the very least, the question of whether there are, or should be, “two worlds” of IPS is something we think it is worth asking afresh. As such, this handbook explores what might be gained from seeing IPS as a myriad of perspectives within a transnational field of politically sociological inquiry into the changing nature of world politics—and its current sense of conjunctural crisis.

“The international”

IPS scholars broadly agree that world politics encompasses the transboundary encounters that serve as points of interaction across political borders, whether these are represented by states, regions, empires, urban formations, or frontiers and whether these encounters take the form of emulation, exchange, coercion, or comparable processes. A core feature of IPS, therefore, is its critique of “methodological nationalism”—the view that the boundaries of social relations map directly onto the boundaries of the nation-state and that nation-states are the natural units of scholarly analysis (Wimmer and Schiller 2002). IPS scholarship argues that there is no distinct “international system” and no pre-determined primary unit within it. Rather, the boundaries of the international are historically contingent and subject to dynamics of emergence, reproduction, and contestation (e.g. Walker 1993; Bartelson 2023). At the same time, IPS scholarship has sought to highlight the ways in which important, yet often overlooked, dimensions of world politics (such as racism, colonialism, sexism, and other systems of stratification) have engendered regimes of exploitation, inequality, and debasement. By “decentering” power away from the modern nation-state and by recentering enquiry on actors (including “everyday” actors) who contest forms of domination; by widening issue areas to include economic, ecological, and humanitarian regimes; and by explicitly confronting the co-implication of normative and scholarly commitments (e.g. Huysman and Nogueira 2016: 302; Walker 1993, 1997; Bigo and Walker 2007; Guzzini 2005), IPS has been conceived as an intellectual sign around which a range of research agendas can be articulated.

To work within IPS is thus to embrace the argument that the boundaries of the international are assembled, contested, and historically contingent. It also involves being attentive to the effects of invoking or claiming the international as a distinct social space that is qualitatively different from domestic societies. If IPS is, as noted above, concerned with the “transversal relations” that challenge the boundaries of political entities (Basaran et al. 2016), then IPS scholarship shares a concern with how these boundaries are created, how they are ordered, and under what conditions they are challenged.

“The political”

IPS therefore focuses on the study of power as it attends to the dynamics of transboundary order-making and contestation. As a result, all IPS scholarship is concerned with broadening existing understandings of power in IR, which have traditionally been associated with the distribution of resources and capabilities within the international system. Most mainstream approaches ally this focus on capabilities with a Weberian (1978 [1922]: 278)-inspired definition of power (macht): “the probability that an actor within a social relationship will be in a position to carry out his will despite resistance, regardless of the basis on which this probability rests.” In short: those with greater resources and capabilities are able to translate their advantages into favorable outcomes. In contrast to this understanding, IPS scholarship maintains that power is a multifaceted phenomenon: it can be seen in the ways in which language constitutes interests (e.g. Epstein 2008) and in how forums of global governance exert “capillary” power in which subjects are disciplined without the overt deployment of either capabilities or “will” (e.g. Sending and Neumann 2006). In part, such notions of power also draw on Weber (1978 [1922]: 278), who saw power as closely bound up with domination (herrschaft): “the probability that a command within a given specific context will be obeyed by a given group of persons.” Power, for Weber, was both a capacity to achieve goals through the organization and mobilization of resources and a means by which to set the rules of the game, ensure that certain issues do not reach agendas, or prevent others from articulating their interests (also see Lukes 2005). In this sense, power is both the capacity to directly affect others and the capacity to make decisions appear to be “natural” or “right.”

As all international orders are made up of multiple political units, establishing order is an enduring point of tension in world politics. This makes contestation, whether through revolutions, social movements, or everyday protest endemic features of IR. This generates questions about how power is organized, who claims power, and how power is legitimized. It also stimulates investigation of changes in time and space into what constitutes power—in other words, how existing forms of power are challenged, reconfigured, and sometimes overturned. This ranges from explorations of corporeal power (Axster and Danewid, this volume) that operate on and through individual actors via research on the productive power of discourses (Alberts, this volume), to discussions of the symbolic power (Hoffmann, this volume), which resides in images, narratives, and signs (Callahan, this volume).

The “sociological”

As Bigo and Walker (2007: 2) make clear, IPS seeks to expand IR’s interdisciplinarity by bringing it into explicit dialogue with sociology: “We are especially conscious of the relative absence of sociological traditions in the most influential analyses of international relations.” To approach IR through the lens of IPS is to think of IR as constituting a social space constituted by actors, their interactions, and their patterning in the form of social structures. By bringing sociology formally into the study of world politics, IPS can “frame more precisely the debates on the themes concerning social practices, their relations to norms and institutions…., the emergence of transnational networks …, and the transformation of domination and inequality when domination emancipates from the territorial weberian state and the westphalian era” (ISA–IPS Section, n.d.).

Just as IR stands to benefit from a deeper recognition of sociological traditions, IPS stresses that sociological traditions, too, benefit from engagement with IR scholarship (e.g. Bigo and Walker, 2007: 1–2). At times, by importing the “great theorists” of sociology into IR, scholars have suggested, perhaps unintentionally, that IR is the product of sociological inquiry rather than productive of it. This does insufficient justice to the ways in which world politics is a significant social space in and of itself, one that has often been overlooked by disciplinary sociology (Rosenberg 2006, 2016; Rosenberg and Kurki 2021). Much of the contemporary discipline of sociology is concerned with “internal” dynamics that take place within nation-states (Go and Lawson 2017). Challenging this assumption of “internalism” and its corollary, “methodological nationalism,” is a central task of IPS.

Over the past decade, a range of sociological approaches have been imported into IR, some of which set out to challenge internalism and methodological nationalism. Social network approaches, inspired by the work of George Simmel, Norbert Elias, and John Padgett, have used this approach to study issue areas ranging from human rights to financial systems (e.g. Montgomery, this volume; Hafner-Burton, Kahler, and Montgomery 2009). Pragmatists drawing on the work of John Dewey have analyzed topics from military basing to the end of the gold standard (e.g. Pratt 2020; Jabko and

Schmidt 2021; Schmidt 2014). Others have drawn extensively from the work of Charles Tilly and his collaborators on contentious politics, using their insights to examine revolutions (Lawson 2019), great power politics (Goddard, MacDonald, and Nexon 2019), and more. Work in historical IR has shown how world orders are profoundly social, resting on historically contingent processes and interactions in which dynamics of transboundary order-making and contestation take center stage. Such a view opens up a historical canvass that extends well beyond the contemporary global order, including to periods with quite different forms of social organization (Buzan and Lawson 2015; Phillips and Sharman 2015; Phillips 2022).

If the influence of sociology on IR is mapped in this way, it is clear that the relationship between sociology and IR is more significant and multifaceted than is often taken to be the case, including by those working within IPS. In our view, IPS would be well served by taking part in an ongoing dialogue with these approaches. Taking this ecumenical approach, we argue, is the most productive way of demonstrating the intellectual promise of IPS, while simultaneously extending its agenda.

META-FOUNDATIONS

This volume, therefore, sees IPS as concerned with any social relations, processes, and mechanisms that operate or are deployed politically across borders. This is a wide remit and necessarily raises the question of whether there is much, or any, IR theory that stands outside IPS. If we are going to relax the assumption of IPS as a self-consciously critical sub-field premised on a specific ontology and set of epistemological commitments and organized around a particular set of theorists and issue areas, then we need to find other ways of connecting work that is identified as IPS. With that in mind, we propose that “doing IPS” is based on three foundational commitments: relationalism, intersubjectivity, and historicism. These are broad commitments, and they are typically interpreted and put to work in different ways, which in turn shapes substantive engagements, as well as the forms of critique that are characteristic of IPS.

Relationalism

As Julian Go and Daniel Nexon (this volume) write in their lead chapter, “relationalism holds that, as the name implies, relations make up social and political life. It emphasizes the causal and constitutive role played by the web of interactions, transactions, and relationships that connect actors and other social sites.” Relationalism makes two intertwined claims: first, that relations are the ontological substrata of the social world; and second, that causal processes are embedded within interactions, shaping actors, action, and outcomes.

The counterpoint to relationalism is “substantialism.” Substantialist approaches begin with fixed entities, usually either structures (such as the international system) or agents (such as states). Within this grouping are “holists,” scholars that start “with an analytical primitive of the social system or structure, which is imbued with essential and enduring characteristics” (Go and Nexon, this volume). Structural realists, for example, locate the causes of state behavior and outcomes in an anarchic structure and the distribution of power (Waltz 1979; Parent forthcoming). For their part, world-systems theorists point to the determinate properties of global capitalist accumulation (e.g. Agnew 2021). Constructivists expand structures to include ideational factors but also rely on fixed structural entities, most notably norms (e.g. Deitelhoff and Zimmerman 2020; Wiener 2018). In contrast, “individualist” theories maintain that behavior and outcomes are best explained in terms of individual attributes and the “preferences, perceptions, or beliefs” of specific actors. These actors are often individual human beings, as in rational-choice scholarship, which builds theories premised on the assumption of individuals as utility-maximizing actors (e.g. Fearon 2005) or psychological theories, which highlight individual perceptions and cognition as the root causes of behavior and outcomes (see e.g. Kertzer and Tingley 2018; McDermott 2019; Saunders 2011). In other cases, collectives are treated as single actors, be they states, empires, firms, or classes.

Relationalism, in contrast, begins with the proposition that entities are not fixed but made up of relations of various kinds and intensities. It is thus the task of analysts “to grasp the process through which such relations are aggregated and used to stabilize and reify some other relations as making up an entity or thing” (Sending, Pouliot, and Neumann 2015: 7). In the short run, actors can appear as fixed entities, with definable interests and intentions. But in the medium and long term, actors emerge, change, and sometimes disappear by virtue of the relations that produce them (Jackson and Nexon 1999; Padgett and Powell 2012). This insight may seem intuitive when we are talking about collective actors—after all, we can plausibly think of states, empires, international organizations, and firms as collections of relations. But in its strongest form, relationalism also suggests that individuals are inseparable from the social relations that constitute them. In this sense, actors do not “have” identities and interests; rather, these are shaped and reshaped through social interactions. At the same time, relations give rise to structures: anarchical and hierarchical orders, normative and discursive formations, systems of racism and patriarchy, and more. Neither agents nor structures are separable from social interactions (see Emirbayer 1997; Go and Krause 2016; Tilly 1998).

This understanding opens up space for exploring a fundamentally social view of agency. If all social action takes place within relatively fixed configurations of social ties, there is considerable variation in terms of whether agency is seen as containing a degree of autonomy from these social ties (Emirbayer and Goodwin 1996: 364; Fligstein and McAdam 2012: 48–49) or whether agency is something that is performed through social structures and associated scripts (Weber 1998; Ringmar 2016). For both positions, however, there is no nonstructured action that is free from social ties, just as there is no social structure that is free from dynamics of emergence, reproduction, and contestation. For relational scholars, social life takes place in “structured contexts of action”

(Emirbayer and Goodwin 1996: 372)—fields of practice formed by configurations of events and experiences. This does not preclude analyses that focuses on relatively stable institutional configurations: bureaucracies, states, forms of international administration, and so on. What it does preclude is defining these entities by listing their attributes as if these were not susceptible to dynamics of emergence, reproduction, and contestation and carrying out research as if these dynamics were not central to their make-up and behavior. For IPS scholarship, it is relations “all the way down.”

In practice, relational scholarship varies considerably in the extent to which it applies these insights. Much analysis starts with a pre-existing actor or structure that then interacts and transforms over time. Some of these approaches are careful to signal that these are analytical and not ontological assumptions, relying on relational language like “social sites,” “assemblages,” or “configurations” to indicate the transient nature of entities (Tilly 2005; Pratt 2020; Bell and Holmqvist this volume). Beyond these ontological first-order claims lies an epistemological argument: the causal processes and mechanisms driving social behavior and outcomes are embedded in relations. We can productively contrast this claim with more mainstream approaches where, as Andrew Abbott (2001: 39) explains, “the social world consists of fixed entities (the units of analysis) that have attributes (the variables).”

In this Handbook, contributors cover four main approaches to relational theorizing, all of which share a central commitment to relationalism and a processual approach to understanding and explanation, but differ in terms of how they operationalize these orientations. Marylou Hamm, Frédéric Mérand, and Anke Obendiak, for example, follow Neil Fligstein and Doug McAdam (2012: 9) in defining fields as “constructed mesolevel social order[s] in which actors … are attuned to and interact with one another on the basis of shared … understandings about the purpose of the field, relationships to others in the field …, and the rules governing legitimate action in the field.” Fields, in this view, are relational and holist at once. Alexander Montgomery’s chapter hones in on social network analysis, warning that some of the methodological choices these theorists adopt risk stripping relationalism from network theory. Colleen Bell and Caroline Holmqvist’s contribution focuses on assemblage theory. As Bell and Holmqvist explain, in assemblage thinking, the social world is understood as a “patchwork of heterogeneous and fluid configurations that, in turn, form part of other configurations … assemblage thinking requires that we accept that the social has indeterminate properties that are unstable and indefinite.” Finally, Paul MacDonald outlines a “relational-realist approach,” with a particular emphasis on repertoires, which draws on the contentious politics tradition in order to argue that IR scholars should use repertoires to understand a variety of activities, from the behavior of armed groups in civil wars to the development of transnational social movements and the diplomatic interactions of states.

As Go and Nexon note, these approaches are far from exhaustive. Elements of relational analysis can also be found in discourse analysis, especially when concerned with how configurations of meaning produce conditions of possibility for agency and action (Bially Mattern 2004). Both actor-network theory and new materialist approaches can also be seen as relational (e.g. Nexon and Pouliot 2013; Lundborg and Vaughn-Williams 2015). So too are pragmatist approaches, which replace the figure of the human being as a processor of information with a creative actor embedded in an ordered social context (Cochran 2002; Friedrichs and Kratochwil 2009; Pratt 2020). As a result, this Handbook only begins to explore the potential significance of relational work to IPS scholarship.

Intersubjectivity

Our second meta-commitment is intersubjectivity. Intersubjectivity characterizes a broad range of IR scholarship, well beyond what we tend to identify as explicitly IPS work, including, notably, some of the constructivist scholarship we referenced in previous sections. Early contributions that engaged the intersubjective character of politics, and social phenomena more generally, focused on how norms shaped actors’ identity (Finnemore 1996) and on the insight that structural features of international politics, such as anarchy, is “what states make of it” (Wendt 1992). Today, IPS scholarship tends to treat intersubjectivity as both fundamental and, at the same time, contingent. It is fundamental because work in IPS often sees intersubjectivity as running “all the way down” to the constitution of actors as subjects. This is what is at stake in the shift from the “individual” to the “subject,” in which the subject is a product of the social (Epstein and Jackson, this volume). In this understanding, it is the social (i.e. relations between social sites) that produces the subject. Yet, some IPS work sees intersubjectivity as contingent in that it is not produced or has consequences through a ready-made, external “structure” but is performed, enacted, and made sense of through agency.

From this commitment to intersubjectivity emerges, as Epstein and Jackson note in their lead chapter, an approach to “actorhood as an empirical rather than an ex ante theoretical question.” For Epstein and Jackson, intersubjectivity is analyzed through the investigation of different practices and forms of meaning-making. This view entails a conceptualization of political agency as flowing from the intersubjective processes through which subjects become self-conscious and recognized by others (Epstein, Lindemann, and Sending 2018). Combined in this way with a commitment to relationalism, a focus on intersubjectivity entails a particular view of social structure in which stability and order emerge from processual dynamics.

Exactly how this intersubjective wager is operationalized varies between IPS approaches. In some versions, intersubjectivity is a point of departure for a focus on the emergence or changing meaning of governance objects (Stampnitzky 2013). In other, perhaps more prominent, versions of IPS, the specific workings of intersubjectivity are the central focus of analysis. Work on discourse, particularly that influenced by Foucault, has been especially prominent in IPS, examining how discursive formations produce subject-positions and define what can and cannot be said and done (Neumann 1999; Hansen 2006: Aalberts, this volume). Others take the same core insight and mobilize the concept of practices, defined in terms of “socially meaningful bundles of activities” (Schatzki 2012) or as “competent performances” (see Adler-Nissen, this volume). Here, a concern for intersubjectivity generates a focus on concrete sites of inquiry, such as diplomatic negotiations. This understanding also features prominently in analyses of performances and performativity (see Jestrovic and Rai, this volume), which brings out the ways in which intersubjective structures are rendered concrete through the performance of meaningful acts that are recognized as such by others. In his chapter, Thomas Lindemann offers a different vantage point, concentrating on the concept of misrecognition to explore how the inherent ambiguities and instability in social relations give rise to tension and politicization, both within and between intersubjectively shared understandings.

This view of intersubjectivity is also mobilized at different scales, including explorations of changing conceptions of sovereignty (Bartelson 1995), the conditions of possibility for the performance of political agency (Ashley 1984; Weldes and Duvall, this volume), and interest in how actors’ identities and political outlooks change over time as a result of either normative or epistemic changes (Sending 2015; Allan 2018). From this emerges an important historical sensibility, which translates into an interest in exploring how political orders are underpinned by the fixing of meaning and how these emerge, are reproduced, and challenged. Herein lies the denaturalizing orientation common to IPS scholarship, which is oriented around the emergence and transformation of what often appear to be static, solid “things.”

Historicism

Our final meta-commitment is historicism. Historicism is a warrant to historically locate practices and dynamics, a concern for the contingent, disruptive, constitutive impact of historical events and processes, and a study of contextualized rationalities and intersubjectivities. To say that IPS has an “historicist” sensibility is not to say that either the substance or the methods of IPS are necessarily interpretative; to the contrary, it is a sensibility that lends itself to a range of methods and methodologies. What historicism does preclude is a commitment to invariant causal models, which assume a stable set of variables with effects that can be generalized across time and space. To adopt an historicist sensibility means accepting that causal work is neither invariant nor universal but operates through the contextual interaction of events, processes, and mechanisms. Unlike much IR theory, causation in IPS is not associated with regularity-deterministic accounts that establish associations between objects that are separated (or at least separable) in space and time (Kurki 2006: 192). Indeed, rather than disaggregate entities into discrete properties, the focus of this volume is on the relational interconnections that constitute entities in the first place. In this sense, causal work in IPS rests on generating an “intelligible connection” between “closely related bundles” of events and practices (Kurki and Suganami 2012: 404; also see Humphreys and Suganami 2024).

The lead chapter in this section, by Tarak Barkawi, Duncan Bell, and Ayşe Zarakol, takes up this challenge. Seeking to mediate “the specific” and “the general” through examining central concerns in IR, such as war and state formation, the authors show the necessity of being attentive not just to particular histories and changing social formations but also to historiographies that show us that “the past is not stable,” existing “only relative to the questions we ask of it in the present.” This provocation is taken on by other contributors to this section. Eren Duzgun demonstrates how the (to date) relatively slight engagement between IPS and historical sociology can be enhanced through attentiveness to non-Eurocentric, multi-linear, pluri-temporal accounts of historical development (see also Krishna 2017; Seth 2016). In his contribution, Brieg Powel takes this approach further back in time and space by “historicizing the global”, seeing history as “a smorgas-bord of relations, experiences, practices and forms of political organization” over many thousands of years. Brieg’s “decentred” approach to history aims to challenge tendencies in IPS toward presentism and “monotemporality” (the co-location of the modern with the West), pushing at the edges of where and when we find IPS. Hendrik Spruyt engages with anthropological work on practices, performances, and gifts in outlining the “fusion of horizons” that enable international orders to emerge, be sustained, and (over time) challenged. Jeanne Morefield’s contribution shows how a “norm against noticing” imperialism in IR has “unseen” key developments in world politics, particularly around US interventionism. Morefield, following Edward Said, puts empire “back at the center of things,” responding to the lead chapter’s request for IPS to more closely link historical inquiry with theoretical diagnosis. In the closing chapter in this part, Meera Sabaratnam highlights the colonial character of contemporary world politics and IR, including IPS, and draws attention to an urgent need for decolonizing, including the work of IPS’s most prominent inspirations: Foucault (also see Howell and Richter-Montpetit 2018) and Bourdieu (also see White 2021). IPS, Sabaratnam argues, has focused too much “on the productive character of liberal-modern power structures, rather than dwelling with and alongside the concrete political struggles taking place from below.” The challenge, she contends, is to join in a “productive and reflexive engagement in which our understanding of the myriad forms and instruments of modern power can be animated by a shared sense of struggle against the alienation produced by its colonial form.”

Our commitments to relationalism, intersubjectivity, and historicism are not chosen arbitrarily. Indeed, this volume is intended to show that these commitments are fundamental to analyzing how social formations in world politics are created, sustained, and transformed. Our vision of IPS, therefore, sees it as a network in which our three meta-commitments act as a central node, with various approaches linking to it, sometimes strongly, at other times at more of a remove. One of the central tasks of this volume is to map the “network structure” of IPS, exploring how different approaches adopt these meta-commitments. In doing so, we hope to invite others to reflect on this mapping in a dialogue about how IPS has developed to date and will continue to evolve. After all, as we note above, IPS is not a closed field of enquiry but one that is itself relationally configured and contested.

SOCIAL STUFF

In the second half of the Handbook, we examine the ways in which IPS and its meta-commitments generate interventions into a range of issue areas. There is, of course, no way that a handbook can be exhaustive in detailing how scholars “do” IPS. IPS scholarship engages with every sub-field in IR. In similar vein, IPS scholars study a wide range of topics, from migration to militarism, speculative fiction to logistics, sonics to diplomacy. There is very little, if anything, substantively that escapes the purview of IPS. To bring some coherence to this diverse scene, we have organized this section of the Handbook around questions of order and change in world politics. The issue of order cuts across subjects and disciplines. If sociological theory from Parsons to Foucault is bound up with the persistence (or not) of social orders, as Christian Reus-Smit and Ann Towns argue in their lead chapter, IR has also been “drawn inexorably to questions of international order for theoretical and historical reasons.” IR theorists attempt to understand a host of “ordered” phenomenon, including the persistence of global economic regimes, the rise and decline of security institutions, and the endurance (or not) of world orders.

Other contributions to this section demonstrate the many ways in which the meta-commitments outlined in the first half of the Handbook impact the character and content of international orders. The chapter by Jorg Kustermans focuses on rule, drawing “attention to how international order is anchored in particular actors or social groups—or at least that international order is assumed or perceived to be anchored in them.” Rule describes situations in which an actor dominates other actors, hence “rulers command, while the ruled obey (or risk suffering the consequences when they do not).” Kustermans outlines several theses about the operation of rule, which he applies to nineteenth-century colonial orders. Of all of the orders underpinning the contemporary international system, orders of sovereignty and capital are, perhaps, the most enduring. As Arjun Chowdhury argues in his chapter, “Sovereignty is a concept that is seen as simultaneously central to IR theory and so historically variable as to be meaningless.” To capture sovereignty in any meaningful sense requires understanding it as “relational on two dimensions, between ruler and ruled, and between rulers” and seeing that these relations entail “intersubjective understandings that regulate relationships, and, as those intersubjective understandings change over time, sovereignty is historically variable.” In her chapter on capital orders, Lisa Tilley argues that we must understand the contemporary neoliberal economic order as “a direct response to the powerful global shifts instigated by waves of decolonisation.” As colonial movements grew, neoliberals “diverted the post-War rights project toward making a case for constructing legal international structures to protect finance capital flows from the collective will of movements and Southern governments.”

As we argued in the previous section, one of the strengths of IPS is that it pushes conventional IR theory into underexplored areas of inquiry. The final three chapters of this section follows this path by examining racial, gendered, and spatial orders, respectively. Clive Gabay traces the formation and transformation of “racial grammars” that stratify world politics, arguing that these grammars produce racialization and racism as a set of violent logics, structures that have to be both “malleable and flexible in order to persist and survive through various modes of resistance, anti-racism and decolonisation.” Laura Sjoberg draws a critical distinction between “gender orders,” where the primary structuring principle is gender (as with many public restrooms), and “gendered orders,” “where something else is a or even the primary distributional principle, and yet gender still plays a key role in the ways that the order is implemented and expressed.” Finally, Joanne Yao shows the ways in which IPS can break new ground in even the oldest of literatures. Space has long been at the center of IR theory. Going back to work at the turn of the twentieth century by Alfred Mahan and Halford MacKider, geography has sometimes been seen as determining actors’ behavior. Yao shows how dominant spatial configurations are “held in place by power, and these configurations have consequences for legitimacy, authority, and the distribution of resources.” She stresses the ways in which her form of IPS does not reduce space to discourse but instead shows how the co-constitution of ideas and materiality lies at the core of modern spatial orders.

The next three sections turn from order to change, examining how, when, where, and by whom orders of various kinds are contested. In their lead chapter for the section on “resistance,” Jef Huysmans and João Pontes Nogueira point out that “IPS work uses resistance as a key concept for analysing societies, politics, and the international less than it does technologies and techniques of arranging and reproducing order.” Using a dual conception of power that “combines a horizontal view of conflicts between forces with a hierarchical understanding of relations of domination,” Huysmans and Nogueira highlight transgressive forms of politics that “fracture” resistance (as in the anti-globalization movements of the 1990s), “situationalist” approaches that examine “uncoordinated instances of protest” (as in the blocking of borders by migrants), and entanglements—or “folds”—across borders that form “transversal alliances” (as in refugees taking part in tactics of de-subjugation). In the process, they emphasize the centrality of transgressive politics to IPS.

These insights are engaged closely in other contributions to this section. Debbie Lisle’s chapter focuses on transversal, “micropolitical practices of bordering” that evolve and mutate, sometimes into durable (if never static) formations. The frictions that lie behind these processes are explored, Lisle outlines, in IPS scholarship on migration, citizenship, and infrastructures. Xavier Guillaume and Nicolas Lemay-Hébert take on the metaphor of “the game” in exploring the forms of “everyday resistance” that can be found in world politics, including peacebuilding operations. Guillaume and Lemay-Hébert develop Ashis Nandy’s idea of the “nonplayer” to show how games, or dynamics of domination and resistance, feature not only players and counter-players but also “nonplayers”—those who reject the rules and expectations of the game itself. Eric Selbin’s contribution uses Huysman and Noguiera’s framework to explore the ways in which revolution “needs to be restituated and reconsidered in an entangled, figurative zone of awkward engagement(s).” Selbin’s “folksonomic” approach examines the changing ways in which peoples around the world have banded together in the hope that, as Delroy Wilson put it, “better must come.” Håkan Thörn, in his chapter on “ecological resistance,” makes a powerful case for combining approaches rooted in governmentality with those drawn from social movements and revolutions in arguing that ecological resistance should be understood as a “politics of responsibility” based around the possibilities of progress(es), revolution(s), and emancipation(s). The final chapter in this section, by Jean-François Drolet, focuses on “conservative resistance.” Drolet traces the filaments of “new right” thought over several decades, arguing that today’s “radical right” is “the product of a decades-long attempt to craft an intellectual, cultural and political project capable of fostering resistance against liberal orders by explaining what is unsatisfactory with the current social reality, identifying the agents of change, and providing norms for criticism and practical goals for transformation.” The result, Drolet observes, is a potent political force.

The third sub-section opens up issues around the many faces of power, going beyond the binary of material and ideational to examine the social sources of power, whether in “hard cases” (such as military force) or through particular networks (such as experts). As noted above, the concept of power is as central to IPS scholarship as it is to the broader field of IR. But IPS work on power typically starts from, and is motivated by, questions about what constitutes power, how it is wielded, and how it varies across time and space. For example, debates in IR that focus on the nature of hegemony and revisionism tend to see power as a stable, measurable resource (e.g. Beckley 2018). In contrast, IPS scholarship explores the ways in which power changes over time, and the dynamics by which it undergirds and stabilizes political orders.

In their lead chapter, Stefano Guzzini and Jimmy Casas Klausen discuss the various dimensions of power and emphasize the importance of being cognizant of what is assumed in exploring these multiple dimensions and their effects. The chapter by Sabrina Axster and Ida Danewid focus on the ways in which corporeal power challenges claims within both IR and IPS in terms of how power should be studied, and understood, from the vantage point of marginalized groups. Stephanie Hofmann explores the uses and potential, but also the limits, of the concept of symbolic power developed most comprehensively by Pierre Bourdieu. In a similar vein, Matthew Eagleton-Pierce discusses how economic power rests on a range of symbolic resources, stressing how this generates potential links between IPS and International Political Economy (IPE). In her chapter, Jennifer Spindel argues that military power rests on symbols and imaginaries of security and insecurity—in other words, the “social, symbolic, and constitutive processes that create military power.”

The final section of the Handbook explores the various sites of IPS agency. Once again, our goal is to show how IPS can open up IR’s theoretical and substantive agendas. In their lead chapter, Jutta Weldes and Bud Duvall challenge scholars to explore the constitution of agency across different scales of analysis. They note that the production of political subjects with forms of agency is both intertextual and intersectional, requiring us to be sensitive to historical changes and cognizant of diverse forms of power—and conceptualizations of politics—across time and place. In his chapter, Iver Neumann provides an analysis of the constitution and form of polities, ranging from tribes to empires, suggesting that emerging research on non-European polities can reduce Eurocentric tendencies within IR, including IPS. Annabelle Littoz-Monnet looks at the constitution of experts (those with politically salient agency) by exploring the macro-level operations of discourses, the meso-level relational configurations of specific actors, and the micro-level context of action. She explores the intertwining of these scales through a discussion of the constitution of “global experts.” Ben Christian and Sebastian Schindler zoom in on the agency of international organizations, exploring both macro-scale technocratic discourses and the ways in which uncertainty and contingency offer a distinct form of agency that is up for grabs at particular moments.

CONCLUSION

We close where we started—with global polycrisis. We hope that this introduction, and the chapters that follow, demonstrate the theoretical and substantive contributions that IPS makes to understanding contemporary world politics—and the multiple crises we are currently experiencing. Relationalism demonstrates the ways in which actors and structures are in flux, illuminating how they are reconstituted and transformed. Work on intersubjectivity frontloads questions about how actors define and understand times of crisis. A commitment to historicism demands that the present conjuncture is placed into context, focusing on how events and experiences of crisis emerge and are—or are not—patterned.

As we noted at the beginning of this chapter, IPS is unusually well placed to examine the contemporary polycrisis, both through work that speaks directly to and from its central agendas and also those that currently occupy a space around its margins. That said, if IPS is to continue to thrive as a vibrant, outward-facing, open-ended research agenda, should it query some of the ways it is currently constituted as a field of practice? If the answer to this question is yes, then the chapters that follow illustrate the possibilities that result from taking up this challenge. While they offer overviews of past debates and the origins of key concepts and theories, and demonstrate the depth of IPS scholarship into a wide array of issues, they also identify limitations and suggest avenues for future research. We very much hope that this volume prompts IPS scholarship, and all of those interested in its approach, to take up this challenge.
